\section{Atmospheric blocking}\label{sec:intro_blocking}
The term \enquote{atmospheric blocking} emerged in weather forecast and atmospheric research
in the early 20th century initially to describe persistent surface high-pressure
regions \enquote{blocking} the usual eastward progression of low-pressure cyclones
(\textcite{rexBlockingActionMiddle1950}; cf.~also \textcite{lupoAtmosphericBlockingEvents2021} and references therein).
It has since come to be understood as a large-scale disturbance
extending through the whole troposphere and interacting with the stratosphere.
The term \enquote{blocking} or simply \enquote{block} notably does not refer
to its geometric shape as a somewhat coherent singular entity, especially since
they can come in different shapes and sizes and are usually embedded in the
wavelike structure of the flow.

Though there is still a lack of consensus on the exact definition of a blocking
and a coherent unifying theory of them is still
at large \parencite{woollingsBlockingItsResponse2018}, the concept broadly encompasses
the notions of a persistent, quasi-stationary, large-scale anticyclonic flow pattern
that occurs in the mid- to high latitudes. Its persistence implies lifetimes on the
order of days to weeks, which is long compared to typical synoptic weather systems.
It is quasi-stationary in that its characteristic flow pattern and position almost do not change
over its lifetime measured by the usual magnitudes of change on those timescales.
This is why some theories liken the phenomenon to quasi-stationary (standing) planetary
waves analogous to waves in other physical systems \parencite[e.g.,~][]{tungTheoryStationaryLong1979, nakamuraAtmosphericBlockingTraffic2018}.

Any weather situation considered a blocking usually involves considerable displacement
of relatively warm and low \gls{pv} subtropical air masses poleward, introducing
or reinforcing a ridge in the upper-level flow. In severe cases this ridge might
even detach from the subtropical reservoir judging from closed contours of low
\gls{pv} values on isentropic surfaces or, equivalently, high potential temperature
values on surfaces of constant \gls{pv} \parencite{pellyNewPerspectiveBlocking2003}.

\subsection{Definitions} \label{sec:intro_block_def}
Consistent with the rather diffuse understanding introduced above, there is no
universally accepted formal definition of atmospheric blocking, and different
diagnostics are adopted depending on purpose, scale of analysis and, arguably,
school of thought \parencite{barnesMethodologyComparisonBlocking2012,barriopedroApplicationBlockingDiagnosis2010}.
Blockings are frequently defined either by indices specifically designed for them
or as one of a number of different weather regimes which, in turn, result from
some kind of clustering of instantaneous atmospheric states.

Indices may be differentiated by which meteorological field they use and by whether
they identify a blocking based on absolute or anomaly fields. The choice of
index will importantly also determine the precise shape of the detected flow field
and will identify different parts of the blocking \parencite{pinheiroAtmosphericBlockingIntercomparison2019}.
For instance, one very
common group of blocking indices is based on the geopotential height field and
its gradient or the associated synoptic winds. This rather kinematic
perspective exploits the blocking's characteristic meridional flow field as a hallmark
and is based on work by \textcite{lejenasCharacteristicsNorthernHemisphere1983} and
\textcite{tibaldiOperationalPredictabilityBlocking1990}. More dynamically and
object-oriented indices identify anomalies in the fields of \gls{pv} or potential
temperature \parencite{schwierzPerspicaciousIndicatorsAtmospheric2004}, a lens that
was pioneered by \textcite{pellyNewPerspectiveBlocking2003} though they identified
gradient reversals instead of anomalies. Since potential temperature and \gls{pv}
are materially conserved under adiabatic and (in the case of \gls{pv}) inviscid conditions,
air-masses that are designated as belonging to a blocking may be traced back and
forth to understand the formation and decay of the blocking event.

A complementary perspective conceptualises blocking as a preferred large-scale
circulation regime within a reduced phase space of atmospheric variability.
This understanding originates from the interpretation of the atmosphere as a
forced-dissipative dynamical system whose long-term behaviour is governed by its
lower-dimensional attractor 
\parencite[e.g.,~][]{dijkstraNonlinearClimateDynamics2013,weilandIntransitiveAtmosphereDynamics2021, strommenTopologicalPerspectiveWeather2023, ghilReviewArticleDynamical2023}.
Temporal evolution then guides the system as a trajectory on the attractor with
its location in phase space determining its dynamic behaviour as well as its expression
in physical space. It may then be insightful to partition this
attractor into a finite set of metastable or recurrent regimes as a dimensionality reduction
\parencite{michelangeliWeatherRegimesRecurrence1995}.
Blocking emerges as one of multiple such weather regimes, defined by persistence
and transition probabilities rather than local anomalies or gradient reversals
in the Eulerian field.

Studying the atmosphere according to a small number of characteristic regimes or
clusters is probably as old as synoptic meteorology itself and has been a ubiquitous
tool in atmospheric sciences ever since \parencite{hannachiLowFrequencyNonlinearity2017},
especially in the North Atlantic European sector 
\parencite[e.g.,~][]{vautardMultipleWeatherRegimes1990,nealFlexibleApproachDefining2016, gramsLifeCycleDefinition2026}. 
Given the enormous dimensionality of the atmospheric system, meaningful insight into
the underlying attractor's structure may necessitate abstraction into low-order
models with some exhibiting blocking-like regimes
\parencite{crommelinMechanismAtmosphericRegime2004,kallenBifurcationPropertiesQuasigeostrophic1982,lucariniNewMathematicalFramework2020}
facilitating translation between paradigm models and the real world.

Finally, from a wave-dynamical perspective, blocking can
be interpreted as a nonlinear manifestation of amplified, breaking, or quasi-stationary
Rossby waves \parencite{tungTheoryStationaryLong1979,nakamuraAtmosphericBlockingTraffic2018}.
In this framework, blockings are associated with \glspl{rwp} \parencite{wirthRossbyWavePackets2018}
or maxima of \gls{lwa} fluxes \parencite{huangLocalFiniteamplitudeWave2016}
and will thus be identified based on characteristic
patterns in the wavenumber rather than the physical domain.

The differing definitions—index-based, regime-based, and wave-dynamical—thus highlight
that blocking may be understood alternately as a local kinematic signature, a metastable
circulation state, or a planetary-wave configuration, depending on the theoretical
and diagnostic perspective adopted.


\subsection{Phenomenology} \label{sec:intro_block_Phenomenology}
Climatologically, blocking events are more prominent in the \gls{nh}
compared to the \gls{sh} due a stronger land-sea contrast and orography \parencite{woollingsBlockingItsResponse2018}.
They are also larger, more intense \parencite{nabizadeh3DStructureNorthern2021}
and more frequent in winter than in summer, with a peak frequency
in the North Atlantic sector of roughly every 5--6 days on average.
Aside from the North Atlantic, the North Pacific and, to a lesser extent, the
Eurasian sector are also preferred regions for blocking occurrence in the \gls{nh}.
Their implied temporal and spatial scales lets them exert a major influence on
the mid-latitude circulation and weather on a continental to planetary scale for
whole seasons even \parencite{jensenDynamicAnalysisRecord2015}.

Where they occur, blockings can exert a considerable impact on societies and
ecosystems alike, a feature that numerous publications have drawn attention to
\parencite[e.g.,~][]{kautzAtmosphericBlockingWeather2021,whanInfluenceAtmosphericBlocking2016,pfahlQuantifyingRelevanceAtmospheric2012}.
The implied persistence can lead to sustained weather conditions of
different nature depending on season and location relative to the blocking high.
Their eponymous blocking and deflecting of air masses can favour anomalously and
extremely hot or cold conditions and stationary cyclones on their flanks leading
to anomalously high moisture transport and associated precipitation
\parencite[e.g.,~][]{lenggenhagerAtmosphericBlocksModulate2019, mohrRelationshipAtmosphericBlocking2019, mohrMultidisciplinaryAnalysisExceptional2023}.

Apart from their effect on the advection of air masses, the high pressure associated
with blockings favours subsidence and, by extension, cloud-free conditions and
reduced vertical mixing in their centers. Together with reduced wind speeds also in the horizontal,
this frequently leads to heat waves and droughts in summer as well as cold spells in winter.
\textcite{herrmannAtmosphericBlocksIncrease2025} showed that disproportianately many
severe wildfires in the Northern high latitudes were associated with blocked episodes in summer.
Reduced mixing may also entail episodes of poor air quality, all of which put severe
pressure on societal systems and human health \parencite{webberDynamicalImpactRossby2017,popeImpactSynopticWeather2016}.
Prolonged episodes of decreases in wind energy supply and increased energy demand
caused by blockings in winter are considered a major risk for the energy sector,
especially with the transition to renewable sources \parencite{oteroCharacterizingRenewableEnergy2022, vanderwielInfluenceWeatherRegimes2019},
potentially aggravating poor air quality through increased fossil fuel use.

\subsection{Interactions} \label{sec:intro_block_interactions}
The lack of a unified theory of blockings
is probably caused by their interaction
with processes and earth system components on a wide range of spatial
and temporal scales.

The large-scale nature of blockings is evident from their interrelation with planetary-scale
teleconnections. Given their spatial proximity, a modulating relation between
blockings and the \gls{nao} \parencite{grayElevenyearSolarCycle2016} and \gls{pna}
\parencite{croci-maspoliAtmosphericBlockingSpacetime2007} for \gls{nh} blockings
and the \gls{sam} \parencite{oliveiraNewClimatologySouthern2014} for the \gls{sh}
seem obvious. More surprisingly perhaps, tropical teleconnections such as
the \gls{enso} \parencite{zhangENSOrelatedSouthernHemisphere2023, mckennaImpactsNinoDiversity2023, barriopedroRelationshipENSOStratospheric2014},
or the \gls{mjo} \parencite{hendersonInfluenceMaddenJulian2016} have also been linked to
blocking frequency and persistence, highlighting the role of planetary-scale disturbances.
More specifically, large scale divergence caused by tropical convection is associated
with excitations of globally propagating Rossby waves \parencite{sardeshmukhGenerationGlobalRotational1988},
which can in turn lead to blocking modification or formation.

The importance of each of the above mechanisms and teleconnections differ regionally and seasonally,
implying a complex two-way interaction between blockings and other components of the earth system
\parencite{burkhardtPlanetarySynopticscaleInteractions2005, nakamuraRoleHighLowFrequency1997}.
More specifically, blocking dynamics are influenced by the polar cryosphere
through heat and moisture fluxes and accompanying sea-ice anomalies on seasonal scales
\parencite{tyrlisUralBlockingDriving2019,woollingsArcticWarmingAtmospheric2014, drijfhoutSpontaneousAbruptClimate2013}.
There, a strong two-way interaction with polar vortex dynamics and the stratosphere
more generally has been established, mediated in particular by upward wave
activity flux and associated tropopause displacements
\parencite{woollingsAssociationsStratosphericVariability2010, colucciDiagnosticComparisonTropospheric2015, yessimbetPathwaysInfluenceNorthern2022}.
From 27 objectively identified \glspl{ssw} in the \gls{nh}
between 1957 and 2001, \textcite{martiusBlockingPrecursorsStratospheric2009}
found that 25 were preceded by blocking events in the North Atlantic sector.

Blockings have also been shown to exert a first order influence on the subpolar
ocean \parencite{hakkinenAtmosphericBlockingAtlantic2011}. On the other hand, \gls{sst}
patterns and according gradients, at times, provide the necessary baroclinic
forcings and heat and moisture content for blockings to form, controlling their
occurrence frequency and intensity \parencite{christSeaSkyUnderstanding2025, mathewsGulfStreamMoisture2024}.
\textcite{narinesinghUniformSSTWarming2024} however showed that a uniform increase
in \glspl{sst} is responsible for the observed change in blocking frequency in a
climate model rather than a modulation of \gls{sst} gradients. This highlights
the uncertainty associated with the precise preconditioning of \glspl{sst} for blockings
with their according fluxes. In a similar manner, blocking dynamics are also shaped by surface level diabatic
sensible and latent heat fluxes over land \parencite{tillyCalculatedHeightTendencies2008}
and their climatological occurrence is modulated by land-sea contrasts and orography
through initiated planetary waves \parencite{heImpactLandSea2014}.

\subsection{Processes and theories of atmospheric blocking}\label{sec:intro_blocking_theories}
Given all that has been stated above about the complex phenomenon of atmospheric blockings
from practical perspectives, it is not surprising that there is not a single mathematical
theory explaining all of its facets. That said, a number of mathematical theories have
been proposed that explain complementing aspects of the phenomenon, based on differing
assumptions and perspectives.

\subsubsection{Barotropic theories} \label{sec:intro_block_barotropic} 
More traditional theories on blockings focus on barotropic large scale
dynamics---a stance that is justified by the fact that the vertical structure
of atmospheric blockings is dominantly equivalent-barotropic, with little vertical
variation in temperature and wind \parencite{nabizadeh3DStructureNorthern2021}.
The earliest synoptic work took a primarly diagnostic stance with some likening the phenomenon
to hydrolic jumps in other jet like flows \parencite{rexBlockingActionMiddle1950}
inspired by the observationally guided understanding of a blockings as a breakdown
of the usual zonal flow \parencite{berggrenAerologicalStudyZonal1949}. These merely
descriptive works set the stage for more quantative theories with some degree of
explanatory power.

In a barotropic flow with external (usually topographic and radiative) forcing
blocking can appear as a stationary (Rossby-) wave phenomenon \parencite{rossbyRelationVariationsIntensity1939}.
This is because it can be shown that for certain Rossby wave constellations, the
waves' group velocity exactly opposes the advection with the mean flow.
Assuming linearized barotropic
dynamics around some climatological flow, this perspective can explain
regions of climatalogical blocking occurrence, but lacks mechanisms necessary to
understand onset and persistence in a way comparable with observations \parencite{hoskinsSteadyLinearResponse1981}.

This was also pointed out by \textcite{charneyMultipleFlowEquilibria1979}, who
proposed a model in which non-linearities were essential for blocking occurrence.
Similar to the notion of stationary waves, their model admitted multiple equilibria
and opened the understanding of blocking as a (quasi-)stationary regime governing
the atmospheric system's dynamics in its vicinity in state space. The model will
be introduced in more detail later. Suffice to say that it offers a mechanism for
regime persistence and sudden transitions, but its applicability is questioned by
its sensitivity to parameter choices \parencite{crommelinMechanismAtmosphericRegime2004}
and whether the real atmosphere possesses regimes in the dynamical sense is debated
\parencite{stephensonExistenceMultipleClimate2004}.

The quasi-stationary wave perspective was advanced by \textcite{tungTheoryStationaryLong1979}
who conceived blockings as waves resonant with topography and land-sea contrast, but
kept in place by specific conditions of the background flow. Non-linearities are
assumed in this model and baroclinic extensions are discussed, though not necessary and
time-dependent amplification explains realistic onset and persistence mechanisms
lacking in the basic Rossby wave concept above.

A separate set of theories argue that due to their nearly equivalent-barotropic
structure, blockings resemple individual vortices interacting with other vortices
in a background flow. \textcite{mcwilliamsApplicationEquivalentModons1980} showed that
the (non-linear) equivalent-barotorpic vorticity equation admits vortex pairs called \enquote{modons}
as solutions similar in structure and behaviour to blockings. Similar results
can be obtained when considering point vortices as abstractions \parencite{mullerApplicationsPointVortex2015}.
In a related view \textcite{hainesEddyForcedCoherentStructures1987}, interpret
synoptic eddies as providing a dipole \gls{pv} forcing that supports the coherent
modon structure against dissipation. Modon theory can be seen as a paradigm-level 
illustration, especially considering that the real background flow is highly variable.

Still, the fact that transient eddies can systematically
transfer vorticity to foster large scale flow configurations has been well established
for a long time \parencite[e.g.,~][]{kuoVorticityTransferRelated1951} and atmospheric blockings
do not seem to form an exception (their spatio-temporal scales characterizing them
as the latter rather than the former). 
Even in barotropic flows eddy-mean flow 
\parencite{shuttsPropagationEddiesDiffluent1983,legrasPersistentAnomaliesBlocking1985},
eddy-eddy \parencite{yamazakiVortexVortexInteractions2013, luoNonlinearMultiscaleInteraction2014}
or sometimes both types of interactions \parencite{cashDynamicalProcessesBlock2000} 
can lead to the kinds of persistent anomalies associated with blocking.

A closely related nonlinear mechanism for blocking onset and maintenance is Rossby wave breaking.
It occurs when amplified planetary waves undergo irreversible overturning 
(visible in \gls{pv} contours on isentropic surfaces), leading to the
meridional displacement of \gls{pv} typical for blockings \parencite{mcintyreBreakingPlanetaryWaves1983,
thorncroftTwoParadigmsBaroclinicwave1993}. Anticyclonic wave breaking in particular
has been linked to the formation of persistent high-pressure anomalies resembling blocking events 
\parencite{pellyNewPerspectiveBlocking2003, masatoWavebreakingCharacteristicsMidlatitude2012}.
Unlike linear stationary-wave theory, this framework emphasizes nonlinear \gls{pv}
rearrangement in physical space and naturally accounts for jet deformation and cutoff formation. 
At the same time, Rossby wave breaking is often preceded by or interacts with
baroclinic wave amplification \parencite{tamarin-brodskyRelationRossbyWavebreaking2024}, 
indicating that while the breaking itself can be described
in largely barotropic \gls{pv} terms, its excitation frequently originates in baroclinic dynamics.

The Rossby wave breaking framework is very similar to theories based on \glspl{rwp} 
\parencite{wirthRossbyWavePackets2018} or \gls{lwa} fluxes 
\parencite{huangLocalFiniteamplitudeWave2016,nakamuraAtmosphericBlockingTraffic2018}.
Here, conservation laws are established for the amount of undulation of the jet stream 
and a severe undulation is then associated with a blocking event. The theory elegantly connects
to more traditional wave-dynamical perspectives, while emphasizing locality. Though principally
barotropic, baroclinic and diabatic processes appear as source terms in this theory and
are, thus, vital elements in the real life explanation of observed events \parencite{neal2021PacificNorthwest2022}.

The above shows that what genuinely barotropic theories generally often lack is
a mechanism for the strength of the
disturbances necessary for blocking formation and maintanence of the scale comparable with observations.
Consistent with this, rapid onsets of blockings are rarely observed in such models and
even if upstream barotropic eddies support this process, cyclones in the real atmosphere
are scarcely barotropic \parencite{colucciExplosiveCyclogenesisLargescale1985,
frederiksenOnsetBlockingCyclogenesis1984, vautardMultipleWeatherRegimes1990}.

\subsubsection{Baroclinic theories} \label{sec:intro_block_baroclinic}
Atmospheric blockings appear not in a single layer flow, but in a stratified, vertically sheared
atmosphere in which baroclinic instability continuously generates synoptic-scale
eddies shaping the general circulation. Classical linear instability theory
\parencite[e.g.,~][]{charneyDynamicsLongWaves1947, eadyLongWavesCyclone1949} and
energy cycle considerations \parencite{lorenzAvailablePotentialEnergy1955},
demonstrate that in such a setting, generic instabilites convert available
potential energy into eddy kinetic energy on synoptic scales.
Baroclinic theories tackle the central question how these synoptic
eddies feed back onto the planetary-scale flow to produce persistent large-scale
anomalies \parencite{shuttsCaseStudyEddy1986}. In this sense, the baroclinic perspective complements more traditional barotropic
frameworks introduced above by seeking a physically consistent explanation for transient
eddies capable of initiating, sustaining or destabilizing large-scale flow regimes, and they
provide models with more realistic assumptions than idealized approaches
based on modons or point-vortex dynamics.

The feedbacks between eddies of any scale and the large scale flow are closely tied
to the nature of turbulent cascades in geophysical flows. In fully three-dimensional
turbulence, energy cascades downscale toward dissipation \parencite{popeTurbulentFlows2000}.
In contrast, two-dimensional turbulence exhibits inverse energy cascades,
transferring kinetic energy to larger scales \parencite{boffettaTwoDimensionalTurbulence2012}.
Quasi-geostrophic turbulence appropriate for a strongly stratified, rapidly rotating
atmosphere shares aspects of both: baroclinic instability may inject energy at synoptic
scales, while nonlinear interactions may transfer kinetic energy upscale and potential
enstrophy downscale or vice versa depending on the extent to 
which relevant processes dominate \parencite{frederiksenUnifiedThreedimensionalInstability1982,
kittungDifferences2DQG2003}.

For instance,
in a two-layer quasi-geostrophic model proposed by 
\textcite{salmonTwolayerQuasigeostrophicTurbulence1978}, baroclinic energy resulting from 
differential heating cascaded downwards towards the Rossby deformation scale, where it
is converted to barotropic modes in which it is cascaded upward again.
More generally, a decomposition of rotating–stratified Boussinesq dynamics into
inertia–gravity wave modes and slow (vortical/\gls{pv}-carrying) modes suggests
that the direction of the cascade depends on which subset of phase space is dynamically active
\parencite{herbertRestrictedEquilibriumEnergy2014}.
In particular, when the statistics is restricted to the slow manifold, an inverse
energy transfer becomes admissable (a statistical mechanics analogy to condensation), 
whereas a direct energy cascade develops in the unrestricted case. The restricted case
most naturally applies to balanced, large-scale dynamics rather than full wave-vortex
systems. 

In this framework, baroclinic instability provides a fundamentally three-dimensional 
energy source, yet nonlinear equilibration and upscale transfer tend to project energy 
onto vertically coherent, barotropic structures at larger scales. Thus, although blocking 
develops in a vertically sheared environment, its mature phase often exhibits a 
pronounced equivalent-barotropic character, rendering the dynamics locally more 
two-dimensional. This depends on the specific flow condition, scale, and stratification, 
but it does not seem far fetched to understand part of a blocking’s persistence as 
an effect of energy transfer to larger scales sustained by baroclinic instability 
and the associated eddy fluxes. This argument relies in part on the observation 
of strong vertical coherence in blockings and the atmosphere appearing more stratified (two-dimensional).
As a side note, an inverse energy cascade can in principle also fuel cyclonic anomalies,
but the received energy will increase intensity rather than size (gradient-wind balance).
Cyclones are also rarely stationary due to Rossby wave radiation and downstream
propagation \parencite[][ch.~5]{holtonIntroductionDynamicMeteorology2013}. 

The upscale energy transfer encapsulates the existence of feedback mechanisms
described above in an abstract way (in spectral space rather than physical space).
In this view, baroclinic theories of blocking thus interpret persistence not as
a steady equilibrium, but as the large-scale manifestation of sustained, yet
principally intermittent, eddy fluxes and inverse energy transfer within a
stratified turbulent flow---some apply the theory directly 
\parencite{hansenComparisonSpectralEnergy1984, jensenUsingEnstrophyAdvection2014, tanakaNumericalSimulationAmplification1991, 
fournierAtmosphericEnergeticsWavelet2003, mullerUsingConceptDynamic2019}. 
Blocking may, hence, be interpreted as a barotropized manifestation of
baroclinically generated turbulence operating in a quasi-two-dimensional dynamical regime, 
though there is even evidence that such baroclinic energy conversions take place
within some blockings themselves, pointing out that the assumption of equivalent-barotropic
structure is not always justified \parencite{martineauBaroclinicBlocking2022}.

Baroclinic theories emphasizing the role of the vertical structure of the flow supporting
a variety of baroclinic over
the years to investigate these processes mechanically  \parencite[e.g.~][]{vautardSourceMidlatitudeLowfrequency1988, 
tanakaNumericalSimulationAmplification1991, marshallDynamicalUnderstandingPlanetaryScale1993}. 
Consistent with the above, many have frequently highlighted the importance of
upstream disturbances (including cyclones) and wave-interactions
for blocking onset and decay. In a simple two-layer model, \textcite{luoPlanetaryscaleBaroclinicEnvelope2000}
observed that an upstream baroclinic eddy can bring a high-wavenumber Rossby wave
into phaselock. In this view, baroclinicity is important to bring about the necessary
amplification of a wave disturbance to reach a blocking configuration, and eventually
dissipate it again. Such conceptualisation is backed by observational evidence of
the equivalent barotropic blocking region being \enquote{enveloped} by baroclinically
active zones up- and downstream \parencite{frederiksenOnsetBlockingCyclogenesis1984, nakamuraObservedChangesBaroclinic1990}.
Whats important is that there is solid evidence that this baroclinicity is essentially 
linked to processes governing blocking formation not only from an observational \parencite{colucciExplosiveCyclogenesisLargescale1985}
but also from modelling perspective \parencite{mullenLocalBalancesVorticity1986, mullenTransientEddyForcing1987}. 

\subsubsection{Diabatic processes} \label{sec:intro_block_diabatic}
Despite their success in explaining eddy amplification, upscale transfer and the maintenance
of large-scale anomalies, dry barotropic and baroclinic theories neglect the role of diabatic 
\gls{pv} modification associated with latent heat release, radiative and surface fluxes. 
These aspects were historically
overlooked probably due to difficulties in representing small-scale processes in models
and due to blockings being conceived as a large-scale phenomenon,
but also since blockings' characteristic large-scale subsidence typically suppresses convection.
Observational and modelling studies indicate that moist processes especially can substantially 
influence virtually all aspects of blockings with important implications for proper understanding
and prediction.

A central result of the recent literature is that latent heat release in clouds
is not merely a secondary modulation but can be of first-order importance for block
formation and maintenance as first drawn attention to by \textcite{croci-maspoliKeyDynamicalFeatures2009}.
The prominence of such effects
especially for blocks over the oceans leaves an imprint
on their climatology where a significant ascending motion it visible on the western
flank of the blocking high compared to subsidence in its center and eastern flank
\parencite{nabizadeh3DStructureNorthern2021}.
Based on Lagrangian trajectory analyses,
\textcite{pfahlImportanceLatentHeat2015} and \textcite{steinfeldRoleLatentHeating2019}
show that a substantial fraction of air masses
arriving in blocking anticyclones experienced strong latent heating prior to arrival,
consistent with cross-isentropic transport of low-\gls{pv} air in moist ascending airstreams.
Apart from the material properties of the transported air masses, the upper-level divergent
outflow of such ascended air streams also contribute to the negative \gls{pv} anomaly, 
an effect that has been thoroughly analyzed both theoretically and observationally 
\parencite{saffinCirculationConservationOutflow2021}.
These effects seem to be most important during onset and for more intense and larger
blocks, and are, naturally, often linked to upstream baroclinic developments---namely in
so-called \glspl{wcb} that transport warm, moist air from the warm sectors of surface cyclones
in the lower troposphere aloft. Since \glspl{wcb} are a central
topic of this thesis, they will be introduced in more detail later on. As a side note,
\textcite{zschenderleinLagrangianAnalysisUppertropospheric2020} observed that diabatically 
heated air parcels travelling into blocks may also have ascended convectively
rather than stratiformly through \glspl{wcb}, highlighting the uncertainty surrounding
these analyses. Organised convective processes are also thought to influence 
blocking dynamics climatologically through tropical teleconnections (see above).

The effect of diabatics on blocking development may be diagnosed elegantly
in quantative \gls{pv} frameworks. \textcite{neal2021PacificNorthwest2022}, for instance
applied \gls{lwa} theory for a particularly strong blocking episode and 
pointed to the critical importance of an upstream cyclone as a source of \gls{lwa}.
\textcite{liuBlockingDiversityCauses2025} applied it in a similar manner to find 
that diabatics may have opposing influences depending on the blocks morphology.

\textcite{hauserHolisticUnderstandingBlocked2023} and \textcite{hauserLifeCycleDynamics2024}
on the other hand combined
classical Eulerian and Lagrangian diagnostics with a quasi-Lagrangian quantative
\gls{pv} framework proposed by \textcite{teublerDynamicsRossbyWave2016} to show for blocks over Greenland
that diabatics are especially important at onset and maintanence, at a similar time
when quasi-barotropic \gls{pv} advection plays a prominent role as well.

The nature of these influences offers the unique opportunity to establish a more direct
cause-and-effect relationship than diagnostic correlations. In sensitivity experiments
with the \gls{ecmwf}s operational \gls{ifs}, \textcite{steinfeldSensitivityAtmosphericBlocking2020}
showed that when latent heating was selectively turned off, some blocks would 
become significantly weaker while others would not develop at all. Similarly, 
\textcite{deshmukhHowDoesDry2025} used a dry \gls{gcm} forced by realistic background
flow and found that while blockings still formed, their occurrence frequency was
reduced by roughly a third and their morphology severely altered.

Beyond latent heating aloft, lower-boundary diabatic forcing can precondition and
locally reinforce blocking highs. Using height-tendency diagnostics,
\textcite{tillyCalculatedHeightTendencies2008} demonstrate that surface sensible and
latent heat fluxes can contribute materially to height rises and thus to block
intensification, particularly during early development stages. On a climatological
level, it is well established that the Earth's surface tremendously shapes blocking
occurrence, not only through fluxes \parencite{heImpactLandSea2014}, but also through
friction and mountain torque \parencite{kallenBifurcationPropertiesQuasigeostrophic1982, metzWintertimeBlockingMountain1985}.

Basin-scale studies
emphasize that such surface forcing is not spatially uniform: \textcite{yamamotoOceanicMoistureSources2021}
identify key oceanic moisture source regions that feed latent-heating pathways associated
with Euro-Atlantic blocks, while \textcite{mathewsGulfStreamMoisture2024} show that
suppressing latent heat fluxes over the Gulf Stream alone significantly reduces  
blocking frequency, lifetime, intensity and spatial extent across the \gls{nh}. The explanation for
this is that turbulent heat fluxes over the Gulf Stream
and its extension generate boundary-layer low-\gls{pv} air masses that are subsequently
ventilated upward through \glspl{wcb} and contribute to the upper-level
negative-\gls{pv} anomaly of the block \parencite{mathewsOceanicMaintenanceAtmospheric2024}.
This mechanism seems to be dependent on \gls{sst} levels \parencite{narinesinghUniformSSTWarming2024} and
gradients \parencite{christSeaSkyUnderstanding2025} as well as model resolution, 
potentially mediated through underresolved 
extreme precipitation \parencite{strommenLinkGulfStream2025}. 

There is consequently growing attention to such small scale and moist processes
in the endavour to understand the biases in blocking representation in weather and
climate models \parencite{wandelWhyMoistDynamic2024, steinfeldResponseMoistDry2022, nabizadeh3DStructureNorthern2021}.
\textcite{dolores-tesillosRoleMoistDry2025} find blocking biases to be caused either by 
misrepresented \glspl{wcb} or through a biased mean flow. Remarkably, the pattern of 
that biased mean flow might be related to the notorious \enquote{too-zonal-too-equatorward} 
storm track bias in climate models, which, in turn, might also be linked to moist processes and their resolution
\parencite{auestadLatentHeatingFeedback2025, schemmEliminatingDecadesOld2023}.

Radiative cooling of anomalously low-pv air masses may play a role in the decay 
of a blocking, but the above studies suggest diabatic processes work more towards
counteracting the decay of a blocking. Even apart from latent heating, cloud radiative
effects upstream of a block may contribute to its maintenance \parencite{lubisCloudRadiativeEffects2025}.
Consequently, in a moist diabatic framework,
the decay of a block is linked more to the cessation of maintaining processes
and the eventual diffusive and advective dissipation of the anomalous air masses 
into the background flow consistent with eddy straining mechanisms considered as mediators
of scale interactions in turbulence theories \parencite{ditlevsenTurbulenceShellModels2010}.

\section{\Glspl{wcb}}\label{sec:intro_wcb}
 
\Glspl{wcb} are coherent, ascending airstreams that transport warm, moist air
from the warm sector of an extratropical cyclone in the lower troposphere
to the upper troposphere and lower stratosphere \parencite{schwenkRoleAscentTimescales2024}.
Its principle existence and effects
where present already in the classical theory of extratropical cyclones at the
polar front by \textcite{BjerknesSolberg1922}, but conceptualisation and dedicated
investigation took place later within the framework of the three-dimensional cyclone
conveyor-belt model \parencite[e.g.,~][]{browningStructureRainbandsMidlatitude1973},
which introduced them along with \glspl{ccb}, dry intrusions and (later) sting jets.

They were objects of interest originally due to their associated rain bands, but have
since also been recognized as important dynamical drivers of synoptic scale baroclinic dynamics
\parencite{madonnaWarmConveyorBelts2014a, binderRoleWarmConveyor2016} and, in effect,
the large-scale mid-latitude flow \parencite{vishnupriyaInteractionWarmConveyor2025, blanchardMidlevelConvectionWarm2021}.
Due to their considerable forcing and moisture content, \glspl{wcb} commonly account
for precipitation extremes in the extratropics on
daily timescales \parencite{pfahlWarmConveyorBelts2014}. They are a regular part
of extratropical cyclones in any time of year with a maximum occurrence in the 
cold season in either hemisphere. Their baroclinic nature makes them most prominent
in the storm tracks, most notably in the North Atlantic and Pacific sectors \parencite{madonnaWarmConveyorBelts2014a}.

\subsection{Definition} \label{sec:intro_wcb_def}
As mentioned above, \glspl{wcb} were traditionally identified subjectively 
by their associated stratiform cloud bands and vicinity to cyclones, but research
has developed more objective methods applicable to model or reanalysis data.
Nowadays, \glspl{wcb} are typically identified in a Lagrangian sense as traced air parcels that ascend
rapidly while undergoing substantial latent heating. A common criterion to select
\glspl{wcb} apart from vicinity to a cyclone is to require an ascent of at least 600 hPa
 within 2 days \parencite[e.g.,~][]{madonnaWarmConveyorBelts2014a}
which goes back to seminal work by \textcite{wernliLagrangianbasedAnalysisExtratropical1997} and
\textcite{wernliLagrangianbasedAnalysisExtratropical1997a}, though other thresholds and
variables have been used in the past, too \parencite{stohl1yearLagrangianClimatology2001,eckhardt15yearClimatologyWarm2004}.
Identifying \glspl{wcb}
from trajectories is, of course, only possible post-hoc, which presents a problem
since \glspl{wcb} are also of practical interest in weather forecasting. This is
why \textcite{quintingEuLerianIdentificationAscending2022a} have recently developed
a machine-learning based approach to identify \glspl{wcb} in an Eulerian framework.
Given relevant meteorological fields, their method can identify \glspl{wcb} in their
inflow, ascent and outflow phases with good accuracy, especially in regions of high
climatological \gls{wcb} occurrence when applied various datasets \parencite{quintingEuLerianIdentificationAscending2022}.

While the \gls{wcb} concept suggests a geometrically coherent airstream, this coherence
is not guaranteed by threshold-based selection of individual trajectories.
Moreover, such selections do not directly yield statements about the number, size,
location, or lifetime of distinct \glspl{wcb} in a given situation and the choice of 
thresholds is always somewhat arbitrary. This motivates a
complementary viewpoint that conceives of \glspl{wcb} as clusters of trajectories, 
i.e.~ensembles of air-parcel paths that remain mutually close judging by some metric
over a finite time window.

More generally, trajectory clustering has a long history in geoscience---in oceanography
in particular \parencite{vansebilleLagrangianOceanAnalysis2018}, but also in atmospheric sciences,
like in air-quality and (moisture) transport studies, where large sets of
trajectories are grouped into a small number of representative transport pathways
\parencite{stohlComputationAccuracyApplications1998,capeUseTrajectoryCluster2000}.
In these applications, clustering is often performed on trajectory positions at selected
times or along a limited portion of the path, which already acknowledges that the
geometry of trajectories can matter beyond instantaneous location, albeit often
with relatively simple similarity measures \parencite{stohlComputationAccuracyApplications1998}.

At the same time, trajectory clustering is a broad and evolving methodological field in its own right
\parencite{yuanReviewMovingObject2017,graserMobilityDLReviewDeep2025}, and atmospheric
applications increasingly adopt multivariate and time-windowed similarity notions that
better reflect physically meaningful airstream coherence. For instance,
\textcite{hartDetectionCoherentAirstreams2015} agglomeratively cluster trajectories using distances in a
standardized multivariate trajectory space over finite time windows, and post hoc interpretation of the resulting
clusters recovers \glspl{wcb} and \glspl{ccb}; related
methodology has also been applied in the context of heatwaves
\parencite{spensbergerDynamicsConcurrentSequential2020}. Though heuristically 
relatable, the approach falls short of presenting a reasonable justification for 
why different standardized variables should be weighted equally and by which
standardization. It also does not directly yield a measure of cluster quality,
instead relying on the within-group versus between group standard deviations across the
clusters as by \textcite{wernliLagrangianbasedAnalysisExtratropical1997}.

Conversely, in settings where the expected geometry is strongly constrained, trajectory
similarity can be tailored to the phenomenon, e.g.~via shape-based metrics such as
Fr\'echet distances (which measures distances under reparametrization of time) 
and rotational and translational invariances in the context of
tropical cyclones \parencite{kremerClusterAnalysisTailored2020} or by clustering 
trajectory segments to identify updrafts in hail storms \parencite{adams-selinThreedimensionalHailTrajectory2023}.
Such invariances are not directly applicable to \glspl{wcb}, but they highlight 
that trajectories may be clustered either by similarity in shape
or by time-local coherence. 

The latter notion invites a formalization of the visual notion of \enquote{trajectories staying
together} by concepts of Lagrangian coherence such as coherent sets or
Lagrangian coherent structures, which aim to identify time-evolving regions that resist
dispersion and mixing under small perturbations \parencite{hallerCoherentLagrangianVortices2013,hallerLagrangianCoherentStructures2015}.
In the coherent-set framework, robustness can be quantified via diffusion-based operators
estimated from trajectory data, whose spectral properties reveal coherent partitions
\parencite{banischUnderstandingGeometryTransport2017,froylandAnalyticFrameworkIdentifying2013,froylandDynamicIsoperimetryGeometry2015}.
A variety of related approaches to partition trajectory data into coherent sets or
transport barriers has been developed 
\parencite{froylandRoughandreadyClusterbasedApproach2015,allshouseLagrangianBasedMethods2015,
hadjighasemSpectralclusteringApproachLagrangian2016,schlueter-kuckCoherentStructureColouring2017,
padberg-gehleNetworkbasedStudyLagrangian2017,koltaiLargeDeviationsSemidistances2018,mowlaviDetectingLagrangianCoherent2022}.
This perspective is also highly adaptable, since it does not assume specific 
geometries and considers full trajectories rather than selected slices. 

Arguably,
spatio-temporal coherence is more appropriate for the identification of \glspl{wcb} 
than shape-based similarity, since trajectories of similar shape may belong to different 
\glspl{wcb}. The operator theoretic framework also provides a natural way to 
assess coherence through the robustness against small random perturbations.
Together, these developments suggest that complementing threshold-based \gls{wcb}
identification with bundle- or coherence-based trajectory partitioning can help address
questions of pathway commonality and of the spatio-temporal extent of distinct airstreams.

\subsection{Dynamics} \label{sec:intro_wcb_dynamics}
 During ascent, latent heat release strongly modifies the thermodynamic and
dynamical structure of the flow, leading to changes in potential vorticity and
the amplification or redistribution of upper-level wave features.
In particular, transport of air masses upward and poleward entails deposition of 
(negatively in the \gls{nh}) anomalous \gls{pv} near the tropopause, which, along with divergent upper-level
outflow can reinforce downstream ridges
and thereby influence the evolution of large-scale flow patterns. The material change of
latent heat release along a particular trajectory depends on the location of maximum
heating, but integrates roughly to zero \parencite{methvenPotentialVorticityWarm2015}. The effect of 
diabatic processes on the \gls{pv} structure of cyclones \parencite{chagnonDiabaticProcessesModifying2013}
and the jet stream \parencite{harveyDiabaticGenerationNegative2020} is well established
and supports the hypothesis of a first order importance for the large-scale flow. 
There exist considerable regional and case-to-case disparities in the interaction between \glspl{wcb} 
and the large-scale flow \parencite{vishnupriyaInteractionWarmConveyor2025}, complicating
the establishment of a general theory for the phenomenon. For instance,
the \glspl{wcb} outflow often splits into multiple branches, with a anticyclonically
turning branch associated with the strongest upper-level \gls{pv} anomalies and 
according downstream ridge amplification \parencite{heitmannWarmConveyorBelt2024}.

Because they link boundary-layer moisture reservoirs with the upper-tropospheric
waveguide, WCBs play a central role in mediating interactions between synoptic-scale
baroclinic development and the planetary-scale circulation. Accordingly, the intensification
phase of a surface cyclones often coincides with the strongest ascent phase
of a \gls{wcb} \parencite{heitmannWarmConveyorBelt2024} and the intensification of a cyclone
determines the amount of air masses involved in such a conveyor belt \parencite{binderRoleWarmConveyor2016}. 
This is due to an implied positive feedback loop between the cyclogenesis and 
the latent heat release, enough moisture provided. In addition, the amount
of latent heating in a \gls{wcb} determines the strength of the ascent and, in turn,
the speed and outflow level rather than leaving an imprint on the material change
in \gls{pv} across the ascent directly \parencite{methvenPotentialVorticityWarm2015}.
This implies that \glspl{wcb} and their large-scale effect is highly sensitive
to the (small-scale) variables governing ascent. \textcite{schaflerImpactInflowMoisture2015}
observed that the correct measurement or assimilation of moisture upstream of the WCB considerably
influences the forecasted effect of a WCB event especially with respect to tropopause and jet stream dynamics.
Though associated with stratiform ascent in the classical cyclone theory, there is
also evidence for embedded mid-level convection within \glspl{wcb} which alters 
the \gls{pv} structure locally modifying the large scale flow downstream
\parencite{oertelConvectiveActivityExtratropical2019, oertelPotentialVorticityStructure2020}.
Notably, \textcite{blanchardMidlevelConvectionWarm2021} point to intensifying effects on the jet stream,
though the effects of embedded convection in \glspl{wcb} seem to play a secondary
role climatologically due to the rarity of its occurrence
\parencite{oertelInteractionMicrophysicsDynamics2023}.

\section{Predictability, error-growth and uncertainty}\label{sec:intro_predictability}

Predicting the weather and climate of the future is arguably the core goal of
atmospheric sciences as a whole, so it comes as no surprise that the study of 
predictability is wide and deep. From a forecasting perspective, errors and, thus,
predictability limits may be decomposed into two main sources:
uncertainties in the initial conditions due to imperfect observations and data
assimilation and imperfect models due to under- or unresolved processes and 
model biases. The differentiation is somewhat arbitrary since model errors also
influence the initial condition through data assimilation (cf.~\cref{sec:intro_eda})
and modify the error growth characteristics of the system, yet it provides a
paradigm for diagnosing error sources. Crucially, both aspects of predictability
are dependent on the flow state and lead time.

\subsection{Chaos and initial conditions} \label{sec:intro_predictability_chaos}
The atmosphere is a chaotic system with rich
dynamics on a vast range of scales generally rendering definitive forecasts
impossible beyond a certain time horizon, often referred to as the predictability limit.
One generally distinguishes between intrinsic and practical predictability 
\parencite{sunIntrinsicPracticalLimits2016, selzTransitionPracticalIntrinsic2022}. The former is a 
property of the system itself and describes the time it takes for infinitesimally
small perturbations to grow to a size comparable to the natural variability of the system.
The latter, on the other hand, assumes realistic initial condition uncertainty
and is therefore always shorter than the intrinsic predictability.

Both notions connect to the concept of the butterfly effect, which describes the 
exponential growth of small perturbations in a chaotic system and, therefore,
its sensitive dependence on initial conditions \parencite{lorenzDeterministicNonperiodicFlow1963,lorenzPredictabilityFlowWhich1969}.


This property is intricatly linked to scale interactions 


There is a wealth of literature studying the processes responsible for error-growth
in atmospheric models at different lead times \parencite{krishnamurthyPredictabilityWeatherClimate2019}.
Error-growth is flow dependent on short
\parencite{puhFlowDependenceForecast2024, rodwellUncertaintyGrowthForecast2023},
medium \parencite{baumgartProcessesGoverningAmplification2019,
baumgartQuantitativeViewProcesses2019, sanchezLinkingRapidForecast2020} and long
\parencite{spaethFlowDependenceEnsembleSpread2024, ardilouzeFlowDependenceWintertime2021,
buelerYearroundSubseasonalForecast2021, osmanMultimodelAssessmentSubseasonal2023,
spaethFlowDependenceEnsembleSpread2024} time scales as well as being dependent
on the initial perturbation characteristics \parencite{raynaudComparisonInitialPerturbation2016,
wangImpactsMultiscaleComponents2023}, hinting at a variety of processes responsible.
The short time scale relevant here exhibits error growth characterized especially
by deep and shallow convection \parencite{selzUpscaleErrorGrowth2015,
selzEstimatingIntrinsicLimit2019, judtInsightsAtmosphericPredictability2018,
bechtoldRoleShallowConvection2014}, that later projects onto the larger spatial
and temporal scales, effectively limiting predictability
\parencite{sanchezLinkingRapidForecast2020, zhangMesoscalePredictabilityMoist2007}.

The dominant role of convective instability for initial error growth has been
attested using convection-permitting models, which have in turn been set as
benchmarks for coarser models relying on parametrization of sub-grid scale processes.
A crucial insight into the long history of parametrization development
\parencite{christensenParametrizationWeatherClimate2022} has been that
ensemble forecast quality suffers from incorrect dispersion and biases when not
accounting for model uncertainty by using deterministic physics parametrizations.
This fueled the development and implementation of stochastically perturbed physics
schemes \parencite{palmerStochasticWeatherClimate2019}.

A line of study concerned with elucidating the exact way in which stochastic
schemes influence biases in the representation of blockings
\parencite{filippucciImpactStochasticPhysics2024, daviniRepresentationWinterNorthern2021,
hartungResolutionPhysicsAtmosphere2017, maddisonImpactModelUpgrades2020} and weather
regimes more generally \parencite{christensenSimulatingWeatherRegimes2015,
dawsonSimulatingWeatherRegimes2015}. Results from idealized (atmospheric)
models have shown how zero-mean additive noise can enhance the regime behaviour
in dynamical systems \parencite{kwasniokEnhancedRegimePredictability2014,
dorringtonInteractionStochasticForcing2023}. It is not far-fetched to
hypothesize that this exact behaviour plays a role in full-complexity models
and the real world, as well. Apart from underresolved topographic forcing and
transient eddy feedback \parencite{dawsonSimulatingWeatherRegimes2015}, warm
conveyor belts (WCBs) and their interactions with the large scale flow have
been suggested as drivers of error growth 
\parencite{gramsAtmosphericDynamicsPerspective2018, picklWarmConveyorBelts2023,
wandelSystematicEvaluationWarm2021, madonnaVerificationNorthAtlantic2015},
especially in interaction with SPPT \parencite{picklEffectStochasticallyPerturbed2022}.
The relevance of WCBs for atmospheric blocking representation is
well-established \parencite{pfahlImportanceLatentHeat2015, steinfeldRoleLatentHeating2019,
yamamotoOceanicMoistureSources2021, zschenderleinLagrangianAnalysisUppertropospheric2020}
and representation biases have been linked to moist dynamics \parencite{wandelWhyMoistDynamic2024}.
We emphasize that influence of stochastic schemes on regime representation unfolds 
on longer time scales that the ones relevant for the EDA, but the studies referenced
above still give indication as to when and how ensemble spread in EDA is associated with weather regimes.

\subsection{Predictability of blockings} \label{sec:intro_predictability_blockings}
Atmospheric blocking events are frequently discussed in the context of operational
predictability on subseasonal to seasonal timescales in particular.
On the one hand, this is due to their extraordinary impact on
natural and societal systems outlined above. On the other hand, \gls{nwp} models
have historically struggled to accurately forecast blocking events, especially
their onset and decay \parencite{dandreaNorthernHemisphereAtmospheric1998, matsuedaBlockingPredictabilityOperational2009,tibaldiOperationalPredictabilityBlocking1990}
whereas medium-range foreast skill is sometimes enhanced during blocking episodes
(\cite{spaethFlowDependenceEnsembleSpread2024}; cf.~also \cite{prestel-kupfererPredictabilityMidlatitudeRossbywave2024}
for a similar behaviour for \glspl{rwp}). As will be discussed below, the onset phase
of blockings is notoriously rich in processes on a wide range of scales, especially
with respect to upstream development like cyclogenesis, which projects onto its 
predictability \parencite{maddisonUpstreamCycloneInfluence2019}.
 Based on the weather regime classification
introduced by \textcite{gramsBalancingEuropesWindpower2017} (cf.~\cite{gramsLifeCycleDefinition2026}
for an updated version),
\textcite{buelerYearroundSubseasonalForecast2021} and \textcite{osmanMultimodelAssessmentSubseasonal2023}
provided evidence that blockings are both the best and worst predicted regimes in the Euro-Atlantic sector,
depending on their location with a blocking over Greenland achieving the longest and
blockings over Central Europe or Scandinavia the shortest forecast skill horizon.
In a similar manner, but using a different set of regimes and only data in winter,
\textcite{ferrantiFlowdependentVerificationECMWF2015} found that operational
forecasts struggled most with transitions to a blocking regime over Northern Europe
while also underestimating their persistence.

The issues faced by \gls{nwp} models in forecasting blockings are reflected in
atmospheric models' difficulties in representing them realistically in the first place,
which is apparent from considerable biases in blocking frequency, intensity and persistence
\parencite{daviniNorthernHemisphereAtmospheric2016}. \textcite{leeIncreasingFrequencyPersistence2026}
recently reported that a seasonal large-ensemble prediction model was able to
reproduce Greenland blocking frequency and persistence but failed to reproduce its trend
due to climate change, making the picture even more complex.
It seems like there are multiple causes for the misrepresentation of blockings,
ranging from insufficient model resolution, coupling to other earth systems
and representation of sub-grid scale processes---all of which exacerbated in the case
of climate models \parencite{woollingsBlockingItsResponse2018}. In an evaluation of the
operational seasonal prediction model of the \gls{ecmwf}, \textcite{daviniRepresentationWinterNorthern2021}
found that while improvements have taken place, significant biases remain in the
European sector in particular. They note that resolution increases improved blocking
representation considerably and highlight that errors in the atmospheric system are
most relevant as opposed to errors in ocean representation, though that behaviour seems
to be different in the climate setting. Climatological evaluations of blocking representation
sometimes also have difficulties in identifying robust signals due to the large 
interannual variability of blocking occurrence \parencite{hartungResolutionPhysicsAtmosphere2017}.

In an evaluation of \gls{cmip} 6, the latest generation
of climate models, \textcite{maddisonMissingIncreaseSummer2024} found that the
increase in summer blocking over Greenland observed in since the beginning of
the century is not reprocuced and suggest this might be linked to \glspl{sst},
sea ice biases or anthopogenic aerosols. Indeed, given what has been mentioned about
the interaction of atmospheric blockings
with other earth system components varying on longer timescales, it seems natural to expect
that the the predictive skill of models predicting blockings depends on whether they
can accurately represent those components and their interaction with blockings.

\subsection{Uncertainty in \glspl{wcb}} \label{sec:intro_wcb_uncertainty}

As outlined above, \glspl{wcb} are tightly coupled to extratropical cyclogenesis 
and the downstream evolution of the large scale flow through intense diabatic heating 
and transport of air masses. As a consequence, even small initial-condition or model 
errors in \gls{wcb} inflow, ascent, and cloud–precipitation processes can undergo 
rapid error growth and project efficiently onto baroclinic and diabatically driven
instabilities of the developing cyclone, thereby amplifying ensemble spread and 
forecast uncertainty downstream \parencite{rodwellWhyWarmConveyor2018,baumgartProcessesGoverningAmplification2019,
wandelSystematicEvaluationWarm2021}.
\textcite{picklWarmConveyorBelts2023} show that for medium range forecasts in the 
Euro-Atlantic sector, high \gls{wcb} activity is associated with anticyclonic
patterns downstream and elevated levels of overall forecast uncertainty. They argue that
this is due to upstream initial condition errors as well as model errors intrinsic to
processes dominant in the cyclone.
This sensitivity makes the parametrized physics within \gls{wcb}s a first-order
uncertainty source: deep-convection schemes can substantially alter \gls{wcb}
structure and its interaction with the jet \parencite{riviereImpactDeepConvection2021},
while microphysical parametrizations modulate latent heating profiles, and precipitation
characteristics \parencite{joosInfluenceMicrophysicalProcesses2012,mazoyerMicrophysicsImpactsWarm2021,mazoyerImpactMixedphaseCloud2023}. 

Importantly, seemingly minor microphysics changes can induce large downstream flow 
differences—consistent with the idea that \gls{wcb} outflow perturbations can trigger 
disproportionate Rossby-wave responses and regime transitions \parencite{joosImpactDifferentIFS2016}. 
This linkage is particularly acute for high-impact forecasts involving blocking: 
misrepresentation of \gls{wcb} outflow has been identified as a key factor in severe
forecast busts with a missed blocking onset \parencite{gramsAtmosphericDynamicsPerspective2018},
but also more systematically associated with elevated forecast uncertainty in blocking events \parencite{wandelWhyMoistDynamic2024}. 
Targeted experiments demonstrate that upstream latent heating in \gls{wcb}s exerts 
strong control on blocking development and maintenance \parencite{steinfeldSensitivityAtmosphericBlocking2020}. 
Case studies further underline that diabatic processes can generate anomalous PV features 
and materially alter the jet structure, reinforcing the dynamical pathway from 
\gls{wcb} physics errors to large-scale uncertainty \parencite{harveyDiabaticGenerationNegative2020}.
In addition, stochastic perturbations with zero mean have been shown to exert a
non-zero mean effect on \gls{wcb} by triggering nonlinear instabilities, making them
more frequent and more intense \parencite{picklEffectStochasticallyPerturbed2022}.

Consistently, \textcite{madonnaVerificationNorthAtlantic2015} have found improvements
in the representation of \glspl{wcb} across the 2000s in the \gls{ifs}, while 
still noting cases of severe forecast errors and \textcite{maddisonImpactModelUpgrades2020} find that model upgrades 
aimed at diabatic and cloud-process realism can mitigate \gls{wcb}-related forecast busts. 
More recently, sensitivity analyses have also provided insights into whether uncertainty 
is dominated by parametric choices or by the synoptic inflow state, 
with recent evidence suggesting that inflow conditions can control bulk ascent and
flow modification while microphysics primarily imprints fine-scale precipitation
structure \parencite{hieronymusAlgorithmicDifferentiationSensitivity2022,oertelSensitivitiesWarmConveyor2025}.
Whether this holds for all flow states, however, remains an open question. Nevertheless, it 
it is fair to say that \glspl{wcb} can be seen as propagators and magnifiers
of forecast uncertainty highlighting the need for further understanding. 

\section{\Gls{eda}}\label{sec:intro_eda}

Data assimilation constitutes an active field of research on its own and a 
comprehensive introduction into the topic is provided elsewhere 
(e.g.~\cite{parkPrinciplesDataAssimilation2022} and 
\cite{parkDataAssimilationAtmospheric2022} for recent research directions). 
Very basically, given a dynamical system (like the atmosphere/Earth), data 
assimilation is concerned with optimally combining a dynamical model of the 
system with observations to obtain an estimate of the system's most likely 
state - either to obtain an accurate initial condition for numerical 
prediction or to obtain a coherent \enquote{history} of the atmosphere (reanalysis).  
A crucial part in this play uncertainties for both the model and the 
observations as well as the best estimate. The approach applied in the 
production of \gls{era5} as well as for finding the initial condition for the 
ECMWF's operational forecasting model \gls{ifs}, an incremental hybrid ensemble 4D-Var system, is described by 
\textcite{hersbachERA5GlobalReanalysis2020} and in more detail by 
\textcite{bonavitaEvolutionECMWFHybrid2016}, which this section largely follows. 
Such or similar data assimilation schemes have been introduced by a number of 
institutions with considerable success 
\parencite{kalnayDataAssimilation2024}.

\subsection{The \gls{era5} data assimilation system} \label{sec:intro_eda_era5}

Denote\footnote{The notation referring to the assimilation methodology 
follows \textcite{ideUnifiedNotationData1997} for consistency and is notably 
different to notation elsewhere.} 
by $\mathbf{d} = \mathbf{y}^{\mathrm{O}} - \mathbf{y}$ the misfit between the 
observations and the guess projected into observation space by 
$\mathbf{y} = H(\mathbf{x}) + b(\mathbf{x}, \boldsymbol{\beta})$, 
where $H$ is the observation operator, $\mathbf{x}$ is the guess and $b$ is 
the (instrument-specific) bias operator with bias parameters 
$\boldsymbol{\beta}$. In each of the 12-hourly time windows through which the 
production of the data set iterates, the best estimate - the \enquote{analysis} 
$\mathbf{x}^{\mathrm{a}}$ - is obtained as the minimizer of the following 
cost function (a variational formulation, hence the \enquote{Var}):

\begin{equation}\label{eqn:Jmin}
    J(\boldsymbol{\delta} \mathbf{x}, \boldsymbol{\delta \beta})=
    \underbrace{\frac{1}{2}{\boldsymbol{\delta} \mathbf{x}}^T 
    \mathbf{B}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\delta} \mathbf{x}}_{\text{background term}} 
    + 
    \underbrace{\frac{1}{2}\boldsymbol{\delta \beta}^T 
    \mathbf{B}_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\delta \beta}}
    _{\text{bias term}} 
    + 
    \underbrace{\frac{1}{2}\mathbf{d}^T 
    \mathbf{R}^{-1}\mathbf{d}}_{\text{misfit term}},
\end{equation}

where $\boldsymbol{\delta}\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{x} - \mathbf{x}^{\mathrm{b}}$ 
and $\boldsymbol{\delta \beta}= \boldsymbol{\beta} - 
\boldsymbol{\beta}^{\mathrm{b}}$ are the increments from the initial guess 
(the background) obtained from the last window directly (for 
$\boldsymbol{\beta}$) or via numerical integration of the model 
(for $\mathbf{x}$). The solution of equation~\eqref{eqn:Jmin} - 
i.e. the minimizing $\mathbf{x}^{\mathrm{a}}$ - is also referred to as 
\gls{hres} in the context of \gls{era5}. To achieve a balance between observations and 
model prediction that is scaled by their respective uncertainty, each of the 
three terms contains weighting by the appropriate covariance matrices 
$\mathbf{B}, \mathbf{B_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}}$ and $\mathbf{R}$ 
\parencite{courtierStrategyOperationalImplementation1994}. 
$\mathbf{R}$ is instrument-dependent (more specifically, observation-
dependent, since, for instance, different spectral bands of some satellite 
radiance instrument can have different error characteristics). 
$\mathbf{B_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}}$ is adjusted to the desired rate of change 
allowed for the bias parameters.

Initially assumed constant in time, allowing the background error covariance 
matrix $\mathbf{B}$ to vary (i.e.~going from 3D-Var to 4D-Var) amounted to a 
tremendous improvement in data assimilation first implemented operationally 
in 1997 by the ECMWF \parencite{anderssonECMWFs4DVarData2008} based on theory 
laid out by \textcite{fisherBackgroundErrorCovariance2003}. The hybrid \gls{eda} system 
detailed below was introduced in 2010 
\parencite{isaksenEnsembleDataAssimilations2010} and further modified to the 
applied set up \parencite{bonavitaEvolutionECMWFHybrid2016}. For a survey of 
different approaches to ensemble based variational data assimilation see 
\textcite{bannisterReviewOperationalMethods2017}.

The background forecast uncertainty is calculated from a climatological and a 
time-varying ensemble derived part according to 

\begin{equation}
    \mathbf{B} = (1-\alpha)\mathbf{B}_{\text{cli}} 
    + \alpha \mathbf{B}_{\text{EDA}}
\end{equation}

with $\alpha$ increasing by wavenumber from $.15$ to $.74$. $\alpha$ was 
inadvertently kept constant during production of \gls{era5}, which affects the 
ensemble spread for data between January 2001 to June 2005, and from January 
2010 to October 2014. The climatological part 
$\mathbf{B}_{\text{cli}}$ is supposed to encompass the climatological 
component of the background uncertainty dominated by the respective 
instrument generation and has been kept constant from January 1940 to 
November 1972 (H.~Hersbach, 2025, personal communication), from November 
1972 to December 1978, from January 1979 to December 1999 and from January 
2000 onwards \parencite{bellERA5GlobalReanalysis2021}. 

$\mathbf{B}_{\text{EDA}}$ is calculated from the spread of a 10 member 
(for \gls{era5}; 1 control and 9 perturbed) ensemble run of 
eqn.~\eqref{eqn:Jmin} at a lower spatial resolution (TL319 as opposed to 
TL639) which, in the context of \gls{era5} is referred to as \gls{eda} 
 in contrast to \gls{hres}. The perturbations are 
added to the observations $\mathbf{y}^{\mathrm{O}}$ 
(including sea ice and sea surface temperature) and are, crucially, scaled 
by the expected observation uncertainties. Each member also calculates its 
own independent background forecast through numerical integration including 
stochastically perturbed physics (except for the control; 
\cite{ecmwfIFSDocumentationCY41R22016a, 
leutbecherStochasticRepresentationsModel2017}). Perturbed physics are also 
applied in the nonlinear part of the construction of $\mathbf{x}$ during the 
minimization of eqn.~\eqref{eqn:Jmin} - the so-called \enquote{outer-loop}. The \gls{eda} 
can be conceived as a Monte Carlo approach to the estimation of the 
posterior distribution of the background forecast error that is supplemented 
by a climatological component (hence, \enquote{hybrid}) due to the sampling errors 
introduced by its computationally demanded low sample size 
\parencite{bonavitaEstimatingBackgrounderrorVariances2011, 
bonavitaEvolutionECMWFHybrid2016, 
bannisterReviewOperationalMethods2017}. As such, it provides the 
\enquote{error-of-the-day} as an impactful improvement to the analysis quality  
compared to climatological background errors especially during extreme 
events \parencite{bonavitaUseEDABackground2012}, though its small sample size 
precludes trustworthy detailed structures 
\parencite{isaksenEnsembleDataAssimilations2010, 
bonavitaEstimatingBackgrounderrorVariances2011}. 

Apart from providing the flow-dependent part of the background forecast 
uncertainty, the \gls{eda} dataset serves also as a relative estimate of the 
uncertainty of the \gls{hres}\footnote{Relative in the sense that the numerical 
values of the ensemble spread should not be taken at face value, since the 
\gls{eda} is underdispersive \parencite{sociERA5GlobalReanalysis2024} and its small 
sample size entails substantial noise that would need to be filtered first 
\parencite{raynaudObjectiveFilteringEnsemblebased2009, 
bonavitaEstimatingBackgrounderrorVariances2011}.}. The dataset therefore offers
unique insights into the nature and sources of uncertainty. Not only is the climatological 
investigation of arguably the most-used reanalysis product to date a very 
relevant subject in its own right; the type of uncertainty comprised in the 
\gls{eda} is also less researched climatologically than others. More specifically, 
since assimilation windows are only 12 hours wide and the initial guess 
forecast is only 15 hours long, the uncertainty visible in the \gls{eda} spread 
can only be caused by uncertainties in the observations or short time scale 
error-growth. Since the spread of the \gls{eda} ensemble is indicative of this
uncertainty, the question of what exactly drives and controls 
ensemble spread deserves some attention.

\subsection{\Gls{eda} spread} \label{sec:intro_eda_spread}

Though variational bias correction is applied in the \gls{eda} production 
\parencite{deeBiasDataAssimilation2005}, bias parameter updates are taken 
from the control and, hence, not independent across the ensemble members 
\parencite{hersbachERA5GlobalReanalysis2020}. Neglecting the bias term in 
eqn.~\eqref{eqn:Jmin}, the linear approximation of the variance of the 
analysis increment 
$\delta\mathbf{x}^{\mathrm{a}}=\mathbf{x}^{\mathrm{a}}-\mathbf{x}^{\mathrm{b}}$ 
is therefore \parencite{deeDataAssimilationPresence1998, 
higham14MatrixInversion2002} 

\begin{align} \label{eqn:AnVar}
    \mathbb{E}\big[\delta\mathbf{x}^{\mathrm{a}}
    \delta\mathbf{x}^{\mathrm{a}^T}\big] 
    &= (\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{K}\mathbf{H})\mathbf{B}
    =(\mathbf{B}^{-1}+\mathbf{H}^T\mathbf{R}^{-1}\mathbf{H})^{-1},\,
    \text{where} \\
    \mathbf{H} 
    &= \left.  \frac{\partial H}{\partial \mathbf{x}}
    \right|_{\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{x}^{\mathrm{b}}}, \,
    \text{the linearized observation operator}\\
    \mathbf{K} 
    &=\mathbf{B}\mathbf{H}^\top
    \big(\mathbf{H}\mathbf{B}\mathbf{H}^\top+\mathbf{R}\big)^{-1}, \,
    \text{the Kalman gain.}
\end{align}

The causes for differences between ensemble members can be divided into 
three parts: firstly, the differences in the perturbations to the 
observations $\mathbf{y}^{\mathrm{O}}$ that are scaled by $\mathbf{R}$ and 
propagated through $\mathbf{H}^{-1}$, secondly, the differences in the 
background forecast $\mathbf{x}^{\mathrm{b}}$ that are scaled by 
$\mathbf{B}$ and, thirdly, the ensemble spread introduced by the physics 
perturbations by the full, non-linear outer-loop simulation across the 
analysis window that is affected by all terms in eqn.~\eqref{eqn:AnVar}.

\subsubsection{Observation Perturbations}\label{sec:intro_eda_obs}

Given the two purposes of the \gls{eda}, the rationale behind the observation 
perturbations becomes quite obvious. In the sense that the observations 
constrain the dynamical model, a stronger constrain should also induce a 
smaller spread of the ensemble around that constrain, so scaling the 
perturbations by respective expected observation error covariances makes 
sense and a larger observation uncertainty will entail a larger ensemble 
spread and thus convey less \enquote{trust} in the \gls{hres} product. As uncertain 
observations also project onto a more uncertain background forecast - a 
forecast is more uncertain if its initial conditions are more uncertain - 
applying observation perturbations to estimate background error covariances 
arguably confirms with intuition 
\parencite{isaksenEnsembleDataAssimilations2010}.

The \gls{eda} observation perturbations are drawn from the observation-error model 
used in the IFS: each perturbation variance equals the per-observation error 
variance (the diagonal of $\mathbf{R}$) or the operator-computed, 
scene-dependent variance for radiances and similar instruments 
\parencite{ecmwfIFSDocumentationCY41R22016, 
isaksenEnsembleDataAssimilations2010, 
hersbachERA5GlobalReanalysis2020}. This implies, for instance, that larger 
perturbations are applied for cloudy areas compared to clear-sky conditions 
(see also~\cite{geerObservationErrorsAllsky2011}). The variances are computed 
from instrument noise plus representativeness error, modified by the 
observation operator and routinely tuned 
\parencite{hollingsworthStatisticalStructureShort1984, 
desroziersDiagnosisObservationBackground2005}. Sea surface temperature and 
sea ice concentration perturbations are drawn according to 
\textcite{hiraharaSeaSurfaceTemperature2016}.

Apart from decreasing error variances through more accurate instruments, the 
density of the observation network also heavily influences ensemble spread. 
Adding observations increases the dimension of $\mathbf{R}$ effectively 
adding positive definite terms to 
$\mathbf{H}^T\mathbf{R}^{-1}\mathbf{H}$, whereby the expected analysis 
increment variance decreases. This has a dominant effect on ensemble spread 
since the number of observations ingested in the production of \gls{era5} has 
grown by more than 3 orders of magnitude between 1940 and today 
\parencite{sociERA5GlobalReanalysis2024}.

\subsubsection{Background and physics perturbations}\label{sec:intro_eda_bg_phys}

The short-range background forecast for each assimilation window is obtained by
numerical integration using the previous window's analysis three hours before
the start of the window as an initial condition. Note that the background
term in eqn.~\eqref{eqn:Jmin} is only evaluated at the assimilation window's
outset, while the short range forecast is used as an initial guess in the
misfit term throughout the full 12-hour width. Conceiving the ensemble of
previous analyses provided by the different members as a probability
distribution, the integration will cause the distribution to shift and deform
in model space in a fashion governed by the dynamical model's short time-scale
error growth characteristics.

The causes and mechanisms of error growth in the atmosphere and models 
representing it have been discussed above in \cref{sec:intro_predictability_chaos}.
Relevant for the \gls{eda} spread are the characteristics of short time-scale 
error growth dominated by deep and shallow convection. 
Exactly how the initial differences (effectively initial perturbations)
between the ensemble members influence the error growth during the short forecast
is hard to tell given their complex construction via EDA, but is very likely of
secondary importance compared to presence or absence of diabatic processes.
For medium to long time scale ensemble forecasts, perturbations from EDA have
been shown to be too slow-growing, negatively affecting forecast skill and
spread \parencite{buizzaPotentialUseEnsemble2008, sociERA5GlobalReanalysis2024},
which is why they are augmented with singular vector perturbations in the
operational ECMWF ensemble forecast \parencite{leutbecherEnsembleForecasting2008,
palmerECMWFEnsemblePrediction2019, langMoreAccuracyLess2021}.

The production of \gls{era5} features two stochastic physics schemes to alleviate
shortcommings in the the representation of sub-grid scale processes and their associated uncertainty;
the \gls{sppt} scheme and the
\gls{skeb} scheme
\parencite{ecmwfIFSDocumentationCY41R22016a, leutbecherStochasticRepresentationsModel2017}.
The \gls{sppt} scheme introduced by \cite{buizzaStochasticRepresentationModel1999}
and revised several times \parencite{lockTreatmentModelUncertainty2019,
leutbecherStochasticRepresentationsModel2017} is supposed to represent model
uncertainties in the representation of physical processes - diabatic processes
especially. The \gls{skeb} scheme on the other hand is motivated by turbulence
theory that predicts cascades that involve transfer of energy from small to
large scales (cf.~\cref{sec:intro_predictability_chaos}).
The scale interaction missing in the model due to truncation
introduce uncertainties that are parametrized by the \gls{skeb}. The development
goes back to \cite{shuttsKineticEnergyBackscatter2005} and
\cite{bernerSpectralStochasticKinetic2009}, for the relevant implementation 
see \cite{ecmwfIFSDocumentationCY41R22016a, leutbecherStochasticRepresentationsModel2017}.
Note that \gls{sppt} applies multiplicative noise whereas \gls{skeb} applies additive noise.


Both of the schemes used for \gls{era5} have been shown to partly alleviate the
shortcomings mentioned above \parencite{bernerSystematicModelError2012,
bernerStochasticParameterizationNew2017, dawsonSimulatingWeatherRegimes2015,
leutbecherStochasticRepresentationsModel2017, langRevisionStochasticallyPerturbed2021}
with the \gls{sppt} usually having a larger influence than the \gls{skeb}. Both schemes
increase \gls{eda} spread throughout the atmosphere, albeit most effectual atop the
planetary boundary layer \parencite{palmerStochasticParametrizationModel2009,
isaksenEnsembleDataAssimilations2010}. They have proven particularly potent at
correcting biases related to tropical convection \parencite{weisheimerAddressingModelError2014,
subramanianImpactStochasticPhysics2017, christensenStochasticParameterizationNino2017,
strommenImpactStochasticParametrisations2018}. Note than the \gls{ecmwf} has deactivated
the \gls{skeb} scheme in 2018 \parencite{ecmwfIFSDocumentationCY45R12018} and replaced
the \gls{sppt} with the \gls{spp} scheme
\parencite{ollinahoProcesslevelRepresentationModel2017, langRevisionStochasticallyPerturbed2021}
in 2024 \parencite{ecmwfIFSDocumentationCY49R12024}.

Finally, if all ensemble members were supplied with the same background forecast
and observations (without perturbations), they would still result in different
analysis. This is because the perturbed physics parametrizations introduced
above are applied in the iterative minimization of $\mathbf{x}$ by the full
non-linear \gls{ifs} (the outer-loop). As a sidenote, even without perturbed
physics, turbulent cascades might introduce some ensemble spread by amplifying
numerical errors, albeit much smaller in magnitude
\parencite{leithAtmosphericPredictabilityTwoDimensional1971,
leutbecherEnsembleForecasting2008, langMoreAccuracyLess2021}.
