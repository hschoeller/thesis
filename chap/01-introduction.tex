\section{Atmospheric blocking}\label{sec:intro_blocking}
The term \enquote{atmospheric blocking} emerged in weather forecast and
atmospheric research in the early 20\textsuperscript{th} century initially to
describe persistent
surface high-pressure regions \enquote{blocking} the usual eastward progression
of low-pressure cyclones (\textcite{rexBlockingActionMiddle1950}; cf.~also
\textcite{lupoAtmosphericBlockingEvents2021} and references therein).
It has since come to be understood as a large-scale disturbance
extending through the whole troposphere.
The term \enquote{blocking} or simply \enquote{block} notably does not refer
to its geometric shape as a somewhat coherent singular entity, especially since
they can come in different shapes and sizes and are usually embedded in the
wavelike structure of the flow.

Though there is still a lack of consensus on the exact definition of a blocking
and a coherent unifying theory of them is still
at large \parencite{wangGapsWaysForward2026}, the concept broadly encompasses
the notions of a persistent, quasi-stationary, large-scale anticyclonic flow
pattern that occurs in the mid- to high-latitudes. Its persistence implies
lifetimes on the order of days to weeks, which is long compared to typical
synoptic weather systems. It is quasi-stationary in that its characteristic
flow pattern and position almost do not change over its lifetime measured by
the usual magnitudes of change on those timescales.
% This is why some theories liken the phenomenon to quasi-stationary (standing)
% planetary waves analogous to waves in other physical systems
% \parencite[e.g.,~][]{tungTheoryStationaryLong1979,
% nakamuraAtmosphericBlockingTraffic2018}.

Any weather situation considered a blocking usually involves considerable
displacement of relatively warm and low \gls{pv} subtropical air masses
poleward, introducing or reinforcing a ridge in the upper-level
flow\footnote{Throughout, \gls{pv} is discussed in terms of its magnitude;
    signed \gls{pv} is positive in the \gls{nh} and negative in the \gls{sh},
    increasing in magnitude poleward in each. Statements about \enquote{low} or
    \enquote{high} \gls{pv} therefore refer to magnitude relative to the poleward
    background.}. In severe
cases this ridge might even detach from the subtropical reservoir judging from
closed contours of low \gls{pv} values on isentropic surfaces or, equivalently,
high potential temperature values on surfaces of constant \gls{pv}
\parencite{pellyNewPerspectiveBlocking2003}.

\subsection{Definitions} \label{sec:intro_block_def}
Consistent with the rather diffuse understanding introduced above, there is no
universally accepted formal definition of atmospheric blocking, and different
diagnostics are adopted depending on purpose, scale of analysis and, arguably,
school of thought \parencite{barnesMethodologyComparisonBlocking2012,
    barriopedroApplicationBlockingDiagnosis2010}. Blockings are frequently defined
either by \emph{indices} specifically designed for them or as one of a number
of different \emph{weather regimes} which, in turn, result from some kind of
clustering of instantaneous atmospheric states.

\emph{Indices} may be differentiated by which meteorological field they use and by
whether they identify a blocking based on absolute or anomaly fields. The
choice of index will importantly also determine the precise shape of the
detected field and will identify different parts of the blocking
\parencite{pinheiroAtmosphericBlockingIntercomparison2019,
    barnesMethodologyComparisonBlocking2012}.
For instance, one very common group of indices is based on the
geopotential height field and its gradient or the associated synoptic winds.
This rather kinematic perspective exploits the blocking's characteristic
meridional flow field as a hallmark and is based on work by
\textcite{lejenasCharacteristicsNorthernHemisphere1983,
    tibaldiOperationalPredictabilityBlocking1990}. It is illustrated by the
green shading in \cref{fig:block_def}.
More dynamically and
object-oriented indices identify and track anomalies in the fields of either
geopotential
height or \gls{pv} or
potential temperature
\parencite{schwierzPerspicaciousIndicatorsAtmospheric2004}. Studying blockings
through \gls{pv} was
pioneered by \textcite{pellyNewPerspectiveBlocking2003} though they identified
gradient reversals instead of anomalies. Since potential temperature and
\gls{pv} are materially conserved under adiabatic and (in the case of \gls{pv})
inviscid conditions, air-masses that are designated as belonging to a blocking
may be traced back and forth to understand the formation and decay of the
blocking event materially. Anomaly based indices are illustrated by the
orange shading in \cref{fig:block_def}.

A complementary perspective conceptualises blocking as a preferred large-scale
\emph{circulation regime} within a reduced state space of atmospheric
variability.
This understanding originates from the interpretation of the atmosphere as a
forced-dissipative dynamical system whose long-term behaviour is governed by
its lower-dimensional attractor \parencite[e.g.,~][]{
    dijkstraNonlinearClimateDynamics2013,weilandIntransitiveAtmosphereDynamics2021,
    strommenTopologicalPerspectiveWeather2023, ghilReviewArticleDynamical2023}.
Temporal evolution guides the system as a trajectory on the attractor with
its location in state space determining its dynamic behaviour as well as its
expression in physical space. It may then be insightful to partition this
attractor into a finite set of metastable or recurrent regimes as a
dimensionality reduction \parencite{michelangeliWeatherRegimesRecurrence1995,
    badzaIdentifyingOnsetDecay2024}.
Blocking emerges as one of multiple such \emph{weather regimes}, defined by
persistence and transition probabilities rather than local anomalies or
gradient reversals in the Eulerian field.

Studying the atmosphere according to a small number of characteristic regimes
or clusters is probably as old as synoptic meteorology itself and has been a
ubiquitous tool in atmospheric sciences ever since \parencite{
    hannachiLowFrequencyNonlinearity2017}, especially in the North
Atlantic--European sector \parencite[e.g.,~][]{
    vautardMultipleWeatherRegimes1990,nealFlexibleApproachDefining2016,
    gramsLifeCycleDefinition2026}. Given the enormous dimensionality of the
atmospheric system, meaningful insight into the underlying attractor's
structure may necessitate abstraction into low-order models with some
exhibiting blocking-like regimes \parencite{
    crommelinMechanismAtmosphericRegime2004,
    kallenBifurcationPropertiesQuasigeostrophic1982,
    lucariniNewMathematicalFramework2020} facilitating translation between
paradigm models and the real world. This is depicted in the top right inset of
\cref{fig:block_def}, where a subset of the state space is identified with
a blocking regime indicated by brown shading.

Finally, from a \emph{wave-dynamical} perspective, blocking can be interpreted as a
non-linear manifestation of amplified, breaking, or quasi-stationary
Rossby waves \parencite{tungTheoryStationaryLong1979,
    nakamuraAtmosphericBlockingTraffic2018}. In this framework, blockings are
associated with \glspl{rwp} \parencite{wirthRossbyWavePackets2018}
or maxima of \gls{lwa} fluxes \parencite{huangLocalFiniteamplitudeWave2016}
and will thus be identified based on characteristic
patterns in the wavenumber rather than the physical domain. Wave activity is
illustrated by the blue shading in \cref{fig:block_def}.

The differing definitions---\emph{index}-based, \emph{regime}-based, and
\emph{wave-dynamical}---highlight that blocking may be understood alternately as
a local kinematic signature, a metastable circulation state, or a
planetary-wave configuration, depending on the theoretical
and diagnostic perspective adopted.

\subsection{Phenomenology} \label{sec:intro_block_Phenomenology}
Climatologically, blocking events are more prominent in the \gls{nh}
compared to the \gls{sh} due to a stronger land--sea contrast and orography
\parencite{woollingsBlockingItsResponse2018}. They are also larger, more
intense and more frequent in winter than in summer, with a peak frequency in
the North Atlantic sector
\parencite{nabizadeh3DStructureNorthern2021}. Aside from the North Atlantic,
the North Pacific and, to a lesser extent, the Eurasian sector are also
preferred regions for blocking occurrence in the \gls{nh}. Their implied
temporal and spatial scales lets them exert a major influence on the
mid-latitude circulation and weather on a continental to planetary scale for
whole seasons even \parencite{jensenDynamicAnalysisRecord2015}. A climatology
for the \gls{nh} based on the anomaly-index by
\textcite{schwierzPerspicaciousIndicatorsAtmospheric2004} is shown in
\cref{fig:clim_blocking}.

Where they occur, blockings can exert a considerable impact on societies and
ecosystems alike, a feature that numerous publications have drawn attention to
\parencite[e.g.,~][]{kautzAtmosphericBlockingWeather2021,
    whanInfluenceAtmosphericBlocking2016,pfahlQuantifyingRelevanceAtmospheric2012}.
The implied persistence can lead to sustained weather conditions of
different nature depending on season and location relative to the high pressure
center. Their eponymous blocking and (meridional) deflecting of air masses can
favour anomalously and extremely hot or cold conditions and stationary cyclones
on their flanks leading to anomalously high moisture transport and associated
precipitation \parencite[e.g.,~][]{brunnerConnectingAtmosphericBlocking2017,
    lenggenhagerAtmosphericBlocksModulate2019,
    mohrRelationshipAtmosphericBlocking2019,
    mohrMultidisciplinaryAnalysisExceptional2023}.

Apart from their effect on the advection of air masses, the high pressure
associated with blockings favours subsidence and, by extension, cloud-free
conditions and reduced vertical mixing in their centers. Together with reduced
wind speeds in the horizontal, this frequently leads to heat waves and droughts
in summer
\parencite{pfahlQuantifyingRelevanceAtmospheric2012,
    brunnerDependencePresentFuture2018}.
\textcite{herrmannAtmosphericBlocksIncrease2025} showed that disproportionately
many severe wildfires in the northern high latitudes were associated with
blocked episodes in summer. Reduced mixing may also entail episodes of poor air
quality, all of which put severe pressure on societal systems and human health
\parencite{webberDynamicalImpactRossby2017,popeImpactSynopticWeather2016}.
Prolonged episodes of decreases in wind energy supply and increased energy
demand caused by blockings in winter are considered a major risk for the energy
sector, especially with the transition to renewable sources
\parencite{oteroCharacterizingRenewableEnergy2022,
    vanderwielInfluenceWeatherRegimes2019}, potentially aggravating poor air
quality caused by blocking-related stable boundary layers through increased
fossil fuel use.

\subsection{Interactions} \label{sec:intro_block_interactions}
Consistent with the impacts described above, blockings interact with
processes and components of the Earth system on a wide range of spatial and
temporal scales.

The large-scale nature of blockings is evident from their interrelation with
planetary-scale teleconnections. Given their spatial proximity, a modulating
relation between blockings and the \gls{nao}
\parencite{grayElevenyearSolarCycle2016} and \gls{pna}
\parencite{croci-maspoliAtmosphericBlockingSpacetime2007} for \gls{nh}
blockings and the \gls{sam} \parencite{oliveiraNewClimatologySouthern2014} for
the \gls{sh} seem natural. More surprisingly perhaps, tropical teleconnections
such as the \gls{enso} \parencite{zhangENSOrelatedSouthernHemisphere2023,
    mckennaImpactsNinoDiversity2023,barriopedroRelationshipENSOStratospheric2014},
or the \gls{mjo} \parencite{hendersonInfluenceMaddenJulian2016} have also been
linked to blocking frequency and persistence, highlighting the role of
hemispheric disturbances. More specifically, large scale divergence caused by
tropical convection is associated with excitations of globally propagating
Rossby waves \parencite{sardeshmukhGenerationGlobalRotational1988},
which can in turn lead to blocking modification or formation.

The importance of each of the above mechanisms and teleconnections differ
regionally and seasonally due to a complex two-way interaction between
blockings and other components of the Earth system
\parencite{burkhardtPlanetarySynopticscaleInteractions2005,
    nakamuraRoleHighLowFrequency1997}. In particular, blocking dynamics are
influenced by the polar cryosphere through heat and moisture fluxes and
accompanying sea--ice anomalies on seasonal scales
\parencite{tyrlisUralBlockingDriving2019,woollingsArcticWarmingAtmospheric2014,
    drijfhoutSpontaneousAbruptClimate2013}. There, a strong two-way interaction
with polar vortex dynamics and the stratosphere more generally has been
established, mediated in particular by upward wave activity flux and associated
tropopause displacements
\parencite{woollingsAssociationsStratosphericVariability2010,
    colucciDiagnosticComparisonTropospheric2015,
    yessimbetPathwaysInfluenceNorthern2022}. For instance, from 27 objectively
identified \glspl{ssw} in the \gls{nh} between 1957 and 2001,
\textcite{martiusBlockingPrecursorsStratospheric2009} found that 25 were
preceded by blocking events in the North Atlantic sector.

Blockings have also been shown to exert a first-order influence on the subpolar
ocean \parencite{hakkinenAtmosphericBlockingAtlantic2011}. On the other hand,
\gls{sst} patterns and according gradients, at times, provide heat and moisture
fluxes and thereby the necessary baroclinic forcings for blockings to form,
controlling their occurrence frequency and intensity
\parencite{christSeaSkyUnderstanding2025, mathewsGulfStreamMoisture2024}.
\textcite{narinesinghUniformSSTWarming2024} however showed that a uniform
increase in \glspl{sst} is sufficient for an observed change in blocking
frequency in a climate model rather than a modulation of \gls{sst} gradients.
This highlights the uncertainty associated with the precise preconditioning of
\glspl{sst} for blockings with their according fluxes. The influence of
\glspl{sst} on blockings is often mediated through \glspl{wcb} and the
associated uncertainty in the interaction is, at least in part, due to
knowledge gaps in the understanding of these processes. The nature of the
interaction between blockings and \glspl{wcb} will be elaborated
on in the following sections. Over land, blocking dynamics are also shaped by
surface level diabatic sensible and latent heat fluxes
\parencite{tillyCalculatedHeightTendencies2008} and their climatological
occurrence is modulated by land--sea contrasts and orography
through planetary waves \parencite{heImpactLandSea2014} initiated via
surface fluxes, friction and mountain torque
\parencite{kallenBifurcationPropertiesQuasigeostrophic1982,
    metzWintertimeBlockingMountain1985}.

\subsection{Processes and theories of atmospheric blocking}
\label{sec:intro_blocking_theories}
The severe impacts and significant interactions of blockings with other
components of the Earth system make it all the more important to understand
and predict them. They have been and still are, however, a major challenge for
weather and climate models alike, a topic that will be picked up in
\cref{sec:intro_predictability_blockings}. Given all that has been stated above
about the complex phenomenon of atmospheric blockings from phenomenological
perspectives, it is not surprising that
there is not a single mathematical theory explaining all of its facets. That
said, a number of theories have been proposed that explain complementing
aspects of the phenomenon, based on differing assumptions and perspectives.

\subsubsection{Barotropic theories} \label{sec:intro_block_barotropic}
More traditional theories on blockings focus on barotropic large scale
dynamics---a stance that is justified by the fact that the vertical structure
of atmospheric blockings is dominantly equivalent-barotropic, with little
vertical variation in potential temperature and wind
\parencite{nabizadeh3DStructureNorthern2021}. The earliest synoptic work took a
primarily diagnostic stance with some likening the phenomenon
to hydraulic jumps in other jet-like flows
\parencite{rexBlockingActionMiddle1950} inspired by the observationally guided
understanding of blockings as a breakdown of the usual zonal flow
\parencite{berggrenAerologicalStudyZonal1949}. These merely descriptive works
set the stage for more quantitative theories with some degree of
explanatory power.

In a barotropic flow with external (usually topographic and radiative) forcing
blocking can appear as a stationary (Rossby) wave phenomenon
\parencite{rossbyRelationVariationsIntensity1939}. This is because it can be
shown that for certain Rossby wave constellations, the waves' group velocity
exactly opposes the advection with the mean flow. Assuming linearized
barotropic dynamics around some climatological flow, this perspective can
explain regions of climatalogical blocking occurrence, but lacks mechanisms
necessary to understand onset and persistence in a way comparable with
observations \parencite{hoskinsSteadyLinearResponse1981}.

This was also pointed out by \textcite{charneyMultipleFlowEquilibria1979}, who
proposed a model in which non-linearities were essential for blocking
occurrence. Similar to the notion of stationary waves, their
model---termed \gls{cdv} model in the following---admits
multiple \emph{unstable fixed points}, each associated with a different
\emph{flow regime}
and opened the understanding of blocking as a (quasi-) stationary regime
governing the atmospheric system's dynamics in its vicinity in state space.
\Cref{fig:CdV_Schematic} illustrates the duality between physical and state
space and the associated perspectives on blocking for the \gls{cdv} model. The
idea, that the mid-latitude atmosphere transitions between such regimes
chaotically due to stochastic forcing from synoptic variability was highly
influential and has been extensively applied to all types of models
\parencite{legrasPersistentAnomaliesBlocking1985,
    zidikheriMultipleEquilibriaAtmospheric2007,
    kondrashovEmpiricalModeReduction2006} as well as real world observations
\parencite{moClusterAnalysisMultiple1988, vautardMultipleWeatherRegimes1990,
    farandaSwitchingZonalBlocked2016}. It offers a mechanism for
regime persistence and sudden transitions, but its applicability is questioned
by its sensitivity to parameter choices
\parencite{crommelinMechanismAtmosphericRegime2004} and whether the real
atmosphere possesses regimes in the dynamical sense is debated
\parencite{stephensonExistenceMultipleClimate2004}.

The quasi-stationary wave perspective was advanced by
\textcite{tungTheoryStationaryLong1979} who conceived blockings as waves
resonant with topography and land-sea contrast, but kept in place by specific
conditions of the background flow. Non-linearities are assumed in this model
and baroclinic extensions are discussed, though not necessary. Time-dependent
amplification explains realistic onset and persistence mechanisms
lacking in the basic Rossby wave concept above, but it does not grasp a
blocking as a distinct, material object with properties governed by the air
that consitutes it and its history.

Such an understanding is delivered by a separate set of theories that argue
that due to their nearly equivalent-barotropic structure, blockings resemble
individual vortices interacting with other vortices in a background flow.
\textcite{mcwilliamsApplicationEquivalentModons1980} showed that the
(non-linear) equivalent-barotorpic vorticity equation admits vortex pairs
called \enquote{modons} as solutions similar in structure and behaviour to
blockings. Similar results can be obtained when considering point vortices as
abstractions of blockings, which provides a particularly simple
understanding of blockings and their dynamics
\parencite{mullerApplicationsPointVortex2015, hirtStatisticsDynamicsBlockings2018}. In a related view,
\textcite{hainesEddyForcedCoherentStructures1987} interpret synoptic eddies as
providing a dipole \gls{pv} forcing that supports the coherent modon structure
against dissipation. Modon and point vortex theory can be seen as
paradigm-level illustrations, especially considering that the real background
flow is highly variable.

Still, the fact that transient eddies can systematically transfer vorticity to
foster large scale flow configurations has been well established for a long
time \parencite[e.g.,~][]{kuoVorticityTransferRelated1951} and atmospheric
blockings do not seem to form an exception (their spatio--temporal scales
characterizing them as the latter rather than the former). Even in barotropic
flows eddy--mean flow \parencite{shuttsPropagationEddiesDiffluent1983,
    legrasPersistentAnomaliesBlocking1985}, eddy--eddy
\parencite{yamazakiVortexVortexInteractions2013,
    luoNonlinearMultiscaleInteraction2014, suittersTransientAnticyclonicEddies2023}
or sometimes both types of interactions
\parencite{cashDynamicalProcessesBlock2000} can lead to the kinds of persistent
anomalies associated with blocking.

A closely related non-linear mechanism for blocking onset and maintenance is
Rossby wave breaking. It occurs when amplified planetary waves undergo
irreversible overturning (visible, for example, in \gls{pv} contours on
isentropic surfaces), leading to the meridional displacement of \gls{pv}
typical for blockings \parencite{mcintyreBreakingPlanetaryWaves1983,
    thorncroftTwoParadigmsBaroclinicwave1993}. Anticyclonic wave breaking in
particular has been linked to the formation of persistent high-pressure
anomalies resembling blocking events
\parencite{pellyNewPerspectiveBlocking2003,
    masatoWavebreakingCharacteristicsMidlatitude2012}. Unlike linear
stationary-wave theory, this framework emphasizes non-linear \gls{pv}
rearrangement in physical space and naturally accounts for jet deformation and
cutoff formation. At the same time, Rossby wave breaking is often preceded by
or interacts with baroclinic wave amplification
\parencite{tamarin-brodskyRelationRossbyWavebreaking2024}, indicating that,
while the breaking itself can be described in largely barotropic \gls{pv}
terms, its excitation frequently originates in baroclinic dynamics.

The Rossby wave breaking framework is very similar to theories based on
\glspl{rwp}  \parencite{wirthRossbyWavePackets2018} or \gls{lwa} fluxes
\parencite{huangLocalFiniteamplitudeWave2016,
    nakamuraAtmosphericBlockingTraffic2018}. Here, conservation laws are
established for the amount of undulation of the jet stream and a severe
undulation is then associated with a blocking event. The theory connects
to more traditional wave-dynamical perspectives, while emphasizing locality.
Though principally barotropic, baroclinic and diabatic processes appear as
source terms in this theory and are, thus, vital elements in the real life
explanation of observed events \parencite{neal2021PacificNorthwest2022}.

The above shows that what genuinely barotropic theories lack is
a mechanism for the strength of the disturbances necessary for blocking
formation and maintenance of the scale comparable with observations. Consistent
with this, rapid onsets of blockings are rarely observed in such models and
even if upstream barotropic eddies support this process, cyclones in the real
atmosphere are scarcely barotropic
\parencite{colucciExplosiveCyclogenesisLargescale1985,
    frederiksenOnsetBlockingCyclogenesis1984, vautardMultipleWeatherRegimes1990}.

\subsubsection{Baroclinic theories} \label{sec:intro_block_baroclinic}
Atmospheric blockings appear not in a single layer flow, but in a stratified,
vertically sheared atmosphere in which baroclinic instability continuously
generates synoptic-scale eddies shaping the general circulation. Classical
linear instability theory \parencite[e.g.,~][]{charneyDynamicsLongWaves1947,
    eadyLongWavesCyclone1949} and energy cycle considerations
\parencite{lorenzAvailablePotentialEnergy1955} demonstrate that in such a
setting, generic instabilites convert \gls{ape} into \gls{eke}
on synoptic scales. Baroclinic theories tackle the central
question how these synoptic eddies initiate, sustain and destabilize
the planetary-scale flow to
produce persistent large-scale anomalies \parencite{shuttsCaseStudyEddy1986}
and they provide models with more
realistic assumptions than idealized approaches based on modons or
point--vortex dynamics.

The feedbacks between eddies of any scale and the large scale flow are closely
tied to the nature of turbulent cascades in geophysical flows. In fully
\emph{three-dimensional turbulence}, energy cascades downscale toward
dissipation
\parencite{popeTurbulentFlows2000}. This behaviour occurs at small scales in the
atmosphere, where the combined effects of rotation and stratification are
insignificant and in
mesoscale flows characterized by strong convection
\parencite{selzEstimationVariabilityMesoscale2019,hirtScaleInteractionsMeso2023}.
By contrast, in \emph{two-dimensional turbulence} the existence of a second
conserved
quantity (the enstrophy in addition to the kinetic energy) induces (dual)
energy and enstrophy cascades in opposing directions. In scales larger than
the excitation scale, there is an inverse energy cascade with energy transfer
from small to large scales, while in scales smaller than the excitation scale,
there is a direct enstrophy cascade with transfer from large to
small scales \parencite{kraichnanInertialRangesTwodimensional1967,
    boffettaTwoDimensionalTurbulence2012}. Both of the above types are depicted
schematically in \cref{fig:energy_spectrum}~(a) and (b).

\emph{\Gls{qg} turbulence} appropriate for a strongly stratified rapidly
rotating atmosphere shares essential features with
two-dimensional turbulence. The conservation of potential enstrophy alongside
energy in the \gls{qg} framework gives rise to an analogous dual cascade
structure \parencite{charneyGeostrophicTurbulence1971}: above the energy
injection scale energy is transferred upscale, while potential enstrophy
cascades downscale below it. Unlike pure two-dimensional flow, however,
\gls{qg} turbulence is not forced externally at a single wavenumber but is
instead driven internally by baroclinic instability, which extracts \gls{ape}
from the mean meridional temperature gradient and converts it
into \gls{eke} near the Rossby deformation radius ($\sim$ 1000 km)
\parencite{frederiksenUnifiedThreedimensionalInstability1982,
    augierNewFormulationSpectral2013}. This injection
scale therefore acts as the effective excitation wavenumber for the
atmospheric cascade. The upscale energy transfer above the injection
scale does not, however, extend indefinitely: it is arrested roughly at the
Rhines scale ($\sim$ 3000 km), where, either through turbulence--Rossby
wave interaction \parencite{rhinesWavesTurbulenceBetaplane1975}
or large-scale friction effects \parencite{sukorianskyArrestInverseEnergy2007},
energy preferentially accumulates in zonal and quasi-stationary planetary
structures rather than continuing to cascade. Blocking is a
synoptic--to--planetary-scale phenomenon residing in the upscale-transfer range,
near the scale at which this transfer is naturally arrested. While there is
still debate about the cascades in the synoptic and mesoscales, the
upward energy cascade in this region is firmly established and robustly supported
by observations \parencite{boerLargescaleTwodimensionalTurbulence1983,
    burgessTropospheretoStratosphereTransitionKinetic2013}. The
process is illustrated in \cref{fig:energy_spectrum}~(c). The kinetic energy
spectra will be discussed in more detail in \cref{sec:intro_predictability_chaos}.

The process of upscale energy transfer can be studied in dedicated models.
For instance, in a two-layer
\gls{qg} model proposed by
\textcite{salmonTwolayerQuasigeostrophicTurbulence1978}, \gls{ape} resulting
from differential heating cascades downscale towards the
Rossby deformation scale, where it is converted by baroclinic instability to
kinetic energy in barotropic modes, which then cascades
upward again. The barotropization is important: it implies that energy
injected by an inherently baroclinic process is projected
onto vertically coherent, quasi-two-dimensional structures at larger scales,
where the dual-cascade arguments outlined above become applicable.

A complementary perspective is offered by
\textcite{herbertRestrictedEquilibriumEnergy2014}, who decompose
rotating-stratified Boussinesq dynamics into inertia-gravity wave modes and
slow (vortical/\gls{pv}-carrying) modes. They show that the direction of the
energy cascade depends on which subset of state space is dynamically active:
when the statistics are restricted to the slow manifold, an inverse energy
transfer becomes admissible (a statistical mechanics analogue of
condensation), whereas a direct cascade develops in the unrestricted case.
The restricted case most naturally applies to (geostrophically) balanced,
large-scale dynamics \parencite{vautardInvariantManifoldsQuasigeostrophy1986},
precisely the regime in which mature blocks reside. The condensation framing
is particularly suggestive in light of the Rhines arrest: blocks may be
understood as a quasi-equilibrium accumulation of upscale-transferred \gls{eke}
at the scale where further upscale transfer is dynamically suppressed,
lending the structure both its large scale and its persistence.

% Thus, although blocking develops in a vertically sheared environment, its
% mature phase often exhibits a pronounced equivalent-barotropic character,
% rendering the dynamics locally more two-dimensional. This argument relies in
% part on the observation of strong vertical coherence in blockings.

The upscale energy transfer described above in an abstract way
(in spectral rather then physical space) encapsulates the existence of feedback
mechanisms that are discussed, e.g. as eddy-eddy interactions or selective
absorption mechanism in the context of blockings and
eddy straining mechanisms in
turbulence theories \parencite{ditlevsenTurbulenceShellModels2010}. In this
view, baroclinic theories of blocking thus interpret
persistence not as a steady equilibrium, but as the large-scale manifestation
of sustained, yet principally intermittent, eddy fluxes and inverse energy
transfer within a stratified turbulent flow
\parencite{hansenComparisonSpectralEnergy1984,
    jensenUsingEnstrophyAdvection2014,
    tanakaNumericalSimulationAmplification1991,
    fournierAtmosphericEnergeticsWavelet2003}.

Baroclinic theories emphasizing the role of the vertical structure of the
flow supporting blocking formation have proposed a variety of abstracted
models over the years to investigate these processes mechanically
\parencite[e.g.~][]{vautardSourceMidlatitudeLowfrequency1988,
    tanakaNumericalSimulationAmplification1991,
    cessiOrographicInstabilityNonsymmetric1985,
    marshallDynamicalUnderstandingPlanetaryScale1993} and a lot of the
barotropic
model introduced above have been extended into multi-layer generalizations.
Some models are simple enough to study the phenomenon from a dynamical systems
perspective. For example, \textcite{charneyFormdragInstabilityMultiple1980}
extend the aforementioned barotropic \gls{cdv} model,
highlighting that the baroclinic
instability attains a first-order importance in bringing about the equilibrium
states associated with blocking, whereas orography becomes less important
compared to the barotropic case. The notion that baroclinic instability
contributes a mechanism of strong growth instrumental for the rapid
onset and decay observed in real blockings is supported by such and more modern
stability analyses of the same model
\parencite{schubertCovariantLyapunovVectors2015,
    schubertDynamicalAnalysisBlocking2016}.

Consistent with the above, studies have frequently highlighted the
importance of upstream disturbances (including cyclones) and wave-interactions
for blocking onset and decay. In a simple two-layer model,
\textcite{luoPlanetaryscaleBaroclinicEnvelope2000} observed that an upstream
baroclinic eddy can bring a high-wavenumber Rossby wave into phaselock. In this
view, baroclinicity is important to bring about the necessary amplification of
a wave disturbance to reach a blocking configuration, and eventually
dissipate it again. Such conceptualisation is backed by observational evidence
of the equivalent barotropic blocking region being \enquote{enveloped} by
baroclinically active zones up- and downstream
\parencite{frederiksenOnsetBlockingCyclogenesis1984,
    nakamuraObservedChangesBaroclinic1990, mullerUsingConceptDynamic2019},
though baroclinic energy conversions may even take place within the
blocking itself \parencite{martineauBaroclinicBlocking2022}.
Similar insights have been obtained by
budget and flux analyses of observations and (early) operational forecast
models by \textcite{mullenLocalBalancesVorticity1986,
    mullenTransientEddyForcing1987}. The precise location of upstream cyclonic
disturbances plays a critical role in determining the blocking development and
morphology. This is especially true for cases involving particularly strong
(\enquote{explosive}) cyclogenesis
\parencite{colucciExplosiveCyclogenesisLargescale1985}.

\subsubsection{Diabatic processes} \label{sec:intro_block_diabatic}
Despite their success in explaining eddy amplification, upscale transfer and
the maintenance of large-scale anomalies, dry barotropic and baroclinic
theories neglect diabatic \gls{pv} modification by latent heat release,
radiation and surface fluxes. Such effects were historically overlooked,
partly because small-scale processes are hard to represent in models and
observations, and partly because blockings were conceived as large-scale
phenomena whose characteristic subsidence suppresses ascent within their
cores. Observational and modelling studies nonetheless show that
diabatic---and especially moist---processes substantially influence
virtually all aspects of blocking, with important implications for its
understanding and prediction.

Moist diabatic processes are increasingly recognized as
first-order. That latent heat release in clouds is not merely a secondary
modulation but can be decisive for block formation and maintenance was
perhaps first emphasized by \textcite{croci-maspoliKeyDynamicalFeatures2009,
    archambaultRelationshipsLargescaleRegime2010},
and it leaves an imprint on the climatology of oceanic blocks, where
pronounced ascent appears on the western flank against subsidence in the
centre and east \parencite{nabizadeh3DStructureNorthern2021}. Lagrangian
trajectory analyses show that a substantial fraction of air arriving in
blocking anticyclones experienced strong latent heating beforehand,
consistent with cross-isentropic transport of low-\gls{pv} air in moist
slant-wise ascending airstreams as \glspl{wcb} \parencite{pfahlImportanceLatentHeat2015,
    steinfeldRoleLatentHeating2019}---though some of this air may ascend
convectively rather than within such airstreams
\parencite{zschenderleinLagrangianAnalysisUppertropospheric2020}. Beyond this
material transport, the upper-level divergent outflow of the ascending air
also reinforces the negative \gls{pv} anomaly, an effect analyzed both
theoretically and observationally
\parencite{saffinCirculationConservationOutflow2021,
    methvenPotentialVorticityWarm2015}. The reinforcement of the negative
\gls{pv} anomaly on the western flank of the block also contributes to its
stationarity against eastward propagation. The prototypical enforcement of
a blocking by a \gls{wcb} is illustrated in \cref{fig:WCBBlock}.
All these effects appear more
important
during onset and for larger, more intense blocks, suggesting that their
neglect in dry theories underlies the poorly represented rapid onset and
persistence noted earlier.

These influences can be diagnosed elegantly in quantitative \gls{pv}
frameworks, whose conservation properties allow them to be tracked along air
masses. \textcite{neal2021PacificNorthwest2022} applied \gls{lwa} theory to a
particularly strong episode and identified an upstream cyclone as the
critical \gls{lwa} source, while \textcite{liuBlockingDiversityCauses2025}
found that diabatic effects can act in opposing directions depending on block
morphology. Combining Eulerian and Lagrangian diagnostics with the
quasi-Lagrangian \gls{pv} framework of
\textcite{teublerDynamicsRossbyWave2016},
\textcite{hauserHolisticUnderstandingBlocked2023,
    hauserLifeCycleDynamics2024} showed for Greenland blocks that diabatic
processes are especially important at onset and maintenance, concurrently
with quasi-barotropic \gls{pv} advection. Because such influences act
materially through conservation laws, they also permit more direct
cause-and-effect attribution than diagnostic correlation: in sensitivity
experiments with the \gls{ecmwf}'s operational \gls{ifs},
\textcite{steinfeldSensitivityAtmosphericBlocking2020} found that switching
off latent heating left some blocks much weaker and prevented others from
forming at all, and a dry \gls{gcm} forced by a realistic background flow
produced roughly a third fewer blocks with severely altered morphology
\parencite{deshmukhHowDoesDry2025}. Together these results make latent
heating a critical determinant of blocking as observed.

At the climatological scale, moist forcing is markedly non-uniform.
\textcite{yamamotoOceanicMoistureSources2021} identify key oceanic moisture
sources feeding the latent-heating pathways of Euro--Atlantic blocks, and
\textcite{mathewsGulfStreamMoisture2024} show that suppressing Gulf Stream
latent heat fluxes alone substantially reduces blocking frequency, lifetime,
intensity and extent across the \gls{nh}, because turbulent fluxes there
generate boundary-layer low-\gls{pv} air that is ventilated upward into the
block's upper-level negative-\gls{pv} anomaly
\parencite{mathewsOceanicMaintenanceAtmospheric2024}. This pathway depends on
\gls{sst} magnitude \parencite{narinesinghUniformSSTWarming2024} and gradients
\parencite{christSeaSkyUnderstanding2025} and on model resolution, possibly
via underresolved extreme precipitation \parencite{strommenLinkGulfStream2025}.
Moist processes also reshape the mean flow and baroclinicity
\parencite{auestadLatentHeatingFeedback2025, schemmEliminatingDecadesOld2023},
so their climatological influence extends beyond these direct mechanisms.

As mentioned above, the moist ascent that feeds blocks is predominantly
organized in \glspl{wcb} originating in upstream surface
cyclones---tying the diabatic
picture back to the upstream baroclinic disturbances already emphasized
by theories described in the last section.
Inherently three-dimensional and highly efficient at scale interaction,
\glspl{wcb} stand in marked contrast to the quasi-two-dimensional,
equivalent-barotropic core of a block, and it is this interaction that the
following section takes up.

Finally, several diabatic processes other than latent heat release modify a block's
\gls{pv}. Radiative cooling of its air masses contributes to the eventual
diffusion of the negative \gls{pv} anomaly, while cloud-radiative effects
upstream may aid maintenance \parencite{lubisCloudRadiativeEffects2025},
though both are generally weaker than the moist effects discussed below. At
the lower boundary, surface sensible and latent heat fluxes can precondition
and locally reinforce blocking highs: using height-tendency diagnostics,
\textcite{tillyCalculatedHeightTendencies2008} show that such fluxes
contribute materially to height rises, and thus to intensification,
particularly during early development.

\section{\Glspl{wcb}}\label{sec:intro_wcb}

\Glspl{wcb} are coherent, ascending airstreams that transport warm, moist air
from the warm sector of an extratropical cyclone in the lower troposphere
to the upper troposphere and lower stratosphere
\parencite{schwenkRoleAscentTimescales2024}. Its principle existence and
effects were present already in the classical theory of extratropical cyclones
at the polar front by \textcite{BjerknesSolberg1922}, but conceptualisation and
dedicated investigation took place later within the framework of the
three-dimensional cyclone conveyor-belt model
\parencite[e.g.,~][]{browningStructureRainbandsMidlatitude1973},
which conceives them as essential components of the cyclone along with
\glspl{ccb}, dry intrusions and (later) sting jets.

They were objects of interest originally due to their associated rain bands,
but have since also been recognized as important dynamical drivers of synoptic
scale baroclinic dynamics
\parencite{madonnaWarmConveyorBelts2014a, binderRoleWarmConveyor2016} and, in
effect, the large-scale mid-latitude flow
\parencite{vishnupriyaInteractionWarmConveyor2025,
    blanchardMidlevelConvectionWarm2021}. Due to their considerable forcing and
moisture content, \glspl{wcb} commonly account for precipitation extremes in
the extratropics on daily timescales \parencite{pfahlWarmConveyorBelts2014}.
They are a regular part of extratropical cyclones in any time of year with a
maximum occurrence in the cold season in either hemisphere. Their baroclinic
nature makes them most prominent in the storm tracks, most notably in the
North Atlantic and Pacific sectors \parencite{madonnaWarmConveyorBelts2014a}.
Annual climatologies for the \gls{nh} for inflow, ascent and outflow phases
are provided in \cref{fig:WCB_clim}.

\subsection{Definition} \label{sec:intro_wcb_def}
As mentioned above, \glspl{wcb} were traditionally identified subjectively
by their associated stratiform cloud bands and vicinity to cyclones, but
research has developed more objective methods applicable to model or
reanalysis data. Nowadays, \glspl{wcb} are typically identified in a Lagrangian
manner as traced air parcels that ascend rapidly while undergoing substantial
latent heating. A common criterion to select \glspl{wcb} apart from vicinity to
a cyclone is to require an ascent of at least 600 hPa within 2 days
\parencite[e.g.,~][]{madonnaWarmConveyorBelts2014a,
    sprengerGlobalClimatologiesEulerian2017}, which goes back to seminal
work by \textcite{wernliLagrangianbasedAnalysisExtratropical1997} and
\textcite{wernliLagrangianbasedAnalysisExtratropical1997a}, though other
thresholds and variables have been used in the past, too
\parencite{stohl1yearLagrangianClimatology2001,
    eckhardt15yearClimatologyWarm2004}. Identifying \glspl{wcb} from
trajectories
requires data in high spatio-temporal resolution and is computationally
demanding which is
why \textcite{quintingEuLerianIdentificationAscending2022a} have recently
developed a machine-learning based approach to identify \glspl{wcb} in a
Eulerian framework. Given relevant meteorological fields, their method can
identify \glspl{wcb} in their inflow, ascent and outflow phases with good
accuracy, especially in regions of high climatological \gls{wcb} occurrence
when applied various datasets
\parencite{quintingEuLerianIdentificationAscending2022}. The climatology shown
in \cref{fig:WCB_clim} is according to the above definition.

While the \gls{wcb} concept suggests a geometrically coherent bundle of
trajectories, this coherence is not guaranteed by threshold-based selection of
individual air parcels. Moreover, such selections do not directly yield
statements about the number, size, location, or lifetime of distinct
\glspl{wcb} in a given situation and the choice of thresholds is always
somewhat arbitrary. This motivates a complementary viewpoint that conceives of
\glspl{wcb} as clusters of trajectories, i.e.~ensembles of air-parcel paths
that remain mutually close judging by some metric over a finite time window.

More generally, trajectory clustering has a long history in geoscience---in
oceanography in particular \parencite{vansebilleLagrangianOceanAnalysis2018},
but also in atmospheric sciences, like in air-quality and (moisture) transport
studies, where large sets of trajectories are grouped into a small number of
representative transport pathways
\parencite{stohlComputationAccuracyApplications1998,
    capeUseTrajectoryCluster2000}. In these applications, clustering is often
performed on trajectory positions at selected times or along a limited portion
of the path, which already acknowledges that the geometry of trajectories can
matter beyond instantaneous location, albeit often with relatively simple
similarity measures \parencite{stohlComputationAccuracyApplications1998}.

At the same time, trajectory clustering is a broad and evolving methodological
field in its own right \parencite{yuanReviewMovingObject2017,
    graserMobilityDLReviewDeep2025}, and atmospheric applications increasingly
adopt multivariate and time-windowed similarity notions that better reflect
physically meaningful airstream coherence. For instance,
\textcite{hartDetectionCoherentAirstreams2015} agglomeratively cluster
trajectories using distances in a standardized multivariate trajectory space
over finite time windows, and post-hoc interpretation of the resulting
clusters recovers \glspl{wcb} and \glspl{ccb}; related
methodology has also been applied in the context of heat waves
\parencite{spensbergerDynamicsConcurrentSequential2020}. Though heuristically
relatable, the approach falls short of presenting a reasonable justification
for why different standardized variables should be weighted equally and by
which standardization. It also does not directly yield a measure of cluster
quality, instead relying on the within-group versus between-group standard
deviations across the clusters as by
\textcite{wernliLagrangianbasedAnalysisExtratropical1997}.

Conversely, in settings where the expected geometry is strongly constrained,
trajectory similarity can be tailored to the phenomenon, e.g.~via shape-based
metrics such as Fr\'echet distances (which measures distances under
reparametrization of time) and rotational and translational invariances in the
context of tropical cyclones \parencite{kremerClusterAnalysisTailored2020} or
by clustering trajectory segments to identify updrafts in hail storms
\parencite{adams-selinThreedimensionalHailTrajectory2023}. Such invariances are
not directly applicable to \glspl{wcb}, but they highlight that trajectories
may be clustered either by similarity in shape or by time-local coherence.

The latter argument invites a formalization of the visual notion of
\enquote{trajectories staying together} by concepts of Lagrangian coherence
such as \emph{coherent sets} or \emph{Lagrangian coherent structures}, which
aim to identify time-evolving regions that resist dispersion and mixing under
small perturbations \parencite{hallerCoherentLagrangianVortices2013,
    hallerLagrangianCoherentStructures2015}. In the coherent-set framework,
robustness can be quantified via diffusion-based operators estimated from
trajectory data, whose spectral properties reveal coherent partitions of the
input \parencite{banischUnderstandingGeometryTransport2017,
    froylandAnalyticFrameworkIdentifying2013,
    froylandDynamicIsoperimetryGeometry2015}. A variety of related approaches
to
computationally partition trajectory data into coherent sets or transport
barriers has been developed
\parencite{froylandRoughandreadyClusterbasedApproach2015,
    allshouseLagrangianBasedMethods2015,
    hadjighasemSpectralclusteringApproachLagrangian2016,
    schlueter-kuckCoherentStructureColouring2017,
    padberg-gehleNetworkbasedStudyLagrangian2017,
    koltaiLargeDeviationsSemidistances2018,mowlaviDetectingLagrangianCoherent2022}.
This perspective is also highly adaptable, since it does not assume specific
geometries and considers full trajectories rather than selected slices.

Arguably, spatio-temporal coherence is more appropriate for the identification
of \glspl{wcb} than shape-based similarity, since trajectories of similar shape
may belong to distinct \glspl{wcb}. The operator theoretic framework also
provides a natural way to assess coherence through the robustness against small
random perturbations. Together, these developments suggest that complementing
threshold-based \gls{wcb} identification with coherence-based
trajectory partitioning can help address questions of pathway commonality and
of the spatio-temporal extent of distinct airstreams---a task we pursue in
\cref{ch:coherence}.

\subsection{Dynamics} \label{sec:intro_wcb_dynamics}
Among diabatic influences on the mid-latitude large-scale flow, \glspl{wcb} are
arguably the most relevant mediators: by transporting boundary-layer moisture
into the upper troposphere and releasing latent heat during ascent, they
reshape the \gls{pv} distribution along the waveguide and thereby couple
small-scale disturbances with synoptic-scale cyclone development to the
planetary-scale circulation.

During ascent, latent heat release strongly modifies the thermodynamic and
dynamical structure of the flow, leading to changes in \gls{pv} and
the amplification and redistribution of upper-level wave features.
In particular, transport of air masses upward and poleward entails deposition
of negatively anomalous \gls{pv} near the tropopause, which,
along with divergent upper-level outflow can reinforce downstream ridges
and thereby influence the evolution of large-scale flow patterns. The material
change of \gls{pv} along a particular trajectory depends on the
location of maximum heating, but integrates roughly to zero
\parencite{methvenPotentialVorticityWarm2015}. The effect of diabatic processes
on the \gls{pv} structure of cyclones
\parencite{chagnonDiabaticProcessesModifying2013} and the jet stream
\parencite{harveyDiabaticGenerationNegative2020} is well established
and supports the hypothesis that \glspl{wcb} are of first-order importance
for the large-scale
flow \parencite{wernliImportanceDiabaticProcesses2024}. There exist
considerable regional and case-to-case disparities in the interaction between
\glspl{wcb} and the large-scale flow
\parencite{vishnupriyaInteractionWarmConveyor2025}, complicating
the establishment of a general theory for the phenomenon. For instance,
the \glspl{wcb} outflow often splits into multiple branches, with an
anticyclonically turning branch associated with the strongest upper-level
\gls{pv} anomalies and according downstream ridge amplification
\parencite{heitmannWarmConveyorBelt2024}.

Because they link boundary-layer moisture reservoirs with the
upper-tropospheric waveguide, \glspl{wcb} play a central role in mediating
interactions between synoptic-scale baroclinic development and the
planetary-scale circulation. Accordingly, the intensification phase of a
surface cyclone often coincides with the strongest ascent phase
of a \gls{wcb} \parencite{heitmannWarmConveyorBelt2024} and the intensification
of a cyclone determines the amount of air masses involved in such a conveyor
belt \parencite{binderRoleWarmConveyor2016}. This is due to an implied positive
feedback loop between the cyclogenesis and the latent heat release, given a
sufficient moisture supply. Consistent with the near-zero net \gls{pv} change
noted above, the amount of latent heating governs ascent rate and outflow
level, rather than material changes in the physical properties of the air masses.

This implies that \glspl{wcb} and their large-scale effect are highly sensitive
to the (small-scale) variables governing ascent.
\textcite{schaflerImpactInflowMoisture2015} observed that the correct
measurement or assimilation of moisture upstream of the WCB considerably
influences the forecasted effect of a WCB event especially with respect to
tropopause and jet stream dynamics. The sensitivity to small-scale
perturbations is underscored by evidence for embedded mid-level
convection within \glspl{wcb} which alters the \gls{pv} structure locally
modifying the large scale flow downstream
\parencite{oertelConvectiveActivityExtratropical2019,
    oertelPotentialVorticityStructure2020}. Notably,
\textcite{blanchardMidlevelConvectionWarm2021} point to intensifying effects on
the jet stream, though the effects of embedded convection in \glspl{wcb} seem
to play a secondary role climatologically due to the rarity of its occurrence
\parencite{oertelInteractionMicrophysicsDynamics2023}.

The strong case--to--case variability and the sensitivity to small-scale
perturbations are testament to the inherent convective and baroclinic
instabilities of \glspl{wcb}. Paired with their central role in shaping
the large-scale flow, they have been notoriously associated with predictability
challenges---a topic that will be dealt with in detail in
\cref{sec:intro_wcb_uncertainty}.

\section{Predictability, uncertainty and error growth}
\label{sec:intro_predictability}
% (Optional: table taxonomy of uncertainty sources.)

Predicting the weather and climate of the future is certainly one of the core
goals of atmospheric sciences as a whole, so it comes as no surprise that the
study of predictability is wide and deep
\parencite{krishnamurthyPredictabilityWeatherClimate2019}.
The atmosphere is a chaotic system with rich dynamics on a vast range of
scales generally rendering definitive forecasts impossible beyond a certain
time horizon, often referred to as the \emph{predictability limit} or
\emph{forecast horizon}. From a forecasting perspective, errors and, thus,
predictability limits may be decomposed into two main sources:
uncertainties in the \emph{initial conditions} due to imperfect observations
and data assimilation on the one hand and \emph{model uncertainty} due to
under- or unresolved processes and model biases on the other hand.
The differentiation is somewhat arbitrary since model errors---through data
assimilation---also influence the initial conditions, which in turn modify the
error-growth characteristics of the system. Nevertheless it provides a
paradigm for diagnosing error sources. Crucially, both aspects of
predictability are dependent on the flow state, region, variable and lead time.

From a practical point of view, predictability is usually studied
by means of ensemble forecasting, where the evolution of the spread between
multiple realizations of a forecast serves as a proxy for the forecast
uncertainty. Representing deficient knowledge about the system by providing
probabilistic in addition to deterministic forecasts has successfully extended
the forecast skill horizon \parencite{buizzaForecastSkillHorizon2015} and has
proven instrumental in guiding decision-making as well as model improvement.

Mathematically, one can think of ensemble forecasting as a Monte Carlo
approximation of the temporal evolution of the probability distribution in the
system's state space. A subtle distinction has to be made between the growth of
the ensemble spread (the width of the probability distribution) and the growth
of the forecast error (the distance between the ensemble mean and the truth),
since the two only coincide in \emph{reliable} ensembles
\parencite{leutbecherEnsembleForecasting2008}. This might not always be the
case and continues to be a challenge, but reliability is routinely assessed in
operational models and ensembles are consequently calibrated
\parencite{rodwellReliabilityEnsembleData2016}.
Importantly, both aspects of uncertainty discussed here crucially impact the
reliability of an ensemble forecast and they are distinctly represented: the
initial spread is either derived from
assimilation uncertainty (like in an operational setting) or designed in some
dedicated way with a specific purpose in mind
\parencite[ch.~6]{kalnayEarthSystemModeling2024}. Model uncertainty is often
represented by stochastic parametrizations.
\parencite{palmerStochasticWeatherClimate2019,
    christensenParametrizationWeatherClimate2022}.

\subsection{Chaos and initial conditions}
\label{sec:intro_predictability_chaos}

One can generally distinguish between \emph{intrinsic} and \emph{practical}
predictability \parencite{sunIntrinsicPracticalLimits2016,
    selzTransitionPracticalIntrinsic2022}. The former is a property of the system
itself and describes the time it takes for infinitesimally small perturbations
to grow to a size comparable to the natural variability of the system. The
\enquote{system} here generally refers to a
\gls{nwp} model, but may also be thought of as the
real atmosphere---the two coinciding under the so-called
\enquote{perfect model assumption}. The practical predictability limit, on
the other hand, assumes realistic initial condition uncertainty and is
therefore always shorter than the intrinsic predictability.

With the current generation of \gls{nwp} models and assimilation and
observation systems, the gap between the two is estimated at around 3.5 to 5
days for the mid-latitudes \parencite{selzTransitionPracticalIntrinsic2022,
    zhangWhatPredictabilityLimit2019}. An even greater gap is observed in the
tropics, where \textcite{judtAtmosphericPredictabilityTropics2020} found
evidence for a longer intrinsic predictability horizon compared to the
mid-latitudes. These findings suggest that there is still ample room for
improving forecast skill by reducing initial condition uncertainty. Moreover,
differences in predictability between locations are indicative of
non-homogeneous impacts of initial perturbations and differences in
error-growth characteristics.

\subsubsection{Intrinsic predictability}

The notion of a predictability limit intrinsic to a dynamical system goes back
to the concept of the \emph{butterfly effect}, which describes the
exponential growth of small perturbations in a chaotic system and, therefore,
its sensitive dependence on initial conditions
\parencite{lorenzDeterministicNonperiodicFlow1963}. What is even more
important is that there is an analogous effect that acts across spatial scales.
This is a crucial insight, because it implies that small-scale errors in the
initial conditions can scale upwards \enquote{polluting} the large scales and
thus limit predictability \parencite{lorenzPredictabilityFlowWhich1969,
    palmerRealButterflyEffect2014}. In this view, it does not really matter,
what
the precise source and nature of the initial perturbations are, since scale
interaction in the turbulent system will spread the error across scales and
variables quickly.

The predictability implications of this cross-scale error growth depend
critically on the shape of the kinetic energy spectrum (i.e.~the curve of
$E_{\mathrm{kin}}(k)$ vs. wavenumber $k$), which, in classical theory, is directly linked
to the direction and nature of energy cascades already mentioned
in \cref{sec:intro_block_baroclinic}. In a $E_{\mathrm{kin}} \sim k^{-3}$
range, the characteristic error-doubling time is scale-independent, and the
predictability horizon is in principle indefinitely extendable by reducing
initial-condition uncertainty. In a $E_{\mathrm{kin}} \sim k^{-5/3}$
range, by contrast, error-doubling times decrease with scale, and the total
time required for errors to propagate from arbitrarily small scales up to a
given large scale is finite and bounded
\parencite{leithPredictabilityTurbulentFlows1972,
    sunNewTheoreticalFramework2020}.
The mid-latitude atmosphere exhibits
both regimes: a -5/3 slope at the planetary scales, a -3 slope at synoptic
scales and a -5/3 slope at meso- and
submesoscales again. A schematic of this is depicted in
\cref{fig:energy_spectrum}, with the transitions between the ranges indicated.
In contrast to idealized two-dimensional turbulence, where the transition
between the ranges is determined by the excitation scale alone
\parencite{kraichnanInertialRangesTwodimensional1967}, in \gls{qg} turbulence,
the \enquote{kink} in the spectrum at smaller scales (higher wavenumbers)
is determined by the breakdown of
the \gls{qg}
approximation through divergent motions and inertia--gravity waves
\parencite{lindborgEnergyCascadeStrongly2006,
    calliesTransitionGeostrophicTurbulence2014} or finite
tropopause depth \parencite{tullochTheoryAtmosphericEnergy2006}\footnote{The (in-)validity of assumptions is also what limits
    intrinsic predictability in the two-dimensional Navier--Stokes equations.
    The inability to resolve the viscous subrange breaks Lipschitz continuity,
    thereby limiting predictability for kinetic energy spectra with slopes
    shallower than -3
    \parencite{leungAtmosphericPredictabilityRevisiting2019}.}.

This dual structure is well-established by both aircraft measurements
\parencite{nastromKineticEnergySpectrum1984, lindborgCanAtmosphericKinetic1999}
and high-resolution simulations
\parencite{skamarockAtmosphericKineticEnergy2014,
    liExploringDifferencesAtmospheric2025}. The dynamical origin of the mesoscale
$-5/3$ range---below synoptic, but above smaller scales, where
three-dimensional turbulence becomes the appropriate assumption---remains
debated \parencite{lindborgEnergyCascadeStrongly2006,gageEvidenceForK51979,
    dewanSaturatedcascadeSimilitudeTheory1997}, but its existence implies that the
atmosphere has a finite, intrinsic predictability horizon regardless of how
well the initial state is observed. Errors injected at arbitrarily small scales
will cascade upwards through the $k^{-5/3}$ range and eventually reach the
synoptic scales relevant for blocking and other large-scale phenomena.

In physical space, this error cascade is often conceptualized in a three-stage
process:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item Initial error growth at small scales, dominated by moist convective
          instability
    \item Escalation of the errors to larger scales either through geostrophic
          adjustment \parencite{zhangMesoscalePredictabilityMoist2007,
              bierdelTheoreticalAspectsUpscale2018} or through near-tropopause
          divergence \parencite{baumgartQuantitativeViewProcesses2019}
    \item Propagation of the error to global scales through baroclinic
          instability \parencite{zhangMesoscalePredictabilityMoist2007} or
          non-linear \gls{pv} interactions
          \parencite{baumgartQuantitativeViewProcesses2019}
\end{enumerate}
Several \gls{nwp} model studies with minuscule
\emph{grid-scale initial perturbations}
(so-called \enquote{identical-twin experiments}) have provided evidence
in favour of such a process chain
\parencite{judtInsightsAtmosphericPredictability2018,
    selzEstimatingIntrinsicLimit2019,
    lloverasExploringIntrinsicPredictability2025}. In this conception, moist
convection appears as an energy source that injects and escalates perturbations
at small scales more effectively than the dry turbulent scale interactions
would. This is consistent with idealised experiments showing
that the fast upscale error cascade characteristic of limited
predictability emerges only well inside the mesoscale range, because the
synoptic-scale $k^{-3}$ range suppresses mesoscale
error growth even where the $k^{-5/3}$ range is already resolved
\parencite{leungImpactMesoscaleRange2020}. That this cascade is recovered
at the comparatively coarse resolutions in the studies mentioned above
underscores the moist convection's role.

The shape of the kinetic energy spectrum may, however, be affected by
the resolution of moist processes as well. In dry--versus--moist
experiments with the idealized baroclinic wave model by
\textcite{zhangMesoscalePredictabilityMoist2007}, removing moist processes both
eliminates the mesoscale $k^{-5/3}$ portion of the kinetic energy spectrum and
consistently, removes the intrinsic predictability limit, with forecast error
then scaling linearly with initial-condition error rather than saturating.
The two effects are linked---moist convection breaks the \gls{qg}
approximation, thus establishing the mesoscale -5/3 range, and it is
precisely this range that enables the cross-scale error cascade described above
\parencite{sunIntrinsicPracticalLimits2016}.

Though other mechanisms, including
stratified turbulence and gravity-wave breaking, can also produce a mesoscale
$-5/3$ slope in dry simulations at sufficiently high resolution
\parencite{waiteMesoscaleKineticEnergy2009,
    skamarockAtmosphericKineticEnergy2014}, the moist convection's influence on the
kinetic energy spectrum is evidenced further by a mostly -5/3 slope in the
tropics and a mostly -3 slope in the polar regions observed by
\textcite{judtAtmosphericPredictabilityTropics2020}. Both the tropics and the
polar regions have much different predictability characteristics compared to
the mid-latitudes, indeed, with the tropics showing a much more limited
short-term predictability, but higher long-term predictability due to large
scale teleconnections and oceanic coupling
\parencite{diasEquatorialWavesSkill2018}, whereas the polar regions
might have a higher intrinsic predictability due to the secondary role of
moist convection and its limited influence on the large scale flow
\parencite{lloverasExploringIntrinsicPredictability2025}. Be it through
injection of perturbations or modification of the cascades themselves,
moist convection is therefore thought to be a key driver of the intrinsic
predictability limit.

\subsubsection{Practical predictability}

For initial perturbations of \emph{realistic size and spectrum}, however,
convection turned out to be far less relevant for error growth both in
operational \parencite{lloverasTwoFourdayPredictability2023} and
idealized models \parencite{sunIntrinsicPracticalLimits2016}. Indeed,
when initial perturbations were successively increased from minuscule to
operational size in global model runs by
\textcite{selzTransitionPracticalIntrinsic2022}, ensemble spread growth
was dominantly in the large scales, with spread even decreasing in the
small scales for short lead times. These observations are consistent
with the kinetic energy spectrum of the mid-latitude atmosphere and the
associated cascades, since relatively small errors at the large scales
may dominate the spread before the small-scale errors have escalated
\parencite{rotunnoGeneralizationLorenzsModel2008,
    durranAtmosphericPredictabilityWhy2014}. Consistent with this,
cyclogenesis case studies identify baroclinic rather than convective
instability as the main driver of spread near the tropopause
\parencite{rodwellUncertaintyGrowthForecast2023}, a conclusion supported
by process-based analyses \parencite{baumgartPotentialVorticityDynamics2018,
    baumgartProcessesGoverningAmplification2019} that also point to wave
interactions.

A complementary line of evidence comes from machine-learning forecast
models, which do not resolve the rapid upscale error growth from the
grid scales owing to their oversmoothing tendencies
\parencite{selzCanArtificialIntelligencebased2023,
    liExploringDifferencesAtmospheric2025}. If that cascade were the dominant
control on short-range predictability, such
models should misrepresent the predictability contrast between the tropics and
the
mid-latitudes---yet, they do not \parencite{keaneMidlatitudeTropicalScales2025}.
All of this calls into question the assumption of the convection-driven
butterfly effect governs short-time-scale error growth for realistic
initial perturbations.

Still, convectively active regions are frequently highlighted in studies
concerned with error growth that utilize operational
ensemble forecasts initialized with realistic initial uncertainty
\parencite{sanchezLinkingRapidForecast2020,
    puhFlowDependenceForecast2024, lilloInvestigatingDynamicsError2017,
    magnussonTropicalCycloneActivities2021}. Given
that the upscale propagation pathway is of limited relevance at realistic
initial perturbations, the elevated spread in these regions may instead
reflect enhanced initial uncertainty due to weaker observational
constraints \parencite{rodwellCharacteristicsOccasionalPoor2013}, or be
produced during the assimilation process itself.

Several lines of evidence support this interpretation. Globally,
\textcite{zagarBalancePropertiesShortrange2013,
    zagarThreedimensionalMultivariateModal2015,
    zagarGlobalPerspectiveLimits2017} found that initial ensemble
spread is concentrated in the unbalanced modes, especially in the tropics,
and that these dominate the error growth for roughly the first day---a
pattern more consistent with an initial-uncertainty origin than with an
upscale convective cascade. \textcite{rodwellUncertaintyGrowthForecast2023}
analysed short-range background-forecast error growth in cyclogenesis and found
that it was not spatially congruent with regions of strong precipitation,
indicating that the spread enters the forecast through observations or
through the singular-vector perturbations rather than through convective
error production. In a similar case study,
\textcite{cardinaliRepresentingModelError2014} found that
considerable \gls{eda} spread was concentrated in cloudy regions of developing
cyclones in an assimilation run without stochastic physics. Consistently,
the projection onto unbalanced modes mentioned above was already present also;
the addition of model-error perturbations only marginally enhanced both the
balanced and the unbalanced components.

Observational and assimilation difficulties in cloudy scenes provide a
natural explanation. Satellite radiance uncertainties are larger in cloudy
conditions \parencite{geerObservationErrorsAllsky2011,
    liAssimilationSatelliteSounder2016, geerAllskySatelliteData2018},
aircraft typically avoid regions of intense convection, and rapid
small-scale variability combined with representation challenges in models
further complicates the assimilation
\parencite{janjicRepresentationErrorData2018}.
In particular, convectively active regions are characterized by shorter
decorrelation length scales, implying that, even for the same observational
coverage and quality, the amount of information extractable via assimilation
shrinks \parencite{cardinaliPerformanceMeasurementAdvanced2011,
    leeBackgroundErrorStatistics2020,
    liComparisonTemperatureWind2023}. Even
dedicated high-effort assimilation experiments using far denser
observations than available operationally still show serious analysis error for
convective cells, dominantly limiting their predictability probably also in
the future \parencite{sodhiLimitsPracticalPredictability2025}. Better
constraining the uncertainties in these regions is sometimes done
for incident tropical cyclones or intense thunderstorms with
dropsonde or aircraft measurements \parencite{petersenImpactFutureBenefits2016},
but such efforts are resource-intensive and therefore not routinely performed.

Crucially, although convection acts on small spatial scales, the
spread it produces in the analysis is not confined to small
wavenumbers. As \textcite{lloverasMesoscalePredictabilityMoist2022}
emphasize, an uncertainty localised in \emph{physical} space is necessarily
broad in \emph{spectral} space; convectively driven assimilation uncertainty
therefore does not rely on an up-scale transfer via the butterfly effect
to limit predictability.

\subsubsection{Initial condition uncertainty}

More broadly, initial-condition uncertainty has been shown to be of
first-order importance for operational forecast error growth, roughly equal
to model formulation \parencite{magnussonDependenceInitialConditions2019}.
Its construction continues to affect ensemble reliability and
spread--skill relationships and remains an active area of research
\parencite{langImpactPerturbationMethods2012,
    raynaudComparisonInitialPerturbation2016,
    wangImpactsMultiscaleComponents2023} and is shaped by
both observational and methodological roots. Observation coverage is
highly inhomogeneous: dense in regions of routine aircraft traffic and
over instrumented landmasses, sparse over oceans, the polar regions, and
the tropics \parencite{sociERA5GlobalReanalysis2024}. Satellite
observations have substantially mitigated this inhomogeneity over the
past decades, to the point that they now provide the dominant share of
information content in modern global analyses, particularly through
hyperspectral microwave and infrared sounders
\parencite{bauerQuietRevolutionNumerical2015}. For example,
\textcite{cardinaliObservationInfluenceDiagnostic2013} showed that
satellite observations accounted for roughly 80\% of the observational
influence on the analysis in \gls{ecmwf}'s operational system at that
time, with the remaining 20\% coming from surface-based observations
(including aircraft). Observation networks are human infrastructure, so apart
from impacts by natural events, they are also subject to
societal disruptions like when aircraft and station measurements are impeded by
a pandemic \parencite{inglebyImpactCOVID19Weather2021,
    ImpactsCOVID19Restrictions2020} or geopolitical issues lead to jamming of
radio occultation measurements \parencite{wuGNSSSignalJamming2024} or reduced
data sharing \parencite{GlobalRadiosondeNetwork2016}. This underlines, again,
the need to consider variability in observation coverage when interpreting
analysis uncertainty.
Apart from coverage, however, the quality of
observations also varies in space and time---not only the cloud-related
issues mentioned above, but also through instrument-specific biases,
representativeness errors, and the difficulty of assimilating observations
with spatially
or temporally correlated errors, effectively reducing the information
content of ingested observations
\parencite{janjicRepresentationErrorData2018}.

The assimilation process itself introduces further uncertainty through
approximate background-error covariance modelling, limited ensemble sizes
in ensemble-based systems, sub-optimal balancing of observations and the first
guess
and linearized or approximate observation operators
\parencite{houtekamerReviewEnsembleKalman2016,fengLimitsKnowingEquilibrium2026,
    bannisterReviewOperationalMethods2017, kalnayEarthSystemModeling2024}.
In variational frameworks the background-error covariances set the
relative weighting of the first guess and the observations
\parencite{bannisterReviewOperationalMethods2017}; modern systems estimate
these covariances flow-dependently from short-range ensemble forecasts,
though the estimates are themselves subject to sampling error and require
empirical inflation and localization
\parencite{houtekamerReviewEnsembleKalman2016}. The \gls{eda} realizes this
idea by running the full
assimilation cycle multiple times with perturbed observations and
stochastic physics, directly sampling the analysis-error distribution
\parencite{isaksenEnsembleDataAssimilations2010}. The \gls{ecmwf} employs
such an \gls{eda} \parencite{bonavitaEvolutionECMWFHybrid2016}; it was also
used to produce the \gls{era5} product, where it is provided as a
diagnostic of analysis uncertainty
\parencite{hersbachERA5GlobalReanalysis2020}. Grounding the perturbations
in the assimilation cycle in this way matters because initial-condition
uncertainty is itself flow-dependent: perturbations that fail to reflect
the structure of actual analysis uncertainty propagate as effectively
random noise, inflating member error without contributing predictive
information about the realised error pattern
\parencite{fengEnsembleForecastingForay2024}, whereas perturbations whose
covariance structure is tied to the assimilation cycle inherit
flow-dependence from the underlying observation network and dynamics
\parencite{leutbecherEnsembleForecasting2008}.

The implication for the predictability arguments of the preceding
paragraphs is that initial-condition uncertainty is not a homogeneous,
small-scale \enquote{seed}, whose precise nature essentially does not matter
and whose impact is dictated by spectral theory.
Realistic analysis errors are flow-dependent, projected preferentially
onto dynamically active structures, and span a broad range of scales
\parencite{magnussonFactorsInfluencingSkill2013,
    zhangWhatPredictabilityLimit2019}. Consequently, the error growth
observed in operational ensembles reflects an interaction between intrinsic
cascade dynamics and the structure of the initial spread itself, with the
latter often dominating at short forecast lead times
\parencite{selzTransitionPracticalIntrinsic2022,
    lloverasTwoFourdayPredictability2023}. Reducing initial-condition
uncertainty---especially at the large scales---therefore remains a major lever
for improving practical predictability, even where intrinsic limits constrain
its eventual horizon. Which flow states and processes dominate this
uncertainty is thus a question of practical relevance---both for interpreting
predictability studies and for identifying where improvements in observations,
assimilation, and model formulation would matter most.

These considerations carry over to reanalysis datasets. Because
assimilation uncertainties serve a dual role---both as initial
perturbations for ensemble prediction
\parencite[ch.~6]{kalnayEarthSystemModeling2024} and as uncertainty estimates
accompanying reanalyses---they can be studied
on a far larger data base than any single ensemble dataset affords.
Similarly expensive are dedicated \gls{ose} or \gls{osse} that target
specifically the impact of observational coverage and uncertainty but
a systematic analysis of flow-dependency has not been carries out thus far.

In the
reanalysis setting the associated uncertainty is primarily informative
about the quality and density of the observing system at a given time,
while retaining the flow-state dependence that dominates the forecast
setting. Yet a systematic climatological characterization of this
flow-dependence appears to be lacking. Such a characterization is the
subject of \cref{ch:weatherRegimes}, where the \gls{eda}-based uncertainty of
the \gls{era5} is examined from a climatological perspective, with
particular attention to its relationship with weather regimes such as blockings
and \glspl{wcb}.

\subsection{Parametrizations and model error}\label{sec:intro_predictability_parametrization}
While the previous section focused on initial-condition uncertainty,
forecast error in operational systems is comparably shaped by model
uncertainty. Even given a perfect initial state, a numerical model would
diverge from the true atmosphere due to its structural and numerical
limitations: finite spatial and temporal discretization, imperfect dynamical
cores, and---most prominently---the parametrization of processes that
occur on scales smaller than the model grid. Diagnostic studies at
operational centres find that model and initial-condition uncertainty
contribute to medium-range forecast error in comparable proportions, with
their relative balance shifting with lead time, scale, and variable
\parencite{magnussonFactorsInfluencingSkill2013,
    magnussonDependenceInitialConditions2019,
    magnussonSkillMediumrangeForecast2022}.

The scale separation between the resolved grid and the processes governing
atmospheric thermodynamics is enormous. Even at convection-permitting
resolutions ($\sim$ 1~km), cloud microphysics, turbulence, radiative transfer,
and boundary-layer mixing operate down to micrometre scales and cannot be
explicitly resolved. Parametrizations bridge this gap by expressing
sub-grid processes as functions of resolved-scale variables,
encoding statistical assumptions about the distribution of sub-grid states
consistent with each grid-scale configuration
\parencite{stensrudParameterizationSchemesKeys2007,
    christensenParametrizationWeatherClimate2022}. These schemes are
indispensable but inherently approximate; they represent the primary
source of structural model error in modern \gls{nwp}
\parencite{bernerStochasticParameterizationNew2017}.

A fundamental limitation of conventional parametrizations is that they
are \emph{deterministic}: the same grid-scale state produces the same sub-grid
forcing. In reality, the sub-grid response to a given grid-scale
configuration is better described by a distribution of plausible
tendencies, since the same grid-scale state is consistent with many
realisations of the unresolved flow and because (often empirically) derived or
tuned parameters in the functional formulations are associated with
uncertainties themselves. In the case of sub-grid turbulence, deterministic
parametrization violates essential self-similarity properties of the turbulent
flow with important detrimental implications for the model dynamics
\parencite{palmerNonlinearDynamicalPerspective2001,
    palmerStochasticParametrizationModel2009,
    palmerStochasticWeatherClimate2019}.

Ensemble forecasts that perturb only the initial
conditions are systematically underdispersed (unreliable), particularly at
short lead times and in convectively active regions
\parencite{buizzaStochasticRepresentationModel1999}. An ensemble
forecast that is underdispersed at the outset can stay underdispersed for
the entire forecast horizon highlighting again the importance of correct
initial sampling. Apart from ensemble forecasting,
misrepresenting model uncertainty is also a problem for ensemble-based
data assimilation, where lack of model uncertainty leads to overconfidence in
the background forecast and, therefore, underestimation of the analysis error
and suboptimal assimilation of observations
\parencite{cardinaliRepresentingModelError2014}.

Convection presents a particular challenge in parametrization,
as its statistical behaviour transitions across
the so-called convective grey zone---grid spacings where convection is
neither fully sub-grid nor fully resolved---so that parametrizations
calibrated at one resolution behave inconsistently at another
\parencite{bechtoldRoleShallowConvection2014, fieldExploringConvectiveGrey2017,
    tomassiniConfrontingConvectiveGray2023,
    krollParameterizationAdaptionNeeded2025}.
Depending on the model resolution this issue arises, for example, for tropical
cyclones \parencite{langImpactPerturbationMethods2012} or mesoscale
convective systems \parencite{clarkeDownstreamInfluenceMesoscale2019}, both
having considerable impacts on the larger-scale flow and, of course, objects of
societal importance. The issue arises in a similar manner for boundary-layer
parametrizations \parencite{honnertAtmosphericBoundaryLayer2020}.

An operational response to these limitations is \emph{stochastic
    parametrization}, which represents model uncertainty by adding random
perturbations to the deterministic parametrization scheme. A particular
approach to represent uncertainty in the physics parametrization is the
\gls{sppt} developed at the \gls{ecmwf}
\parencite{buizzaStochasticRepresentationModel1999,
    leutbecherStochasticRepresentationsModel2017}. It applies a multiplicative
scaling to the total physics tendency, which is spatially and temporally
correlated, thereby \enquote{giving life} back to the sub-grid processes rather than
treating them as \enquote{slaves} of the resolved flow
\parencite{palmerStochasticWeatherClimate2019}. Its successor, the \gls{spp}
scheme \parencite{ollinahoProcesslevelRepresentationModel2017,
    langRevisionStochasticallyPerturbed2021} instead perturbs the uncertain
parameters themselves allowing for more granular control over the impact
of individual processes. The recovery of the self-similarity broken by
determinist parametrization of subgrid-scale turbulence is the motivation of
the \gls{skeb} scheme, that compensates excessive dissipation of kinetic energy
through mountain drag, numerical diffusion and other processes
\parencite{shuttsKineticEnergyBackscatter2005,
    bernerSpectralStochasticKinetic2009}. Its introduction accordingly led to the
representation of the observed -5/3 slope in the kinetic energy spectrum in
the mesoscales \parencite{palmerRepresentingModelUncertainty2005}.

The \gls{sppt} and \gls{spp} usually have a larger influence than the
\gls{skeb}, especially in the tropics and near the surface
\parencite{subramanianImpactStochasticPhysics2017,
    christensenStochasticParameterizationNino2017,
    strommenImpactStochasticParametrisations2018}, whereas the \gls{skeb} has a
more pronounced effect in the free troposphere and in the storm track region
\parencite{weisheimerAddressingModelError2014,
    bernerStochasticParameterizationNew2017}. For identical initial conditions,
\gls{sppt} generates  medium-range ensemble spread comparable to multi-model
ensembles \parencite{magnussonSkillMediumrangeForecast2022}, which are
considered the skillful sample of the model uncertainty space given their
fundamental differences in model design
\parencite{palmerRepresentingModelUncertainty2005,
    parkTIGGEPreliminaryResults2008,
    krishnamurtiReviewMultimodelSuperensemble2016}.

Generally, stochastic parametrization schemes have been shown to partly
alleviate the shortcomings of deterministic parametrization, improving model
biases, ensemble reliability and spread--skill relationships
\parencite{leutbecherStochasticRepresentationsModel2017,
    langRevisionStochasticallyPerturbed2021}. Stochasticity can also improve the
representation of weather regime characteristics in a model
\parencite{bernerSystematicModelError2012,
    dawsonSimulatingWeatherRegimes2015}. On a conceptual level, stochasticity can
contribute significantly to the exploration of a system's state space and
the misrepresentation or negligence of which can, in turn, severely alter the
the occurrence and transition frequencies between metastable regions of the
state space. For \gls{nwp} models, this notion translates to weather regime
statistics, whose occurrence and transitions are governed by synoptic forcing
and the \enquote{noisy} influence of sub-grid scale processes
\parencite{molteniRegimesWintertimeCirculation1990}. Frequently, this
interaction occurs via tropical teleconnections preconditioning weather regime
behaviour on seasonal timescales
\parencite{jungInfluenceStochasticParameterization2005}. Consistent with
a changing exploration of the system's attractor rather than a changing
attractor structure itself, the regimes themselves might not change with
the introduction of stochastic parametrization
\parencite{weisheimerAddressingModelError2014}. The influence of stochastic
parametrization on the representation of blockings as one kind of weather
regime as well as \glspl{wcb} will be discussed in more detail in the next
sections.

\subsection{Flow-dependence and state-space structure}
\label{sec:intro_predictability_flowAndState}

As epitomized by both the location-specific effect of the above presented
stochastic schemes as well as the state-dependent initial condition
uncertainties, predictability is severely flow-dependent. Error-growth
rates, the dominant error source, and the resulting forecast uncertainty all
vary substantially with the synoptic regime, geographical region, and
variable under consideration \parencite{magnussonFactorsInfluencingSkill2013,
    ardilouzeFlowDependenceWintertime2021,
    rodwellCharacteristicsOccasionalPoor2013,
    ferrantiFlowdependentVerificationECMWF2015}. This flow-dependence has
multiple origins, several of which have already been mentioned in the
preceding sections. Initial-condition errors scale with the observational
coverage and quality and, in turn, project preferentially onto dynamically
unstable structures, so the same nominal analysis uncertainty produces
different forecast spread depending on the underlying flow
\parencite{buizzaTargetingObservationsUsing1999}. Parametrized processes are
themselves activated unevenly: convection, gravity-wave drag, and
boundary-layer mixing contribute to model uncertainty in ways that depend on
whether the local atmosphere is convectively active, stably stratified, or in
a quiescent regime, so that stochastic parametrizations also produce
flow-dependent spread \parencite{christensenParametrizationWeatherClimate2022}.
The weather systems themselves may differ in their intrinsic propensity to
amplify disturbances: rapidly intensifying cyclones, \glspl{wcb}, and
organized convective systems are typically associated with larger relative
forecast errors than slowly evolving large-scale features
\parencite{ferrantiFlowdependentVerificationECMWF2015,
    gramsAtmosphericDynamicsPerspective2018,
    clarkeDownstreamInfluenceMesoscale2019}.

This flow-dependence is given a precise and unifying formulation in the
language of dynamical systems. The tendency of a system to escalate
minuscule perturbations is measured by Lyapunov exponents, Lyapunov vectors,
and their finite-time counterparts \parencite{youngMathematicalTheoryLyapunov2013,
    ginelliCovariantLyapunovVectors2013}, which have been applied to a wide
range of low-order models \parencite{decruzExploringLyapunovInstability2018,
    vannitsemStatisticalDynamicalProperties2016,
    vannitsemPredictabilityLargescaleAtmospheric2017,
    schubertCovariantLyapunovVectors2015,
    viennetGuidelinesDatadrivenApproaches2022,
    yodenFiniteTimeLyapunovStability1993,
    okaneTransversalityCharacterizationFinite2026} and to real-world data
\parencite{blachutTaleTwoVortices2020a, rypinaApplyingDynamicalSystems2022,
    quinnSystematicCalculationFinitetime2022}. Observation-based evaluation
must first reduce the dimensionality of the
system, since the computation of Lyapunov quantities scales non-linearly
with the degrees of freedom and a number of design choices must be made
including the time scale considered
\parencite{okaneTransversalityCharacterizationFinite2026} and the choice of
metric and variable.

A growing body of work building on
\textcite{lucariniExtremesRecurrenceDynamical2016} characterises the
\emph{local} instantaneous instability of the atmospheric flow through two
complementary quantities: a local dimension, measuring the number of
directions along which the state can locally evolve, and a persistence,
measuring how quickly the flow leaves the vicinity of a given state---both are
markedly state-dependent \parencite{messoriDynamicalSystemsApproach2017,
    leeDynamicalFootprintYearround2024,hochmanAtlanticeuropeanWeatherRegimes2021,
    holmbergLinkEuropeanWarmtemperature2023}.
These data-driven quantities are established through the local geometry and
density of observations instead of relying on a models' tangent linear and
adjoint. The methodology is, however, sensitive to sampling density and
anomalously low dimensionality asserted during peak weather regime occurrence
may be attributed to the high density of observations in in that region of the
state space \parencite{platzerDisentanglingDensityGeometry2025}, so it is
unclear whether this metric truly reflects unstable dimensions in the
Lyapunov sense.

The number of locally unstable dimensions is tightly coupled to
predictability through its bearing on multidimensional error growth: the
stronger and higher-dimensional the local instability, the more independent
directions must be sampled to capture the resulting uncertainty---in terms of
initial conditions as well as model uncertainty. A particularly consequential
case arises when this subspace structure breaks
down. A dynamical system is hyperbolic where its tangent space splits
continuously into invariant stable and unstable subspaces, the former
contracting and the latter expanding; this splitting breaks down when the
covariant Lyapunov vectors align, since the dimensionality of the subspace
decomposition then changes \parencite{beimsAlignmentLyapunovVectors2016,
    sharafiCriticalTransitionsPerturbation2017,
    lucariniNewMathematicalFramework2020, axelsenHyperbolicitySouthernHemisphere2025}.
Alignment of the leading local Lyapunov vectors causes ensemble predictions
to converge onto a small subset of outcomes, effectively collapsing the
ensemble and removing uncertainty. This process thus limits predictability
to the life cycle of coherent structures associated with local hyperbolicity.

The flow-dependent predictability in this geometric sense impacts assimilation
and initial condition uncertainty, too, because all unstable dimensions
need to be sampled to properly capture the resulting uncertainty
\parencite{quinnApplicationLocalAttractor2020,fengEnsembleForecastingForay2024}.
This requirement is hard to meet at high
resolution and in full-scale models \parencite{craigDistributionsConvergenceForecast2022,
    tempestConvergenceForecastDistributions2023}.
Singular and bred vectors generate initial spread
by targeting the most unstable directions, but---in contrast to an
\gls{eda}---do so independently of the observation uncertainties, so that the
flow-dependence they capture is purely dynamical rather than observational.
The variability in the number of local unstable dimensions also implies that
the directions sampled by singular and bred vectors will change depending on
the time scale considered, with longer time scales
potentially sampling directions indicating regime transitions compared to
within-regime variability at shorter time scales
\parencite{derembleFixedPointsStable2009}.

The two origins of flow-dependence---the intrinsic instability of the
attractor and the uncertainty imprinted through the observing system,
pursued in \cref{ch:weatherRegimes}---are therefore best understood as
complementary. Where the flow is dynamically active and its unstable subspace
richer, both the intrinsic error growth and the difficulty of constraining
the state through observations tend to increase together.
This combined flow-dependency is prominently visible in the two features of
synoptic
variability central to this thesis---atmospheric blockings and
\glsentrylongpl{wcb}---which have accordingly attracted considerable
attention, and to which we now turn.

\subsection{Predictability of blockings}
\label{sec:intro_predictability_blockings}

Atmospheric blocking events are frequently discussed in the context of
operational predictability on subseasonal timescales in particular.
On the one hand, this is due to their extraordinary impact on natural and
societal systems outlined in \cref{sec:intro_block_Phenomenology}. On the other
hand, \gls{nwp} models have historically struggled to accurately forecast
blocking events, especially their onset and decay
\parencite{dandreaNorthernHemisphereAtmospheric1998,
    mauritsenBlockingPredictionEnsemble2004,
    quandtForecastVariabilityBlocking2017,
    matsuedaBlockingPredictabilityOperational2009,
    tibaldiOperationalPredictabilityBlocking1990}.
As discussed in \cref{sec:intro_blocking_theories},
the onset and decay phases of blockings especially are notoriously rich in
processes on a
wide range of scales, especially with respect to upstream development like
cyclogenesis and \glspl{wcb}, which projects onto their
predictability \parencite{maddisonUpstreamCycloneInfluence2019}.

Given the long history of research into the dynamical nature of blockings,
their predictability has also been studied using the abstracted low-order
models introduced in \cref{sec:intro_blocking_theories} and using the
dynamical-systems techniques introduced above. Along the notion of
a blocking as a meta-stable regime, the transitions into and out of it are
characterised by enhanced instability and, hence, reduced predictability,
whereas the blocked phase itself may be comparatively stable. To the extent
that such abstracted insights into the structural stability of the attractor
can be related to a real-world phenomenon, some studies have indeed found
larger instability during blocking onset and decay than during the blocked
phase \parencite{schubertDynamicalAnalysisBlocking2016,
    lucariniNewMathematicalFramework2020, hamiltonUsingUnstablePeriodic2025},
though this appears to depend on the type and location of the blocking.

The stability properties
of blockings diagnosed in models have been shown to appear in the real
atmosphere as well \parencite{quinnDynamicalAnalysisReduced2021,
    axelsenHyperbolicitySouthernHemisphere2025}, consistent with the more
general association of meta-stable transitions with enhanced instability
\parencite{farandaSwitchingZonalBlocked2016, farandaDynamicalProxiesNorth2017,
    quinnSystematicCalculationFinitetime2022}. These assessments are, however,
not consistent across all types and locations of blockings. For example,
\textcite{hochmanAtlanticeuropeanWeatherRegimes2021,
    leeDynamicalFootprintYearround2024} calculate the local dimension and persistence
mentioned above for sets of weather regimes in the Euro--Atlantic
\parencite{gramsBalancingEuropesWindpower2017} and
North American sector respectively. The former find that
blocking-associated regimes do not systematically exhibit lower dimensionality
or higher persistence than other regimes with only the Greenland blocking
standing out,
while the latter report particularly low dimension for ridge regimes.
Clearly, determining whether blockings in the real world are characterized
by the same state-space geometry as in idealized models is a non-trivial task
and the predictability and representation in full-scale models judging from
more conventional metrics is a similarly complex picture.

\subsubsection{Weather and climate models}

The operational predictability of blockings depends strongly on their
definition and location. Based on the weather regime classification introduced
by \textcite{gramsBalancingEuropesWindpower2017},
\textcite{buelerYearroundSubseasonalForecast2021} and
\textcite{osmanMultimodelAssessmentSubseasonal2023} provided evidence that
blockings are both the best and worst predicted regimes in the Euro--Atlantic
sector, depending on their location, with a blocking over Greenland achieving
the longest and blockings over Central Europe or Scandinavia the shortest
forecast skill horizon.
In a similar manner, but using a different set of
regimes and only data in winter,
\textcite{ferrantiFlowdependentVerificationECMWF2015} found that operational
forecasts struggled most with transitions to a blocking regime over Northern
Europe while also underestimating their persistence. The persistence of
blocking can also work in its favour: in an analysis of exceptionally good and
poor forecasts, \textcite{hauserExceptionallyPoorGood2026} found blocked
regimes to be comparatively well predicted owing to their persistence, whereas
cyclonic and wavetrain regimes were more prone to forecast busts.
With regard to impact, a blockings stationarity
may also increase the forecast skill horizon once established, like in the
case of the aforementioned Greenland blocking
\parencite{spaethFlowDependenceEnsembleSpread2024}. A similar behaviour is
observed for \glspl{rwp}, in which onset and decay are also associated
with enhanced instability in contrast to their propagation phase
\parencite{prestel-kupfererPredictabilityMidlatitudeRossbywave2024}.

Forecast skill on seasonal to subseasonal time scales can be recovered by
exploiting the slow, predictable Earth system components interacting
with blockings (cf.~\cref{sec:intro_block_interactions}).
\textcite{parkHybridApproachSkillful2024} showed that seasonal forecast skill
for North Pacific blocking can be improved
by leveraging \gls{sst} and \gls{enso} teleconnections, and analogous windows
of opportunity arise for Greenland blocking through the \gls{mjo} and the
stratospheric polar vortex \parencite{spaethFlowDependenceEnsembleSpread2024,
    mockertWindowsOpportunitySubseasonal2026}.

The issues faced by \gls{nwp} models in forecasting blockings are reflected in
atmospheric models' difficulties in representing them realistically on
a climatological level, which is apparent from considerable biases in blocking
frequency, intensity and persistence
\parencite{daviniNorthernHemisphereAtmospheric2016}.
In the climate setting these problems are compounded by long-term trends and
couplings to other Earth-system components.
\textcite{leeIncreasingFrequencyPersistence2026} recently reported that a
seasonal large-ensemble prediction model was able to reproduce Greenland
blocking frequency and persistence but failed to reproduce its trend due to
climate change---illustrating that improved initialization cannot remedy a
mean-state bias. Similarly, \textcite{maddisonMissingIncreaseSummer2024}
found that the increase in summer blocking over Greenland observed since the
beginning of the century is not reproduced, and suggest this might be linked to
\glspl{sst}, sea-ice biases or anthropogenic aerosols. Given what has been
mentioned about the interaction of atmospheric blockings with other
Earth-system components varying on longer timescales, it seems natural to
expect that the predictive skill of models in representing blockings depends on
whether they can accurately represent those components and their interaction
with blockings.

Accurate interactions in models requires faithful representation of small-scale
processes---a challenge in and of itself, as has been discussed above.
Consistently, model resolution and parametrization have a first order impact
on the representation of blockings in climate models especially
\parencite{daviniImprovedWinterEuropean2017,
    schiemannResolutionSensitivityNorthern2017,
    woollingsBlockingItsResponse2018,
    strommenSensitivityEuroatlanticRegimes2019,
    schiemannNorthernHemisphereBlocking2020}. Increasing horizontal resolution,
often together with improved boundary forcing, does reduce biases
\parencite{gaoEnhancedSimulationAtmospheric2025}, but resolution increases
alone do not always remove them \parencite{fabianoEuroatlanticWeatherRegimes2020}.
Climatological evaluations moreover sometimes struggle to robustly identify
effects of resolution and parametrization on blocking representation, owing to
the large interannual variability of blocking
occurrence \parencite{hartungResolutionPhysicsAtmosphere2017}. Resolution is
thus important but not, on its own, sufficient.

This insufficiency has directed growing attention to small-scale and moist
processes in understanding blocking biases in weather and climate models
\parencite{wandelWhyMoistDynamic2024, steinfeldResponseMoistDry2022,
    nabizadeh3DStructureNorthern2021}.
\textcite{dolores-tesillosRoleMoistDry2025}
find blocking biases to be caused either by misrepresented \glspl{wcb} or
through a biased mean flow. Remarkably, the pattern of that biased mean flow
might be related to the notorious \enquote{too-zonal-too-equatorward}
storm-track bias in climate models, which, in turn, might also be linked to
moist processes and their resolution
\parencite{auestadLatentHeatingFeedback2025, schemmEliminatingDecadesOld2023}.

\subsubsection{Parametrization and stochasticity}

Because the relevant small-scale processes are largely sub-grid, their role in
blocking biases has been probed most directly through parametrization
experiments---with mixed results. \textcite{dawsonSimulatingWeatherRegimes2015}
observed that the regime structure generally, and the representation of
blockings in particular, was improved by introducing \gls{sppt} to a similar
extent as doubling the horizontal resolution.
\textcite{daviniRepresentationWinterNorthern2021} found that the inclusion of
\gls{sppt} and \gls{skeb} improved blocking representation in the \gls{ecmwf}
seasonal prediction model, though the improvements were insufficient to
eliminate significant biases in the European sector; they further note that
errors in the atmospheric component are more relevant than errors in ocean
representation. Using the same climate model,
\textcite{hartungResolutionPhysicsAtmosphere2017} observed that parametrization
upgrades improved blocking representation especially in winter, while
\textcite{filippucciImpactStochasticPhysics2024} found that blocking
representation deteriorated upon inclusion of both \gls{sppt} and \gls{skeb},
arguing for a retuning of model parameters.
% Clearly, the role of stochasticity
% in blocking representation is complex and not yet fully understood, motivating
% further research into the underlying mechanisms.

A conceptual rationale for why stochasticity should matter at all is offered by
\textcite{molteniRegimesWintertimeCirculation1990}, who argue that improved
representation of blockings---whether through resolution increase or more
accurate parametrization---does not necessarily translate into more predictable
blockings. In their conception, the representation pertains to the long-term
statistics of the system, determined by correct attractor structure and
stochastic forcing, while the precise timing of a blocking event remains
random---or at least hard to predict. If \gls{nwp} models indeed tend to relax
to their climatological mean state, then stochasticity supports the
system to develop meridional jet
configurations and, by extension, blockings as frequently as observed
\parencite{weisheimerAddressingModelError2014,
    palmerRepresentingModelUncertainty2005,
    jungInfluenceStochasticParameterization2005}. There are still many open
questions pertaining to the role of stochasticity in blocking representation,
in particular with regard to the role of mean-state changes compared to
changes in the variability and predictability and the conflicting evidence
about the respective roles of resolution and stochastic parametrization in
representing blockings underscore the need for a better understanding of the
underlying mechanisms.

The clearest evidence that stochasticity actively shapes regime behaviour comes
from low-order models, where the effect can be isolated from the complexity of
full \gls{nwp} systems. \textcite{christensenSimulatingWeatherRegimes2015}
found increased regime persistence and structure in an idealized model when
introducing autocorrelated noise, while white noise had no comparable effect.
A similar persistence-increasing effect of random perturbations has been
identified in the \textcite{lorenzDeterministicNonperiodicFlow1963} system by
\textcite{kwasniokEnhancedRegimePredictability2014} and in the
\gls{cdv} model by
\textcite{dorringtonInteractionStochasticForcing2023}. The latter model
has been introduced in \cref{sec:intro_blocking_theories} and is, in their
formulation, driven by
noise in addition to its deterministic flow---and the noise itself may be
read as a stand-in for the diabatic forcing that moist processes
such as \glspl{wcb} supply in the real atmosphere or the stochastic
perturbations noted above to impact blocking representation in models.

In the setting of \textcite{dorringtonInteractionStochasticForcing2023},
for a subregion of the attractor in the vicinity of the
unstable
fixed point identified with a blocking, adding an intermediate level of noise
results in an increase in the time steps needed between entering and
exiting---an effect that may be termed \emph{stochastic intertia}
(similar phenomena
are known to exist in related forms in other mathematical settings;
\textcite{franaszekInfluenceNoiseMean1991,reimannNoisyOnedimensionalMaps1996,
    altmannNoiseEnhancedTrappingChaotic2010}).
\textcite{dorringtonInteractionStochasticForcing2023} argue that this
behaviour is caused by the system accelerating away from the blocking
fixed point along a single unstable dimension, which is a property observable
in the deterministic setting already. This suggests that the \emph{stochastic
    inertia} may be identified without the need to actually observe or simulate
the noisy system, which would be a crucial advantage for real-world
applications, since systems may either be too complex to simulate or because
the underlying dynamics are unknown. \Cref{ch:stochasticInertia} presents
a method to achieve this, while offering valuable insights into the precise
mechanics behind the interaction of stochasticity and (blocking) regime
behaviour. Given the complex picture painted by the inquiries into the
blockings from a dynamical-systems perspective given above, studying the
behaviour of the \gls{cdv} system's response to noise also helps to elucidate
the role of unstable dimensions.

As elaborated in \cref{sec:intro_predictability_parametrization},
stochasticity is used in operational models to represent the uncertainty
associated with sub-grid processes. The strong baroclinic and moist convective
instabilities in \glspl{wcb} make them efficient propagators and amplifiers
of this uncertainty, as will be discussed in the following section.

\subsection{Uncertainty in \glspl{wcb}} \label{sec:intro_wcb_uncertainty}
\glspl{wcb} are tightly coupled to extratropical cyclogenesis and the
downstream evolution of the large-scale flow through intense diabatic
heating and transport of air masses. As a consequence, even small
initial-condition or model errors in \gls{wcb} inflow, ascent, and
cloud--precipitation processes can undergo rapid error growth and project
efficiently onto the baroclinic and diabatically driven instabilities of
the developing cyclone, thereby amplifying ensemble spread and forecast
uncertainty downstream \parencite{rodwellWhyWarmConveyor2018,
    baumgartProcessesGoverningAmplification2019,
    wandelSystematicEvaluationWarm2021}.
\textcite{picklWarmConveyorBelts2023} show that for medium-range forecasts
in the Euro--Atlantic sector, high \gls{wcb} activity is associated with
anticyclonic patterns downstream and elevated levels of overall forecast
uncertainty, which they attribute to both upstream initial-condition errors
of upstream large-scale patterns
and model errors intrinsic to the processes dominant in the cyclone. This
attribution connects back to one of the central topics of this section:
the relative importance of initial-condition and model uncertainty.

The case for model uncertainty rests on the fact that the diabatic
processes within \glspl{wcb} are themselves parametrized and uncertain, so
that they act as a source of error rather than merely amplifying errors
introduced elsewhere. Deep-convection schemes can substantially alter
\gls{wcb} structure and its interaction with the jet
\parencite{riviereImpactDeepConvection2021}, while microphysical
parametrizations modulate latent-heating profiles and precipitation
characteristics \parencite{joosInfluenceMicrophysicalProcesses2012,
    mazoyerMicrophysicsImpactsWarm2021, mazoyerImpactMixedphaseCloud2023}. The
consequences propagate to the large scale: misrepresentation of diabatic
processes can lead to insufficient Rossby-wave amplification and
ridge-building \parencite{graySystematicModelForecast2014}, and seemingly
minor microphysics changes can induce large downstream flow differences,
consistent with the idea that \gls{wcb} outflow perturbations can trigger
disproportionate Rossby-wave responses and regime transitions
\parencite{joosImpactDifferentIFS2016}. That model formulation matters is
underscored by the observation that stochastic perturbations from \gls{sppt},
though zero-mean by construction, exert a non-zero mean effect on \glspl{wcb}
by triggering non-linear instabilities, making them more frequent and more
intense \parencite{picklEffectStochasticallyPerturbed2022}. The abstract
additive noise studied in low-order models
(\cref{ch:stochasticInertia}) can be read as a proxy for precisely this
kind of stochastic diabatic forcing, linking the dynamical-systems
perspective on regime persistence to the moist processes that supply it in
the real atmosphere.

These model deficiencies are particularly consequential for blocking, which
\glspl{wcb} frequently and heavily affect (\cref{sec:intro_block_diabatic}):
misrepresentation of \gls{wcb} outflow has been identified as a key factor
in severe forecast busts with a missed blocking onset
\parencite{gramsAtmosphericDynamicsPerspective2018}, and is more
systematically associated with elevated forecast uncertainty in blocking
events \parencite{wandelWhyMoistDynamic2024}. Encouragingly, the situation
has improved over time: \textcite{madonnaVerificationNorthAtlantic2015}
found improvements in the representation of \glspl{wcb} across the 2000s in
the \gls{ifs}, while still noting cases of severe forecast errors, and
\textcite{maddisonImpactModelUpgrades2020} find that model upgrades aimed at
diabatic and cloud-process realism can mitigate \gls{wcb}-related forecast
busts of blockings.

The competing role of the initial state has come into
focus more recently: sensitivity analyses suggest that inflow conditions can
control bulk ascent and flow modification while microphysics primarily
imprints fine-scale precipitation structure
\parencite{hieronymusAlgorithmicDifferentiationSensitivity2022,
    oertelSensitivitiesWarmConveyor2025}. Whether this balance holds across all
flow states, however, remains an open question. Crucially, these studies
frame the initial-state contribution as one of \emph{model} initial
conditions; the extent to which \gls{wcb}-related amplification instead
reflects elevated \emph{observational} and \emph{assimilation} uncertainty
in the inflow region is examined in \cref{ch:weatherRegimes}. In either case,
it is fair to say that \glspl{wcb} act as propagators and amplifiers of
forecast uncertainty---in particular when interacting with blockings,
highlighting again the need for further research.


\section{Research questions}\label{sec:intro_roadmap}

Atmospheric blocking and \glspl{wcb} are important features of the mid-latitude
circulation with large impacts on natural and societal systems. They are also
associated with predictability issues both on theoretical and practical
grounds. Adequately, a growing body of research has been dedicated to
understanding the interactions between the two, their representation in models
as well as intrinsic dynamical nature. The aim of this thesis is to contribute
to this research. The complex multiscale nature of the phenomena suggests
important insights may be uncovered through advanced, non-linear methods from
dynamical systems theory and statistics. The translation and application of
such methods to the phenomena of interest constitutes a key contribution of
this thesis. More specifically, the following research questions will be
addressed in the following chapters, with the topics ordered from most
abstracted to most applied:

\begin{description}[
        % style=multiline,
        % leftmargin=3em,
        % labelsep=0em
    ]
    \item[\textbf{\Cref{ch:stochasticInertia}}] How do stochastic perturbations interact with the regime dynamics of a
        dynamical system? What does this interaction tell us about the
        predictability and stability of the system? Does the impact of
        stochasticity on regime behaviour observed in the studies cited in
        \cref{sec:intro_predictability_blockings}
        occur because the system's attractor changes or because the system's
        movement on the attractor is altered? Can we study these effects
        from an observational perspective and without access to the underlying
        dynamics?
    \item[\textbf{\Cref{ch:weatherRegimes}}] What governs assimilation uncertainty on synoptic scales? What are
        the relative roles of observation, model and intrinsic uncertainty? How do
        the answers to these questions depend on the active weather regime,
        the presence of diabatic processes in general and  the occurrence of
        \glspl{wcb} specifically? What are the implied repercussions in light of
        the predictability characteristics of blockings and \glspl{wcb} described
        in \cref{sec:intro_predictability_blockings,sec:intro_wcb_uncertainty} on
        the one hand and the relative importance of initial-condition vs. model
        uncertainty on the other hand (\cref{sec:intro_predictability_chaos,sec:intro_predictability_parametrization})?
    \item[\textbf{\Cref{ch:coherence}}] How spatially coherent are the air masses that pass through a
        blocking? What additional insights can be gained from Lagrangian
        tracing of air parcels, if instead of individual trajectories,
        sets of air parcels that are robust to small perturbations are
        considered? Does this provide an avenue to
        objectively identify \glspl{wcb} explicitly conceiving them as spatially
        coherent (cf.~\cref{sec:intro_wcb_def})? How do the answers to these question change across the
        lifecycle of a blocking and a \gls{wcb}?
    \item[\textbf{\Cref{ch:conclusion}}] What do the answers to the above questions imply for the
        representation and predictability of blockings and \glspl{wcb} in models
        and the real world? What is the role of model and initial condition
        uncertainty? How does this connect to the established theories and
        what questions do they raise for future research?
\end{description}
