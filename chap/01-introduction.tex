\section{Atmospheric blocking}\label{sec:intro_blocking}
The term \enquote{atmospheric blocking} emerged in weather forecast and atmospheric research
in the early 20th century initially to describe persistent surface high-pressure
regions \enquote{blocking} the usual eastward progression of low-pressure cyclones
(\textcite{rexBlockingActionMiddle1950}; cf.~also \textcite{lupoAtmosphericBlockingEvents2021} and references therein).
It has since come to be understood as a large-scale disturbance
extending through the whole troposphere and interacting with the stratosphere.
The term \enquote{blocking} or simply \enquote{block} notably does not refer
to its geometric shape as a somewhat coherent singular entity, especially since
they can come in different shapes and sizes and are usually embedded in the
wavelike structure of the flow.

Though there is still a lack of consensus on the exact definition of a blocking
and a coherent unifying theory of them is still
at large \parencite{woollingsBlockingItsResponse2018}, the concept broadly encompasses
the notions of a persistent, quasi-stationary, large-scale anticyclonic flow pattern
that occurs in the mid- to high latitudes. Its persistence implies lifetimes on the
order of days to weeks, which is long compared to typical synoptic weather systems.
It is quasi-stationary in that its characteristic flow pattern and position almost do not change
over its lifetime measured by the usual magnitudes of change on those timescales.
This is why some theories liken the phenomenon to quasi-stationary (standing) planetary
waves analogous to waves in other physical systems \parencite[e.g.,~][]{tungTheoryStationaryLong1979, nakamuraAtmosphericBlockingTraffic2018}.

Any weather situation considered a blocking usually involves considerable displacement
of relatively warm and low \gls{pv} subtropical air masses poleward, introducing
or reinforcing a ridge in the upper-level flow. In severe cases this ridge might
even detach from the subtropical reservoir judging from closed contours of low
\gls{pv} values on isentropic surfaces or, equivalently, high potential temperature
values on surfaces of constant \gls{pv} \parencite{pellyNewPerspectiveBlocking2003}.

\subsection{Definitions} \label{sec:intro_block_def}
Consistent with the rather diffuse understanding introduced above, there is no
universally accepted formal definition of atmospheric blocking, and different
diagnostics are adopted depending on purpose, scale of analysis and, arguably,
school of thought \parencite{barnesMethodologyComparisonBlocking2012,barriopedroApplicationBlockingDiagnosis2010}.
Blockings are frequently defined either by indices specifically designed for them
or as one of a number of different weather regimes which, in turn, result from
some kind of clustering of instantaneous atmospheric states.

Indices may be differentiated by which meteorological field they use and by whether
they identify a blocking based on absolute or anomaly fields. The choice of
index will importantly also determine the precise shape of the detected flow field
and will identify different parts of the blocking \parencite{pinheiroAtmosphericBlockingIntercomparison2019}.
For instance, one very
common group of blocking indices is based on the geopotential height field and
its gradient or the associated synoptic winds. This rather kinematic
perspective exploits the blocking's characteristic meridional flow field as a hallmark
and is based on work by \textcite{lejenasCharacteristicsNorthernHemisphere1983} and
\textcite{tibaldiOperationalPredictabilityBlocking1990}. More dynamically and
object-oriented indices identify anomalies in the fields of \gls{pv} or potential
temperature \parencite{schwierzPerspicaciousIndicatorsAtmospheric2004}, a lens that
was pioneered by \textcite{pellyNewPerspectiveBlocking2003} though they identified
gradient reversals instead of anomalies. Since potential temperature and \gls{pv}
are materially conserved under adiabatic and (in the case of \gls{pv}) inviscid conditions,
air-masses that are designated as belonging to a blocking may be traced back and
forth to understand the formation and decay of the blocking event.

A complementary perspective conceptualises blocking as a preferred large-scale
circulation regime within a reduced phase space of atmospheric variability.
This understanding originates from the interpretation of the atmosphere as a
forced-dissipative dynamical system whose long-term behaviour is governed by its
lower-dimensional attractor 
\parencite[e.g.,~][]{dijkstraNonlinearClimateDynamics2013,weilandIntransitiveAtmosphereDynamics2021, strommenTopologicalPerspectiveWeather2023, ghilReviewArticleDynamical2023}.
Temporal evolution then guides the system as a trajectory on the attractor with
its location in phase space determining its dynamic behaviour as well as its expression
in physical space. It may then be insightful to partition this
attractor into a finite set of metastable or recurrent regimes as a dimensionality reduction
\parencite{michelangeliWeatherRegimesRecurrence1995}.
Blocking emerges as one of multiple such weather regimes, defined by persistence
and transition probabilities rather than local anomalies or gradient reversals
in the Eulerian field.

Studying the atmosphere according to a small number of characteristic regimes or
clusters is probably as old as synoptic meteorology itself and has been a ubiquitous
tool in atmospheric sciences ever since \parencite{hannachiLowFrequencyNonlinearity2017},
especially in the North Atlantic European sector 
\parencite[e.g.,~][]{vautardMultipleWeatherRegimes1990,nealFlexibleApproachDefining2016, gramsLifeCycleDefinition2026}. 
Given the enormous dimensionality of the atmospheric system, meaningful insight into
the underlying attractor's structure may necessitate abstraction into low-order
models with some exhibiting blocking-like regimes
\parencite{crommelinMechanismAtmosphericRegime2004,kallenBifurcationPropertiesQuasigeostrophic1982,lucariniNewMathematicalFramework2020}
facilitating translation between paradigm models and the real world.

Finally, from a wave-dynamical perspective, blocking can
be interpreted as a nonlinear manifestation of amplified, breaking, or quasi-stationary
Rossby waves \parencite{tungTheoryStationaryLong1979,nakamuraAtmosphericBlockingTraffic2018}.
In this framework, blockings are associated with \glspl{rwp} \parencite{wirthRossbyWavePackets2018}
or maxima of \gls{lwa} fluxes \parencite{huangLocalFiniteamplitudeWave2016}
and will thus be identified based on characteristic
patterns in the wavenumber rather than the physical domain.

The differing definitions—index-based, regime-based, and wave-dynamical—thus highlight
that blocking may be understood alternately as a local kinematic signature, a metastable
circulation state, or a planetary-wave configuration, depending on the theoretical
and diagnostic perspective adopted.


\subsection{Phenomenology} \label{sec:intro_block_Phenomenology}
Climatologically, blocking events are more prominent in the \gls{nh}
compared to the \gls{sh} due a stronger land-sea contrast and orography \parencite{woollingsBlockingItsResponse2018}.
They are also larger, more intense \parencite{nabizadeh3DStructureNorthern2021}
and more frequent in winter than in summer, with a peak frequency
in the North Atlantic sector of roughly every 5--6 days on average.
Aside from the North Atlantic, the North Pacific and, to a lesser extent, the
Eurasian sector are also preferred regions for blocking occurrence in the \gls{nh}.
Their implied temporal and spatial scales lets them exert a major influence on
the mid-latitude circulation and weather on a continental to planetary scale for
whole seasons even \parencite{jensenDynamicAnalysisRecord2015}.

Where they occur, blockings can exert a considerable impact on societies and
ecosystems alike, a feature that numerous publications have drawn attention to
\parencite[e.g.,~][]{kautzAtmosphericBlockingWeather2021,whanInfluenceAtmosphericBlocking2016,pfahlQuantifyingRelevanceAtmospheric2012}.
The implied persistence can lead to sustained weather conditions of
different nature depending on season and location relative to the high pressure center.
Their eponymous blocking and (meridional)deflecting of air masses can favour anomalously and
extremely hot or cold conditions and stationary cyclones on their flanks leading
to anomalously high moisture transport and associated precipitation
\parencite[e.g.,~][]{brunnerConnectingAtmosphericBlocking2017,
lenggenhagerAtmosphericBlocksModulate2019, mohrRelationshipAtmosphericBlocking2019,
 mohrMultidisciplinaryAnalysisExceptional2023}.

Apart from their effect on the advection of air masses, the high pressure associated
with blockings favours subsidence and, by extension, cloud-free conditions and
reduced vertical mixing in their centers. Together with reduced wind speeds also 
in the horizontal, this frequently leads to heat waves and droughts in summer 
as well as cold spells in winter \parencite{pfahlQuantifyingRelevanceAtmospheric2012, brunnerDependencePresentFuture2018}.
\textcite{herrmannAtmosphericBlocksIncrease2025} showed that disproportianately many
severe wildfires in the Northern high latitudes were associated with blocked episodes in summer.
Reduced mixing may also entail episodes of poor air quality, all of which put severe
pressure on societal systems and human health \parencite{webberDynamicalImpactRossby2017,popeImpactSynopticWeather2016}.
Prolonged episodes of decreases in wind energy supply and increased energy demand
caused by blockings in winter are considered a major risk for the energy sector,
especially with the transition to renewable sources \parencite{oteroCharacterizingRenewableEnergy2022, vanderwielInfluenceWeatherRegimes2019},
potentially aggravating poor air quality through increased fossil fuel use.

\subsection{Interactions} \label{sec:intro_block_interactions}
The lack of a unified theory of blockings
is probably caused by their interaction
with processes and earth system components on a wide range of spatial
and temporal scales.

The large-scale nature of blockings is evident from their interrelation with planetary-scale
teleconnections. Given their spatial proximity, a modulating relation between
blockings and the \gls{nao} \parencite{grayElevenyearSolarCycle2016} and \gls{pna}
\parencite{croci-maspoliAtmosphericBlockingSpacetime2007} for \gls{nh} blockings
and the \gls{sam} \parencite{oliveiraNewClimatologySouthern2014} for the \gls{sh}
seem natural. More surprisingly perhaps, tropical teleconnections such as
the \gls{enso} \parencite{zhangENSOrelatedSouthernHemisphere2023, mckennaImpactsNinoDiversity2023, barriopedroRelationshipENSOStratospheric2014},
or the \gls{mjo} \parencite{hendersonInfluenceMaddenJulian2016} have also been linked to
blocking frequency and persistence, highlighting the role of planetary-scale disturbances.
More specifically, large scale divergence caused by tropical convection is associated
with excitations of globally propagating Rossby waves \parencite{sardeshmukhGenerationGlobalRotational1988},
which can in turn lead to blocking modification or formation.

The importance of each of the above mechanisms and teleconnections differ regionally and seasonally,
implying a complex two-way interaction between blockings and other components of the earth system
\parencite{burkhardtPlanetarySynopticscaleInteractions2005, nakamuraRoleHighLowFrequency1997}.
More specifically, blocking dynamics are influenced by the polar cryosphere
through heat and moisture fluxes and accompanying sea-ice anomalies on seasonal scales
\parencite{tyrlisUralBlockingDriving2019,woollingsArcticWarmingAtmospheric2014, drijfhoutSpontaneousAbruptClimate2013}.
There, a strong two-way interaction with polar vortex dynamics and the stratosphere
more generally has been established, mediated in particular by upward wave
activity flux and associated tropopause displacements
\parencite{woollingsAssociationsStratosphericVariability2010, colucciDiagnosticComparisonTropospheric2015, yessimbetPathwaysInfluenceNorthern2022}.
From 27 objectively identified \glspl{ssw} in the \gls{nh}
between 1957 and 2001, \textcite{martiusBlockingPrecursorsStratospheric2009}
found that 25 were preceded by blocking events in the North Atlantic sector.

Blockings have also been shown to exert a first order influence on the subpolar
ocean \parencite{hakkinenAtmosphericBlockingAtlantic2011}. On the other hand, \gls{sst}
patterns and according gradients, at times, provide the necessary baroclinic
forcings and heat and moisture content for blockings to form, controlling their
occurrence frequency and intensity \parencite{christSeaSkyUnderstanding2025, mathewsGulfStreamMoisture2024}.
\textcite{narinesinghUniformSSTWarming2024} however showed that a uniform increase
in \glspl{sst} is responsible for the observed change in blocking frequency in a
climate model rather than a modulation of \gls{sst} gradients. This highlights
the uncertainty associated with the precise preconditioning of \glspl{sst} for blockings
with their according fluxes. In a similar manner, blocking dynamics are also shaped by surface level diabatic
sensible and latent heat fluxes over land \parencite{tillyCalculatedHeightTendencies2008}
and their climatological occurrence is modulated by land-sea contrasts and orography
through initiated planetary waves \parencite{heImpactLandSea2014}.

\subsection{Processes and theories of atmospheric blocking}\label{sec:intro_blocking_theories}
Given all that has been stated above about the complex phenomenon of atmospheric blockings
from practical perspectives, it is not surprising that there is not a single mathematical
theory explaining all of its facets. That said, a number of mathematical theories have
been proposed that explain complementing aspects of the phenomenon, based on differing
assumptions and perspectives.

\subsubsection{Barotropic theories} \label{sec:intro_block_barotropic} 
More traditional theories on blockings focus on barotropic large scale
dynamics---a stance that is justified by the fact that the vertical structure
of atmospheric blockings is dominantly equivalent-barotropic, with little vertical
variation in potential temperature and wind \parencite{nabizadeh3DStructureNorthern2021}.
The earliest synoptic work took a primarly diagnostic stance with some likening the phenomenon
to hydrolic jumps in other jet like flows \parencite{rexBlockingActionMiddle1950}
inspired by the observationally guided understanding of a blockings as a breakdown
of the usual zonal flow \parencite{berggrenAerologicalStudyZonal1949}. These merely
descriptive works set the stage for more quantative theories with some degree of
explanatory power.

In a barotropic flow with external (usually topographic and radiative) forcing
blocking can appear as a stationary (Rossby-) wave phenomenon \parencite{rossbyRelationVariationsIntensity1939}.
This is because it can be shown that for certain Rossby wave constellations, the
waves' group velocity exactly opposes the advection with the mean flow.
Assuming linearized barotropic
dynamics around some climatological flow, this perspective can explain
regions of climatalogical blocking occurrence, but lacks mechanisms necessary to
understand onset and persistence in a way comparable with observations \parencite{hoskinsSteadyLinearResponse1981}.

This was also pointed out by \textcite{charneyMultipleFlowEquilibria1979}, who
proposed a model in which non-linearities were essential for blocking occurrence.
Similar to the notion of stationary waves, their model admitted multiple unstable
fixed points, each associated with a different flow regime
and opened the understanding of blocking as a (quasi-)stationary regime governing
the atmospheric system's dynamics in its vicinity in state space. The idea, that
the mid-latitude atmosphere switches between such regimes chaotically due to 
stochastic forcing from synoptic variability was highly influential and has been
extensively applied to all types of models \parencite{legrasPersistentAnomaliesBlocking1985,
kondrashovEmpiricalModeReduction2006}
as well as
 the real world \parencite{
moClusterAnalysisMultiple1988, vautardMultipleWeatherRegimes1990,
farandaSwitchingZonalBlocked2016}.
It offers a mechanism for
regime persistence and sudden transitions, but its applicability is questioned by
its sensitivity to parameter choices \parencite{crommelinMechanismAtmosphericRegime2004}
and whether the real atmosphere possesses regimes in the dynamical sense is debated
\parencite{stephensonExistenceMultipleClimate2004}.

The quasi-stationary wave perspective was advanced by \textcite{tungTheoryStationaryLong1979}
who conceived blockings as waves resonant with topography and land-sea contrast, but
kept in place by specific conditions of the background flow. Non-linearities are
assumed in this model and baroclinic extensions are discussed, though not necessary.
Time-dependent amplification explains realistic onset and persistence mechanisms
lacking in the basic Rossby wave concept above.

A separate set of theories argue that due to their nearly equivalent-barotropic
structure, blockings resemple individual vortices interacting with other vortices
in a background flow. \textcite{mcwilliamsApplicationEquivalentModons1980} showed that
the (non-linear) equivalent-barotorpic vorticity equation admits vortex pairs called \enquote{modons}
as solutions similar in structure and behaviour to blockings. Similar results
can be obtained when considering point vortices as abstractions \parencite{mullerApplicationsPointVortex2015}.
In a related view, \textcite{hainesEddyForcedCoherentStructures1987} interpret
synoptic eddies as providing a dipole \gls{pv} forcing that supports the coherent
modon structure against dissipation. Modon theory can be seen as a paradigm-level 
illustration, especially considering that the real background flow is highly variable.

Still, the fact that transient eddies can systematically
transfer vorticity to foster large scale flow configurations has been well established
for a long time \parencite[e.g.,~][]{kuoVorticityTransferRelated1951} and atmospheric blockings
do not seem to form an exception (their spatio-temporal scales characterizing them
as the latter rather than the former). 
Even in barotropic flows eddy-mean flow 
\parencite{shuttsPropagationEddiesDiffluent1983,legrasPersistentAnomaliesBlocking1985},
eddy-eddy \parencite{yamazakiVortexVortexInteractions2013, luoNonlinearMultiscaleInteraction2014}
or sometimes both types of interactions \parencite{cashDynamicalProcessesBlock2000} 
can lead to the kinds of persistent anomalies associated with blocking.

A closely related nonlinear mechanism for blocking onset and maintenance is Rossby wave breaking.
It occurs when amplified planetary waves undergo irreversible overturning 
(visible in \gls{pv} contours on isentropic surfaces), leading to the
meridional displacement of \gls{pv} typical for blockings \parencite{mcintyreBreakingPlanetaryWaves1983,
thorncroftTwoParadigmsBaroclinicwave1993}. Anticyclonic wave breaking in particular
has been linked to the formation of persistent high-pressure anomalies resembling blocking events 
\parencite{pellyNewPerspectiveBlocking2003, masatoWavebreakingCharacteristicsMidlatitude2012}.
Unlike linear stationary-wave theory, this framework emphasizes nonlinear \gls{pv}
rearrangement in physical space and naturally accounts for jet deformation and cutoff formation. 
At the same time, Rossby wave breaking is often preceded by or interacts with
baroclinic wave amplification \parencite{tamarin-brodskyRelationRossbyWavebreaking2024}, 
indicating that while the breaking itself can be described
in largely barotropic \gls{pv} terms, its excitation frequently originates in baroclinic dynamics.

The Rossby wave breaking framework is very similar to theories based on \glspl{rwp} 
\parencite{wirthRossbyWavePackets2018} or \gls{lwa} fluxes 
\parencite{huangLocalFiniteamplitudeWave2016,nakamuraAtmosphericBlockingTraffic2018}.
Here, conservation laws are established for the amount of undulation of the jet stream 
and a severe undulation is then associated with a blocking event. The theory elegantly connects
to more traditional wave-dynamical perspectives, while emphasizing locality. Though principally
barotropic, baroclinic and diabatic processes appear as source terms in this theory and
are, thus, vital elements in the real life explanation of observed events \parencite{neal2021PacificNorthwest2022}.

The above shows that what genuinely barotropic theories generally often lack is
a mechanism for the strength of the
disturbances necessary for blocking formation and maintanence of the scale comparable with observations.
Consistent with this, rapid onsets of blockings are rarely observed in such models and
even if upstream barotropic eddies support this process, cyclones in the real atmosphere
are scarcely barotropic \parencite{colucciExplosiveCyclogenesisLargescale1985,
frederiksenOnsetBlockingCyclogenesis1984, vautardMultipleWeatherRegimes1990}.

\subsubsection{Baroclinic theories} \label{sec:intro_block_baroclinic}
Atmospheric blockings appear not in a single layer flow, but in a stratified, vertically sheared
atmosphere in which baroclinic instability continuously generates synoptic-scale
eddies shaping the general circulation. Classical linear instability theory
\parencite[e.g.,~][]{charneyDynamicsLongWaves1947, eadyLongWavesCyclone1949} and
energy cycle considerations \parencite{lorenzAvailablePotentialEnergy1955},
demonstrate that in such a setting, generic instabilites convert available
potential energy into eddy kinetic energy on synoptic scales.
Baroclinic theories tackle the central question how these synoptic
eddies feed back onto the planetary-scale flow to produce persistent large-scale
anomalies \parencite{shuttsCaseStudyEddy1986}. In this sense, the baroclinic perspective
complements more traditional barotropic
frameworks introduced above by seeking a physically consistent explanation for transient
eddies capable of initiating, sustaining or destabilizing large-scale flow regimes, and they
provide models with more realistic assumptions than idealized approaches
based on modons or point-vortex dynamics.

The feedbacks between eddies of any scale and the large scale flow are closely tied
to the nature of turbulent cascades in geophysical flows. In fully three-dimensional
turbulence, energy cascades downscale toward dissipation \parencite{popeTurbulentFlows2000}.
This behaviour occurs at small scales in the atmosphere, where the rotation and
stratification are less influential. 
By contrast, in two--dimensional turbulence the existence of a second conserved 
quantity (the enstrophy in addition to the kinetic energy) induces 
(dual) energy and enstrophy cascades in opposing directions. In scales larger than
the excitation scale, there is an inverse energy cascade with energy transfer from small
to large scales, while in scales smaller than the excitation scale, there is a
direct enstrophy cascade with enstrophy transfer from large to small scales
\parencite{kraichnanInertialRangesTwodimensional1967,boffettaTwoDimensionalTurbulence2012}.

\Gls{qg} turbulence, appropriate for a strongly stratified, rapidly
rotating atmosphere at synoptic scales, shares essential features with
two--dimensional turbulence. The conservation of potential enstrophy alongside
energy in the \gls{qg} framework gives rise to an analogous dual cascade structure
\parencite{charneyGeostrophicTurbulence1971}: above the energy injection scale
energy is transferred upscale, while potential enstrophy cascades downscale
below it. Unlike pure two--dimensional flow, however, \gls{qg} turbulence is not
forced externally at a single wavenumber but is instead driven internally by
baroclinic instability, which extracts available potential energy from the
mean meridional temperature gradient and converts it into eddy kinetic energy
near the Rossby deformation radius ($\sim 1000 km$)
\parencite{frederiksenUnifiedThreedimensionalInstability1982}. This injection
scale therefore acts as the effective excitation wavenumber for the
atmospheric cascade. The upscale energy transfer above the injection
scale does not, however, extend indefinitely: it is arrested roughly at the Rhines
scale ($\sim 2000-3000 km$), where, either through turbulence--Rossby wave interaction
\parencite{rhinesWavesTurbulenceBetaplane1975}
or frictional processes \parencite{sukorianskyArrestInverseEnergy2007}, energy preferentially accumulates
in zonal and quasi-stationary planetary structures rather than continuing
to cascade. Blocking is a synoptic--to--planetary scale
phenomenon residing in the upscale-transfer range, near the scale at which
this transfer is naturally arrested. Observations robustly
support upscale energy transfer in this range
\parencite{nastromKineticEnergySpectrum1984,
boerLargescaleTwodimensionalTurbulence1983}.

For instance, in a two-layer \gls{qg} model proposed by
\textcite{salmonTwolayerQuasigeostrophicTurbulence1978}, available potential
energy resulting from differential heating cascades downwards towards the
Rossby deformation scale, where it is converted by baroclinic instability to
kinetic energy in barotropic modes. That kinetic energy is then cascaded
upward again. The barotropization is significant: it implies that energy
injected by an inherently three-dimensional, baroclinic process is projected
onto vertically coherent, quasi-two-dimensional structures at larger scales,
where the dual-cascade arguments outlined above become applicable.

A complementary perspective is offered by
\textcite{herbertRestrictedEquilibriumEnergy2014}, who decompose
rotating--stratified Boussinesq dynamics into inertia--gravity wave modes and
slow (vortical/\gls{pv}-carrying) modes. They show that the direction of the
energy cascade depends on which subset of phase space is dynamically active:
when the statistics are restricted to the slow manifold, an inverse energy
transfer becomes admissible (a statistical mechanics analogue of
condensation), whereas a direct cascade develops in the unrestricted case.
The restricted case most naturally applies to balanced, large-scale dynamics
precisely the regime in which mature blocks reside, supporting the
view that the upscale transfer arguments above are appropriate for
synoptic-to-planetary scale phenomena, even though the underlying baroclinic
energy source is three-dimensional. The
condensation framing is particularly suggestive in light of the Rhines
arrest: blocks may be understood as a quasi-equilibrium accumulation of
upscale-transferred eddy energy at the scale where further upscale transfer
is dynamically suppressed, lending the structure both its large scale and
its persistence.

Thus, although blocking develops in a vertically sheared environment, its
mature phase often exhibits a pronounced equivalent-barotropic character,
rendering the dynamics locally more two-dimensional. This depends on the
specific flow condition, scale, and stratification, but it does not seem
far-fetched to understand part of a blocking's persistence as an effect of
energy transfer to larger scales sustained by baroclinic instability and the
associated eddy fluxes. This argument relies in part on the observation of
strong vertical coherence in blockings and the atmosphere appearing more
stratified (two-dimensional).

The upscale energy transfer encapsulates the existence of feedback mechanisms
described above in an abstract way (in spectral space rather than physical space).
In this view, baroclinic theories of blocking thus interpret persistence not as
a steady equilibrium, but as the large-scale manifestation of sustained, yet
principally intermittent, eddy fluxes and inverse energy transfer within a
stratified turbulent flow---some apply the theory directly 
\parencite{hansenComparisonSpectralEnergy1984, jensenUsingEnstrophyAdvection2014, tanakaNumericalSimulationAmplification1991, 
fournierAtmosphericEnergeticsWavelet2003, mullerUsingConceptDynamic2019}. 
Blocking may, hence, be interpreted as a barotropized manifestation of
baroclinically generated turbulence operating in a quasi-two-dimensional dynamical regime, 
though there is even evidence that such baroclinic energy conversions take place
within some blockings themselves, pointing out that the assumption of equivalent-barotropic
structure is not always justified \parencite{martineauBaroclinicBlocking2022}.

Baroclinic theories emphasizing the role of the vertical structure of the flow supporting
blocking formation have proposed a variety of abstracted models over
the years to investigate these processes mechanically  \parencite[e.g.~][]{vautardSourceMidlatitudeLowfrequency1988,
tanakaNumericalSimulationAmplification1991, cessiOrographicInstabilityNonsymmetric1985,
marshallDynamicalUnderstandingPlanetaryScale1993}. Some models are simple enough
to study the phenomenon from a dynamical systems perspective. For example, 
\textcite{charneyFormdragInstabilityMultiple1980} extend the aforementioned barotropic
model introduced by \textcite{charneyMultipleFlowEquilibria1979},
highlighting that the baroclinic instability is of first order importance in
bringing about the equilibrium states associated with blocking, whereas orography
becomes less important compared to the barotropic case. The notion that baroclinic
instability contributes a mechanism of strong growth instrumental for the rapid
onset and decay observed in real blockings is supported by such and more modern
stability analyses of the same model \parencite{schubertCovariantLyapunovVectors2015,schubertDynamicalAnalysisBlocking2016}. 

Consistent with the above, many studies have frequently highlighted the importance of
upstream disturbances (including cyclones) and wave-interactions
for blocking onset and decay. In a simple two-layer model, \textcite{luoPlanetaryscaleBaroclinicEnvelope2000}
observed that an upstream baroclinic eddy can bring a high-wavenumber Rossby wave
into phaselock. In this view, baroclinicity is important to bring about the necessary
amplification of a wave disturbance to reach a blocking configuration, and eventually
dissipate it again. Such conceptualisation is backed by observational evidence of
the equivalent barotropic blocking region being \enquote{enveloped} by baroclinically
active zones up- and downstream \parencite{frederiksenOnsetBlockingCyclogenesis1984, nakamuraObservedChangesBaroclinic1990}.
Similar insights have been obtained by budget and flux analyses of observations 
and (early) operational forecast models by \textcite{mullenLocalBalancesVorticity1986, mullenTransientEddyForcing1987}.
The precise location of upstream cyclonic disturbances play a critical role in
determining the blocking development and morphology. This is especially true for
cases involving particularly strong (\enquote{explosive}) cyclogenesis 
\parencite{colucciExplosiveCyclogenesisLargescale1985}.

\subsubsection{Diabatic processes} \label{sec:intro_block_diabatic}
Despite their success in explaining eddy amplification, upscale transfer and the maintenance
of large-scale anomalies, dry barotropic and baroclinic theories neglect the role of diabatic 
\gls{pv} modification associated with latent heat release, radiative and surface fluxes. 
These aspects were historically
overlooked probably due to difficulties in representing small-scale processes in models
and due to blockings being conceived as a large-scale phenomenon,
but also since blockings' characteristic large-scale subsidence typically suppresses convection.
Observational and modelling studies indicate that moist processes especially can substantially 
influence virtually all aspects of blockings with important implications for proper understanding
and prediction.

A central result of the recent literature is that latent heat release in clouds
is not merely a secondary modulation but can be of first-order importance for block
formation and maintenance as first drawn attention to by \textcite{croci-maspoliKeyDynamicalFeatures2009}.
The prominence of such effects
especially for blocks over the oceans leaves an imprint
on their climatology where a significant ascending motion it visible on the western
flank of the blocking high compared to subsidence in its center and eastern flank
\parencite{nabizadeh3DStructureNorthern2021}.
Based on Lagrangian trajectory analyses,
\textcite{pfahlImportanceLatentHeat2015} and \textcite{steinfeldRoleLatentHeating2019}
show that a substantial fraction of air masses
arriving in blocking anticyclones experienced strong latent heating prior to arrival,
consistent with cross-isentropic transport of low-\gls{pv} air in moist ascending airstreams.
Apart from the material transportation such air masses upward and (more importantly)
poleward, the upper-level divergent
outflow of such ascended air streams also contribute to the negative \gls{pv} anomaly, 
an effect that has been thoroughly analyzed both theoretically and observationally 
\parencite{saffinCirculationConservationOutflow2021,methvenPotentialVorticityWarm2015}.

These effects seem to be most important during onset and for more intense and larger
blocks, and are, naturally, often linked to upstream baroclinic developments---namely in
so-called \glspl{wcb} that transport warm, moist air from the warm sectors of surface cyclones
in the lower troposphere aloft. Since \glspl{wcb} are a central
topic of this thesis, they will be introduced in more detail later on. As a side note,
\textcite{zschenderleinLagrangianAnalysisUppertropospheric2020} observed that diabatically 
heated air parcels travelling into blocks may also have ascended convectively
rather than stratiformly through \glspl{wcb}, highlighting the uncertainty surrounding
these analyses. Organised convective processes are also thought to influence 
blocking dynamics climatologically through tropical teleconnections (see above).

The effect of diabatics on blocking development may be diagnosed elegantly
in quantative \gls{pv} frameworks. \textcite{neal2021PacificNorthwest2022}, for instance
applied \gls{lwa} theory for a particularly strong blocking episode and 
pointed to the critical importance of an upstream cyclone as a source of \gls{lwa}.
\textcite{liuBlockingDiversityCauses2025} applied it in a similar manner to find 
that diabatics may have opposing influences depending on the blocks morphology.

\textcite{hauserHolisticUnderstandingBlocked2023} and \textcite{hauserLifeCycleDynamics2024}
on the other hand combined
classical Eulerian and Lagrangian diagnostics with a quasi-Lagrangian quantative
\gls{pv} framework proposed by \textcite{teublerDynamicsRossbyWave2016} to show for blocks over Greenland
that diabatics are especially important at onset and maintanence, at a similar time
when quasi-barotropic \gls{pv} advection plays a prominent role as well.

The nature of these influences offers the unique opportunity to establish a more direct
cause-and-effect relationship than diagnostic correlations. In sensitivity experiments
with the \gls{ecmwf}s operational \gls{ifs}, \textcite{steinfeldSensitivityAtmosphericBlocking2020}
showed that when latent heating was selectively turned off, some blocks would 
become significantly weaker while others would not develop at all. Similarly, 
\textcite{deshmukhHowDoesDry2025} used a dry \gls{gcm} forced by realistic background
flow and found that while blockings still formed, their occurrence frequency was
reduced by roughly a third and their morphology severely altered.

Beyond latent heating aloft, lower-boundary diabatic forcing can precondition and
locally reinforce blocking highs. Using height-tendency diagnostics,
\textcite{tillyCalculatedHeightTendencies2008} demonstrate that surface sensible and
latent heat fluxes can contribute materially to height rises and thus to block
intensification, particularly during early development stages. On a climatological
level, it is well established that the Earth's surface tremendously shapes blocking
occurrence, not only through fluxes \parencite{heImpactLandSea2014}, but also through
friction and mountain torque \parencite{kallenBifurcationPropertiesQuasigeostrophic1982, metzWintertimeBlockingMountain1985}.

Basin-scale studies
emphasize that such surface forcing is not spatially uniform: \textcite{yamamotoOceanicMoistureSources2021}
identify key oceanic moisture source regions that feed latent-heating pathways associated
with Euro-Atlantic blocks, while \textcite{mathewsGulfStreamMoisture2024} show that
suppressing latent heat fluxes over the Gulf Stream alone significantly reduces  
blocking frequency, lifetime, intensity and spatial extent across the \gls{nh}. The explanation for
this is that turbulent heat fluxes over the Gulf Stream
and its extension generate boundary-layer low-\gls{pv} air masses that are subsequently
ventilated upward through \glspl{wcb} and contribute to the upper-level
negative-\gls{pv} anomaly of the block \parencite{mathewsOceanicMaintenanceAtmospheric2024}.
This mechanism seems to be dependent on \gls{sst} levels \parencite{narinesinghUniformSSTWarming2024} and
gradients \parencite{christSeaSkyUnderstanding2025} as well as model resolution, 
potentially mediated through underresolved 
extreme precipitation \parencite{strommenLinkGulfStream2025}. 

There is consequently growing attention to such small scale and moist processes
in the endavour to understand the biases in blocking representation in weather and
climate models \parencite{wandelWhyMoistDynamic2024, steinfeldResponseMoistDry2022, nabizadeh3DStructureNorthern2021}.
\textcite{dolores-tesillosRoleMoistDry2025} find blocking biases to be caused either by 
misrepresented \glspl{wcb} or through a biased mean flow. Remarkably, the pattern of 
that biased mean flow might be related to the notorious \enquote{too-zonal-too-equatorward} 
storm track bias in climate models, which, in turn, might also be linked to moist processes and their resolution
\parencite{auestadLatentHeatingFeedback2025, schemmEliminatingDecadesOld2023}.

Radiative cooling of anomalously low-pv air masses may play a role in the decay 
of a blocking, but the above studies suggest diabatic processes work more towards
counteracting the decay of a blocking. Even apart from latent heating, cloud radiative
effects upstream of a block may contribute to its maintenance \parencite{lubisCloudRadiativeEffects2025}.
Consequently, in a moist diabatic framework,
the decay of a block is linked more to the cessation of maintaining processes
and the eventual diffusive and advective dissipation of the anomalous air masses 
into the background flow consistent with eddy straining mechanisms considered as mediators
of scale interactions in turbulence theories \parencite{ditlevsenTurbulenceShellModels2010}.

\section{\Glspl{wcb}}\label{sec:intro_wcb}
 
\Glspl{wcb} are coherent, ascending airstreams that transport warm, moist air
from the warm sector of an extratropical cyclone in the lower troposphere
to the upper troposphere and lower stratosphere \parencite{schwenkRoleAscentTimescales2024}.
Its principle existence and effects
where present already in the classical theory of extratropical cyclones at the
polar front by \textcite{BjerknesSolberg1922}, but conceptualisation and dedicated
investigation took place later within the framework of the three-dimensional cyclone
conveyor-belt model \parencite[e.g.,~][]{browningStructureRainbandsMidlatitude1973},
which introduced them along with \glspl{ccb}, dry intrusions and (later) sting jets.

They were objects of interest originally due to their associated rain bands, but have
since also been recognized as important dynamical drivers of synoptic scale baroclinic dynamics
\parencite{madonnaWarmConveyorBelts2014a, binderRoleWarmConveyor2016} and, in effect,
the large-scale mid-latitude flow \parencite{vishnupriyaInteractionWarmConveyor2025, blanchardMidlevelConvectionWarm2021}.
Due to their considerable forcing and moisture content, \glspl{wcb} commonly account
for precipitation extremes in the extratropics on
daily timescales \parencite{pfahlWarmConveyorBelts2014}. They are a regular part
of extratropical cyclones in any time of year with a maximum occurrence in the 
cold season in either hemisphere. Their baroclinic nature makes them most prominent
in the storm tracks, most notably in the North Atlantic and Pacific sectors \parencite{madonnaWarmConveyorBelts2014a}.

\subsection{Definition} \label{sec:intro_wcb_def}
As mentioned above, \glspl{wcb} were traditionally identified subjectively 
by their associated stratiform cloud bands and vicinity to cyclones, but research
has developed more objective methods applicable to model or reanalysis data.
Nowadays, \glspl{wcb} are typically identified in a Lagrangian sense as traced air parcels that ascend
rapidly while undergoing substantial latent heating. A common criterion to select
\glspl{wcb} apart from vicinity to a cyclone is to require an ascent of at least 600 hPa
 within 2 days \parencite[e.g.,~][]{madonnaWarmConveyorBelts2014a}
which goes back to seminal work by \textcite{wernliLagrangianbasedAnalysisExtratropical1997} and
\textcite{wernliLagrangianbasedAnalysisExtratropical1997a}, though other thresholds and
variables have been used in the past, too \parencite{stohl1yearLagrangianClimatology2001,eckhardt15yearClimatologyWarm2004}.
Identifying \glspl{wcb}
from trajectories is, of course, only possible post-hoc, which presents a problem
since \glspl{wcb} are also of practical interest in weather forecasting. This is
why \textcite{quintingEuLerianIdentificationAscending2022a} have recently developed
a machine-learning based approach to identify \glspl{wcb} in an Eulerian framework.
Given relevant meteorological fields, their method can identify \glspl{wcb} in their
inflow, ascent and outflow phases with good accuracy, especially in regions of high
climatological \gls{wcb} occurrence when applied various datasets \parencite{quintingEuLerianIdentificationAscending2022}.

While the \gls{wcb} concept suggests a geometrically coherent airstream, this coherence
is not guaranteed by threshold-based selection of individual trajectories.
Moreover, such selections do not directly yield statements about the number, size,
location, or lifetime of distinct \glspl{wcb} in a given situation and the choice of 
thresholds is always somewhat arbitrary. This motivates a
complementary viewpoint that conceives of \glspl{wcb} as clusters of trajectories, 
i.e.~ensembles of air-parcel paths that remain mutually close judging by some metric
over a finite time window.

More generally, trajectory clustering has a long history in geoscience---in oceanography
in particular \parencite{vansebilleLagrangianOceanAnalysis2018}, but also in atmospheric sciences,
like in air-quality and (moisture) transport studies, where large sets of
trajectories are grouped into a small number of representative transport pathways
\parencite{stohlComputationAccuracyApplications1998,capeUseTrajectoryCluster2000}.
In these applications, clustering is often performed on trajectory positions at selected
times or along a limited portion of the path, which already acknowledges that the
geometry of trajectories can matter beyond instantaneous location, albeit often
with relatively simple similarity measures \parencite{stohlComputationAccuracyApplications1998}.

At the same time, trajectory clustering is a broad and evolving methodological field in its own right
\parencite{yuanReviewMovingObject2017,graserMobilityDLReviewDeep2025}, and atmospheric
applications increasingly adopt multivariate and time-windowed similarity notions that
better reflect physically meaningful airstream coherence. For instance,
\textcite{hartDetectionCoherentAirstreams2015} agglomeratively cluster trajectories using distances in a
standardized multivariate trajectory space over finite time windows, and post hoc interpretation of the resulting
clusters recovers \glspl{wcb} and \glspl{ccb}; related
methodology has also been applied in the context of heatwaves
\parencite{spensbergerDynamicsConcurrentSequential2020}. Though heuristically 
relatable, the approach falls short of presenting a reasonable justification for 
why different standardized variables should be weighted equally and by which
standardization. It also does not directly yield a measure of cluster quality,
instead relying on the within-group versus between group standard deviations across the
clusters as by \textcite{wernliLagrangianbasedAnalysisExtratropical1997}.

Conversely, in settings where the expected geometry is strongly constrained, trajectory
similarity can be tailored to the phenomenon, e.g.~via shape-based metrics such as
Fr\'echet distances (which measures distances under reparametrization of time) 
and rotational and translational invariances in the context of
tropical cyclones \parencite{kremerClusterAnalysisTailored2020} or by clustering 
trajectory segments to identify updrafts in hail storms \parencite{adams-selinThreedimensionalHailTrajectory2023}.
Such invariances are not directly applicable to \glspl{wcb}, but they highlight 
that trajectories may be clustered either by similarity in shape
or by time-local coherence. 

The latter notion invites a formalization of the visual notion of \enquote{trajectories staying
together} by concepts of Lagrangian coherence such as coherent sets or
Lagrangian coherent structures, which aim to identify time-evolving regions that resist
dispersion and mixing under small perturbations \parencite{hallerCoherentLagrangianVortices2013,hallerLagrangianCoherentStructures2015}.
In the coherent-set framework, robustness can be quantified via diffusion-based operators
estimated from trajectory data, whose spectral properties reveal coherent partitions
\parencite{banischUnderstandingGeometryTransport2017,froylandAnalyticFrameworkIdentifying2013,froylandDynamicIsoperimetryGeometry2015}.
A variety of related approaches to partition trajectory data into coherent sets or
transport barriers has been developed 
\parencite{froylandRoughandreadyClusterbasedApproach2015,allshouseLagrangianBasedMethods2015,
hadjighasemSpectralclusteringApproachLagrangian2016,schlueter-kuckCoherentStructureColouring2017,
padberg-gehleNetworkbasedStudyLagrangian2017,koltaiLargeDeviationsSemidistances2018,mowlaviDetectingLagrangianCoherent2022}.
This perspective is also highly adaptable, since it does not assume specific 
geometries and considers full trajectories rather than selected slices. 

Arguably,
spatio-temporal coherence is more appropriate for the identification of \glspl{wcb} 
than shape-based similarity, since trajectories of similar shape may belong to different 
\glspl{wcb}. The operator theoretic framework also provides a natural way to 
assess coherence through the robustness against small random perturbations.
Together, these developments suggest that complementing threshold-based \gls{wcb}
identification with bundle- or coherence-based trajectory partitioning can help address
questions of pathway commonality and of the spatio-temporal extent of distinct airstreams.

\subsection{Dynamics} \label{sec:intro_wcb_dynamics}
 During ascent, latent heat release strongly modifies the thermodynamic and
dynamical structure of the flow, leading to changes in potential vorticity and
the amplification or redistribution of upper-level wave features.
In particular, transport of air masses upward and poleward entails deposition of 
(negatively in the \gls{nh}) anomalous \gls{pv} near the tropopause, which, along with divergent upper-level
outflow can reinforce downstream ridges
and thereby influence the evolution of large-scale flow patterns. The material change of
latent heat release along a particular trajectory depends on the location of maximum
heating, but integrates roughly to zero \parencite{methvenPotentialVorticityWarm2015}. The effect of 
diabatic processes on the \gls{pv} structure of cyclones \parencite{chagnonDiabaticProcessesModifying2013}
and the jet stream \parencite{harveyDiabaticGenerationNegative2020} is well established
and supports the hypothesis of a first order importance for the large-scale flow. 
There exist considerable regional and case-to-case disparities in the interaction between \glspl{wcb} 
and the large-scale flow \parencite{vishnupriyaInteractionWarmConveyor2025}, complicating
the establishment of a general theory for the phenomenon. For instance,
the \glspl{wcb} outflow often splits into multiple branches, with a anticyclonically
turning branch associated with the strongest upper-level \gls{pv} anomalies and 
according downstream ridge amplification \parencite{heitmannWarmConveyorBelt2024}.

Because they link boundary-layer moisture reservoirs with the upper-tropospheric
waveguide, WCBs play a central role in mediating interactions between synoptic-scale
baroclinic development and the planetary-scale circulation. Accordingly, the intensification
phase of a surface cyclones often coincides with the strongest ascent phase
of a \gls{wcb} \parencite{heitmannWarmConveyorBelt2024} and the intensification of a cyclone
determines the amount of air masses involved in such a conveyor belt \parencite{binderRoleWarmConveyor2016}. 
This is due to an implied positive feedback loop between the cyclogenesis and 
the latent heat release, enough moisture provided. In addition, the amount
of latent heating in a \gls{wcb} determines the strength of the ascent and, in turn,
the speed and outflow level rather than leaving an imprint on the material change
in \gls{pv} across the ascent directly \parencite{methvenPotentialVorticityWarm2015}.
This implies that \glspl{wcb} and their large-scale effect is highly sensitive
to the (small-scale) variables governing ascent. \textcite{schaflerImpactInflowMoisture2015}
observed that the correct measurement or assimilation of moisture upstream of the WCB considerably
influences the forecasted effect of a WCB event especially with respect to tropopause and jet stream dynamics.
Though associated with stratiform ascent in the classical cyclone theory, there is
also evidence for embedded mid-level convection within \glspl{wcb} which alters 
the \gls{pv} structure locally modifying the large scale flow downstream
\parencite{oertelConvectiveActivityExtratropical2019, oertelPotentialVorticityStructure2020}.
Notably, \textcite{blanchardMidlevelConvectionWarm2021} point to intensifying effects on the jet stream,
though the effects of embedded convection in \glspl{wcb} seem to play a secondary
role climatologically due to the rarity of its occurrence
\parencite{oertelInteractionMicrophysicsDynamics2023}.

\section{Predictability, uncertainty and error-growth}\label{sec:intro_predictability}
% Error growth, flow dependence, trajectory vs statistical predictability (short).
% Initial conditions vs model error; forecast vs analysis uncertainty (short).
% (Optional: table taxonomy of uncertainty sources.)
% Why stochastic physics exists; documented circulation/regime impacts (high-level).

Predicting the weather and climate of the future is certainly one of the core goals of
atmospheric sciences as a whole, so it comes as no surprise that the study of 
predictability is wide and deep \parencite{krishnamurthyPredictabilityWeatherClimate2019}.
 The atmosphere is a chaotic system with rich
dynamics on a vast range of scales generally rendering definitive forecasts
impossible beyond a certain time horizon, often referred to as the predictability limit.
From a forecasting perspective, errors and, thus,
predictability limits may be decomposed into two main sources:
uncertainties in the initial conditions due to imperfect observations and data
assimilation on the one hand and model uncertainty due to under- or unresolved
processes and model biases on the other hand. 
The differentiation is somewhat arbitrary since model errors -- 
through data assimilation --  also
influence the initial conditions, which in turn modify the error growth 
characteristics of the system. Nevertheless it provides a
paradigm for diagnosing error sources. Crucially, both aspects of predictability
are dependent on the flow state, region, variable and lead time.

From a practical point of view, predictability is usually studied
by means of ensemble forecasting, where the evolution of the spread between
multiple realizations of a forecast serves as a proxy for the forecast uncertainty.
Acknowledging deficient
knowledge about the system by providing probabilistic
in addition to deterministic forecasts has successfully extended the forecast
skill horizon \parencite{buizzaForecastSkillHorizon2015} and has proven instrumental
in guiding decision-making as well as model improvement.

Mathematically, one can think of ensemble forecasting
 as a Monte Carlo approximation of the temporal
evolution of the probability distribution in the system's phase space. A subtle
distinction has to be made between the growth of the ensemble spread (the width
of the probability distribution) and the growth
of the forecast error (the distance between the ensemble mean and the truth), since the
two only coincide in reliable ensembles \parencite{leutbecherEnsembleForecasting2008}.
This might not always be the case, but reliability is routinely assessed in operational
models and ensembles are consequently calibrated \parencite{rodwellReliabilityEnsembleData2016}. 

The two aspects of uncertainty mentioned above are distinctly present in an
ensemble forecasting setting:
the initial spread is either derived from assimilation uncertainty (like in an
operational setting) or designed in some dedicated way with a specific 
purpose in mind \parencite[ch.~6]{kalnayEarthSystemModeling2024}.
Model uncertainty is often represented by stochastically perturbed physics
\parencite{palmerStochasticWeatherClimate2019, christensenParametrizationWeatherClimate2022}.

\subsection{Chaos and initial conditions} \label{sec:intro_predictability_chaos}

One can generally distinguish between intrinsic and practical predictability 
\parencite{sunIntrinsicPracticalLimits2016, selzTransitionPracticalIntrinsic2022}.
The former is a property of the system itself and describes the time it takes for
infinitesimally small perturbations to grow to a size comparable to the natural
variability of the system. The \enquote{system} here generally refers to a 
\gls{nwp} model, but may also be thought of as the 
real atmosphere (the two coinciding under the so-called 
\enquote{perfect model assumption}).
The practical predictability limit, on the other hand, assumes realistic initial 
condition uncertainty and is therefore always shorter than the intrinsic predictability.

With the current generation of \gls{nwp} models and assimilation and observation systems,
the gap between the two is estimated at around 3.5 to 5 days for the mid-latitudes
\parencite{selzTransitionPracticalIntrinsic2022,zhangWhatPredictabilityLimit2019}.
An even greater gap is observed in the tropics, where \textcite{judtAtmosphericPredictabilityTropics2020}
found evidence for a longer intrinsic predictability horizon compared to the
mid-latitudes. These findings suggest that there is still ample room for improving 
forecast skill by reducing initial condition uncertainty. Moreover, differences in
predictability between locations might be indicative of non-homogeneous
impacts of initial perturbations or differences in the error--growth characteristics.

The notion of a predictability limit intrinsic to a dynamical system goes back
to the concept of the butterfly effect, which describes the 
exponential growth of small perturbations in a chaotic system and, therefore,
its sensitive dependence on initial conditions \parencite{lorenzDeterministicNonperiodicFlow1963}.
What is even more important is that there is an analogous effect that acts across
spatial scales. This is a crucial insight, because it implies that small--scale
errors in the initial conditions can scale upwards \enquote{polluting} the 
large scales and thus limiting predictability 
\parencite{lorenzPredictabilityFlowWhich1969,palmerRealButterflyEffect2014}.
In this view, it does not really matter, what the precise source and nature
of the initial perturbations are, since scale interaction in the turbulent system
will spread the error across scales and variables quickly.

The predictability implications of this cross-scale error growth depend
critically on the shape of the kinetic energy spectrum (i.e.~the curve of 
$E_{\mathrm{kin}}(k)$ vs. $k$), which is directly linked
to the direction and nature of energy and enstrophy cascades already mentioned
in \cref{sec:intro_block_baroclinic}. In a $E_{\mathrm{kin}} \sim k^{-3}$ range,
the characteristic error-doubling time is scale-independent, and the
predictability horizon is in principle indefinitely extendable by reducing
initial-condition uncertainty. In a $E_{\mathrm{kin}} \sim k^{-5/3}$
range, by contrast, error-doubling times decrease with scale, and the total
time required for errors to propagate from arbitrarily small scales up to a
given large scale is finite and bounded
\parencite{leithPredictabilityTurbulentFlows1972, sunNewTheoreticalFramework2020}.
The mid-latitude atmosphere exhibits
both regimes: a $-3$ slope at synoptic scales and a $-5/3$ slope at meso- and submesoscales, with
the transition occurring around a few hundred kilometres. In contrast to 
idealized two-dimensional turbulence, where the transition between the ranges
is determined by the excitation scale \parencite{kraichnanInertialRangesTwodimensional1967},
in \gls{qg} turbulence, the transition is determined by the validity of the 
\gls{qg} approximation, which is at scales smaller than the baroclinic excitation
scales \parencite{charneyGeostrophicTurbulence1971, lindborgCanAtmosphericKinetic1999}.

This dual
structure is well-established by both aircraft measurements
\parencite{nastromKineticEnergySpectrum1984, lindborgCanAtmosphericKinetic1999}
and high-resolution simulations
\parencite{skamarockAtmosphericKineticEnergy2014,
liExploringDifferencesAtmospheric2025}. The dynamical origin of the
mesoscale $-5/3$ range -- below synoptic, but above smaller scales, where three--dimensional
turbulence becomes the appropriate assumption -- remains debated 
\parencite{lindborgEnergyCascadeStrongly2006, gageEvidenceForK51979, dewanSaturatedcascadeSimilitudeTheory1997},
but its existence implies that the atmosphere has a finite, intrinsic predictability
horizon regardless of how well the initial state is observed. Errors
injected at arbitrarily small scales will cascade upwards through the
$k^{-5/3}$ range and eventually reach the synoptic scales relevant for
blocking and other large-scale phenomena.

In physical space, this error cascade is often conceptualized in a three--stage
process: 
\begin{enumerate}
    \item Initial error growth at small scales, dominated by moist convective
instability
    \item Escalation of the errors to larger scales either through geostrophic 
adjustment \parencite{zhangMesoscalePredictabilityMoist2007, bierdelTheoreticalAspectsUpscale2018} or through near--tropopause
divergence \parencite{baumgartQuantitativeViewProcesses2019}
    \item Propagation of the error to global scales through baroclinic instability
\parencite{zhangMesoscalePredictabilityMoist2007} or
non--linear \gls{pv} advection \parencite{baumgartQuantitativeViewProcesses2019} 
\end{enumerate}
Several studies with grid--scale initial perturbations have provided evidence
in favour of such a process chain \parencite{judtInsightsAtmosphericPredictability2018,
selzEstimatingIntrinsicLimit2019, lloverasExploringIntrinsicPredictability2025}. In this conception, moist convection
appears as an energy source that escalates and injects perturbations at small 
scales more effectively than the dry turbulent scale interactions would.

Moist convection may however also have an effect on the shape of the kinetic energy
spectrum and the associated cascades as well. In dry-versus-moist
experiments with the idealized baroclinic wave model by 
\textcite{zhangMesoscalePredictabilityMoist2007}, removing moist processes both eliminates the
mesoscale $k^{-5/3}$ portion of the kinetic energy spectrum and, consistently,
removes the intrinsic predictability limit, with forecast error then scaling
linearly with initial-condition error rather than saturating. The two
effects are linked --- moist convection breaks the \gls{qg} approximation, thus
establishing the mesoscale $-5/3$ range, and it is precisely this range
that enables the cross-scale error cascade described above
\parencite{sunIntrinsicPracticalLimits2016}. Though other mechanisms, including stratified
turbulence and gravity-wave breaking, can also produce a mesoscale $-5/3$
slope in dry simulations at sufficiently high resolution
\parencite{waiteMesoscaleKineticEnergy2009, skamarockAtmosphericKineticEnergy2014},
the moist convection's influence on the kinetic energy spectrum is evidenced further
by a mostly $-5/3$ slope in the tropics and a mostly $-3$ slope in the polar regions observed
by \textcite{judtAtmosphericPredictabilityTropics2020}. Both the tropics and the
polar regions have much different predictability characteristics compared to the
mid-latitudes, indeed, with the tropics showing a much more limited short-term
predictability, but higher long-term predictability due to large scale teleconnections
and oceanic coupling \parencite{diasEquatorialWavesSkill2018}, whereas the polar regions
might have a higher intrinsic predictability due to the secondary role of 
moist convection and its limited influence on the large scale flow
\parencite{lloverasExploringIntrinsicPredictability2025}.
Be it through injection of perturbations or modification of the cascades themselves,
moist convection is therefore thought to be a key driver of the intrinsic
predictability limit.

For initial perturbations of realistic size and spectrum, however, convection
turned out to be far less relevant for error growth both in operational
 \parencite{lloverasTwoFourdayPredictability2023} and idealized models \parencite{sunIntrinsicPracticalLimits2016}.
Indeed, when initial perturbations were successively increased from minuscule 
to operational size in global model runs by \textcite{selzTransitionPracticalIntrinsic2022},
ensemble spread growth was dominantly in the large scales with spread even decreasing
in the small scales for short lead times. In the simplest 
 barotropic \gls{qg} model adjacent to the \textcite{lorenzPredictabilityFlowWhich1969}
 model, upscale error-growth and, accordingly, finite-time predictability
 appears only at resolutions comparable to a few kilometers for global models, even
 though the $-5/3$ range is well resolved at coarser resolutions already.
Further evidence of the irrelevance of the rapid upscale error growth from
grid scales is provided by stricter predictability limits on short time
scales in the tropics compared to mid-latitudes even judging
from machine-learning based forecast models \parencite{keaneMidlatitudeTropicalScales2025},
that do not resolve the rapid upscale error growth from grid scales \parencite{selzCanArtificialIntelligencebased2023}.

These observations are actually completely
consistent with the kinetic energy spectrum of the mid--latitude
atmosphere and the associated
cascades, since relatively small errors at the large scales may
dominate the spread before the small--scale errors have escalated
\parencite{rotunnoGeneralizationLorenzsModel2008, durranAtmosphericPredictabilityWhy2014}. 
Consistent with this,
cyclogenesis case studies identify baroclinic rather than convective
instability as the main driver of spread near the tropopause
\parencite{rodwellUncertaintyGrowthForecast2023}, a conclusion supported
by process-based analyses
\parencite{baumgartPotentialVorticityDynamics2018, baumgartProcessesGoverningAmplification2019}
that also point to wave interactions.

Yet, convectively active regions are frequently highlighted in studies 
concerned with error growth that utilize operational
ensemble forecasts initialized with assimilation uncertainty
\parencite{sanchezLinkingRapidForecast2020,
puhFlowDependenceForecast2024, lilloInvestigatingDynamicsError2017,
magnussonTropicalCycloneActivities2021}. For global models,
\textcite{zagarBalancePropertiesShortrange2013, zagarThreedimensionalMultivariateModal2015, zagarGlobalPerspectiveLimits2017}
showed that initial spread was largest in unbalanced modes in the tropics associated 
with convection, before balanced modes and midlatitudes become dominant -- 
the tropics show a much more limited short forecast skill compared to the mid-latitudes
generally \parencite{diasEquatorialWavesSkill2018}.  

A hypothesis
that reconciles the two findings is that convective regions do also have an
impact on the initial ensemble spread, potentially shortening the predictability
limit regardless of the kinetic energy spectrum. Even though convection typically
acts on small scales compared to the large barotropic
scales dominating the forecast evolution, they are also localized in physical
space and therefore broad in spectral space rather than localized at small
wavenumbers \parencite{lloverasMesoscalePredictabilityMoist2022}.

The elevated spread in convectively active regions may
reflect enhanced initial uncertainty due to weaker observational
constraints \parencite{rodwellCharacteristicsOccasionalPoor2013}, or be 
produced during the assimilation process itself. An argument in favour of 
a substantial observational contribution is the lack of strong congruency between
regions of large background forecast spread and regions high precipitation
\parencite{rodwellUncertaintyGrowthForecast2023}. Similarly, 
\textcite{cardinaliRepresentingModelError2014} found that the elevated initial
condition uncertainty in unbalanced modes was largely present in data assimilation
runs that did not account for model error. The inclusion of which, in turn,
increased the spread by roughly 10-25 \% depending on the region and this spread
was in both balanced and unbalanced modes.
This is consistent with the fact that observation uncertainty is indeed larger
 in cloudy scenes for satellite
instruments \parencite{geerObservationErrorsAllsky2011,
liAssimilationSatelliteSounder2016, geerAllskySatelliteData2018, 
bormannEstimatesObservationerrorCharacteristics2011},
aircraft typically avoid regions of intense convection, but large
variability on small scales together with representation challenges in
models also further complicate the assimilation. In particular, microwave 
measurements have been shown to have less information content, but still improve
 forecast error more in cloudy scenes compared to clear-sky conditions, 
 proving that observation and model uncertainty can go along to 
 inflate initial uncertainty \parencite{cardinaliPerformanceMeasurementAdvanced2011}.

like when aircraft and station measurements are impeded by a pandemic \parencite{inglebyImpactCOVID19Weather2021, ImpactsCOVID19Restrictions2020} or geopolitical issues lead to jamming of radio occultation measurements \parencite{wuGNSSSignalJamming2024} or reduced data sharing \parencite{GlobalRadiosondeNetwork2016}. , though incident tropical cyclones or intense thunderstorms also prompt dedicated dropsonde or aircraft measurements \parencite{petersenImpactFutureBenefits2016}.

More broadly, initial condition uncertainty has been shown to be of first--order
importance for operational forecast error--growth, roughly on-par with model
formulation \parencite{magnussonDependenceInitialConditions2019}. It is shaped 
by both observational and methodological roots.
Observation coverage is highly inhomogeneous: dense
in regions of routine aircraft traffic and over instrumented landmasses,
sparse over oceans, the polar regions, and the tropics
\parencite{sociERA5GlobalReanalysis2024}. 
Satellite observations have
substantially mitigated this inhomogeneity over the past three decades,
to the point that they now provide the dominant share of information
content in modern global analyses, particularly through hyperspectral
microwave and infrared sounders
\parencite{bauerQuietRevolutionNumerical2015}. For example,
\textcite{cardinaliObservationInfluenceDiagnostic2013} showed that satellite observations
accounted for roughly 80\% of the observational influence on the analysis in 
\gls{ecmwf}'s operational system at that time with the remaining 20\% coming from
surface based observations (including aircraft).
Apart from coverage, however,
the quality of observations also varies in space and time -- not only
the cloud-related issues mentioned above, but also through instrument-specific
biases, representativeness errors, and the difficulty of assimilating
spatially or temporally correlated errors, which often forces practitioners
to thin observations or inflate prescribed error variances
\parencite{janjicRepresentationErrorData2018}. 

The assimilation
process itself introduces further uncertainty through approximate
background-error covariance modelling, limited ensemble sizes in ensemble-based
schemes, and the use of linearized or approximate observation operators
\parencite{houtekamerReviewEnsembleKalman2016, bannisterReviewOperationalMethods2017, kalnayEarthSystemModeling2024}.
Modern ensemble and hybrid
schemes attempt to mitigate this by using flow-dependent background-error
covariances estimated from short-range ensemble forecasts, but the resulting
covariances are themselves subject to sampling error from limited ensemble
sizes and require empirical inflation and localization
\parencite{houtekamerReviewEnsembleKalman2016}.

These uncertainty sources do not act
uniformly across scales or flow regimes: analysis errors tend to be larger
where the flow is dynamically active and observations are weak, producing
flow-dependent initial spread that subsequently shapes error growth. This, again, 
goes especially for small scale convective phenomena that are both hard to observe,
highly variable and exhibit strong error-growth. In fact, even dedicated 
high-effort assimilation experiments using observations much denser than operational
show serious levels of error for storm cells that dominantly limit their 
predictability \parencite{sodhiLimitsPracticalPredictability2025}.
A particular challenge in operational ensemble forecasting is how to
construct the initial ensemble spread itself. Since the analysis distribution
is not directly available, initial perturbations must be approximated
through dedicated schemes, and the chosen approach has a substantial
influence on the resulting forecast spread evolution and skill
\parencite{leutbecherEnsembleForecasting2008}. The importance of this
construction step is hard to overstate: because initial-condition
uncertainty is itself flow-dependent and case-contingent on the large-scale
state, perturbations that fail to reflect the structure of actual analysis
uncertainty propagate as effectively random noise through the forecast
model, inflating member error without contributing predictive information
about the realised error pattern \parencite{fengEnsembleForecastingForay2024}.
Conversely, perturbations whose covariance structure is physically tied to
the assimilation cycle inherit flow-dependence from the underlying observation
network and dynamics, and can therefore represent uncertainty in a way that
is consistent with how analysis error actually arises.

Historically, operational
centres adopted contrasting strategies: ECMWF used singular vectors ---
perturbations that grow most rapidly over a fixed optimisation interval,
designed to capture the dynamically most uncertain directions in phase
space \parencite{buizzaTargetingObservationsUsing1999, palmerSingularVectorsPredictability2013} ---
while NCEP and the Canadian centre used breeding and ensemble-transform
methods that cycle perturbations through the assimilation system to align
them with the natural error structure \parencite{tothEnsembleForecastingNCEP1997}.
Both approaches have known limitations: singular vectors emphasise
fast-growing modes and may under-represent uncertainty in slowly evolving
or geographically remote regions, while breeding-based perturbations can
be too closely aligned with leading instabilities and miss other relevant
directions \parencite{magnussonComparisonSingularVectors2008}. Modern
operational systems increasingly draw perturbations from ensembles of data
assimilations (EDA), in which the full assimilation cycle is run multiple
times with perturbed observations and boundary conditions to directly
sample the analysis error distribution
\parencite{isaksenEnsembleDataAssimilations2010}. This provides
flow-dependent, broadband perturbations more consistent with the
assimilation uncertainty itself, addressing the limitations of singular
vectors and breeding by grounding the perturbation structure in the same
stochastic processes that generate analysis error in the first place,
though the design choice continues to
affect ensemble reliability and spread-skill relationships and remains an
active area of research
\parencite{langImpactPerturbationMethods2012,
raynaudComparisonInitialPerturbation2016,
wangImpactsMultiscaleComponents2023}.

The implication for the predictability arguments of the preceding paragraphs
is that initial-condition uncertainty is not a homogeneous, small-scale
\enquote{seed} that grows according to spectral theory alone. Realistic analysis
errors are flow-dependent, projected preferentially onto dynamically active
structures, and span a broad range of scales
\parencite{magnussonFactorsInfluencingSkill2013, zhangWhatPredictabilityLimit2019}.
Consequently, the error growth observed in operational ensembles reflects an
interaction between intrinsic cascade dynamics and the structure of the
initial spread itself, with the latter often dominating at short
forecast lead times \parencite{selzTransitionPracticalIntrinsic2022,
lloverasTwoFourdayPredictability2023}. Reducing initial-condition
uncertainty --- especially at the large scales where most spread initially
resides --- therefore remains a major lever for improving practical
predictability, even where intrinsic limits constrain its eventual horizon.

\subsection{Parametrizations and model error} \label{sec:intro_predictability_parametrization} 
While the previous section focused on initial-condition uncertainty,
forecast error in operational systems is comparably shaped by model
uncertainty. Even given a perfect initial state, a numerical model would
diverge from the true atmosphere due to its structural and numerical
limitations: finite spatial and temporal discretization, imperfect dynamical
cores, and --- most prominently --- the parametrization of processes that
occur on scales smaller than the model grid. Diagnostic studies at
operational centres find that model and initial-condition uncertainty
contribute to medium-range forecast error in comparable proportions, with
their relative balance shifting with lead time, scale, and variable
\parencite{magnussonFactorsInfluencingSkill2013, magnussonDependenceInitialConditions2019, magnussonSkillMediumrangeForecast2022}.

The scale separation between the resolved grid and the processes governing
atmospheric thermodynamics is enormous. Even at convection-permitting
resolutions ($\sim 1$~km), cloud microphysics, turbulence, radiative transfer,
and boundary-layer mixing operate down to micrometre scales and cannot be
explicitly resolved. Parametrizations bridge this gap by expressing
sub-grid processes as functions of resolved-scale variables,
encoding statistical assumptions about the distribution of sub-grid states
consistent with each grid-scale configuration
\parencite{stensrudParameterizationSchemesKeys2007, christensenParametrizationWeatherClimate2022}.
 These schemes are indispensable but inherently approximate; they represent the
 primary
source of structural model error in modern \gls{nwp} \parencite{bernerStochasticParameterizationNew2017}.

A fundamental limitation of conventional parametrizations is that they are
deterministic: the same grid-scale state produces the same sub-grid
forcing. In reality, the sub-grid response to a given grid-scale
configuration is better described by a distribution of plausible
tendencies, since the same grid-scale state is consistent with many
realisations of the unresolved flow and because (often empirically) derived or tuned
parameters in the functional formulations are associated with uncertainties themselves.
In the case of sub-grid turbulence, such parametrization
violates essential self-similarity properties of the turbulent flow with important
detrimental implications for the model dynamics
\parencite{palmerNonlinearDynamicalPerspective2001, palmerStochasticParametrizationModel2009, palmerStochasticWeatherClimate2019}.

Ensemble forecasts that perturb only the initial
conditions are systematically underdispersed (unreliable), particularly at short lead
times and in convectively active regions, because they fail to sample
model-related uncertainty
\parencite{buizzaStochasticRepresentationModel1999}. An ensemble
forecast that is underdispersed at the outset can stay underdispersed for 
the entire forecast horizon highlighting again the importance of correct
initial sampling. Apart from ensemble forecasting
misrepresenting model uncertainty is also a problem for ensemble-based
data assimilation, where lack of 
model uncertainty leads to overconfidence in the background forecast and,
therefore, underestimation of the analysis error and suboptimal assimilation
of observations \parencite{cardinaliRepresentingModelError2014}. 

Convection is
particularly problematic, as its statistical behaviour transitions across
the so-called convective grey zone --- grid spacings where convection is
neither fully sub-grid nor fully resolved --- so that parametrizations
calibrated at one resolution behave inconsistently at another 
\parencite{bechtoldRoleShallowConvection2014, fieldExploringConvectiveGrey2017,
tomassiniConfrontingConvectiveGray2023, krollParameterizationAdaptionNeeded2025}.
Depending on the model resolution this issue arises, for example, for tropical cyclones 
\parencite{langImpactPerturbationMethods2012} or mesoscale
convective systems \parencite{clarkeDownstreamInfluenceMesoscale2019}, either
having considerable impacts on the larger-scale flow and objects of societal importance.
The issue 
arises in a similar manner for boundary--layer parametrizations 
\parencite{honnertAtmosphericBoundaryLayer2020}.

An operational response to these limitations is stochastic
parametrization, which represents model uncertainty by adding random
perturbations to the deterministic parametrization scheme. A particular approach
to represent uncertainty in the physics parametrization is the \gls{sppt}
developed at the \gls{ecmwf}
\parencite{buizzaStochasticRepresentationModel1999,
leutbecherStochasticRepresentationsModel2017}. It applies a multiplicative
scaling to the total physics tendency, which is spatially and temporally correlated,
thereby \enquote{giving life} back to the sub-grid processes rather than 
treating them as \enquote{slaves} of the resolved flow \parencite{palmerStochasticWeatherClimate2019}.
Its successor, the \gls{spp} scheme \parencite{ollinahoProcesslevelRepresentationModel2017,
langRevisionStochasticallyPerturbed2021} instead perturbs the uncertain parameters
themselves allowing for more granular control over the impact of individual
processes. The recovery of the self-similarity broken by determinist parametrization of
subgrid-scale turbulence is the motivation of the \gls{skeb} scheme, that 
compensates excessive dissipation of kinetic energy through mountain drag,
numerical diffusion and other processes \parencite{shuttsKineticEnergyBackscatter2005,bernerSpectralStochasticKinetic2009}.
Its introduction accordingly lead to the representation of the observed
$-5/3$ slope in the kinetic energy spectrum in the mesoscales \parencite{palmerRepresentingModelUncertainty2005}.

The \gls{sppt} usually has a larger influence than the \gls{skeb}, especially
in the tropics and near the surface
\parencite{subramanianImpactStochasticPhysics2017, christensenStochasticParameterizationNino2017,
strommenImpactStochasticParametrisations2018}, whereas the \gls{skeb} has a more pronounced
effect in the free troposphere and in the storm track region
\parencite{weisheimerAddressingModelError2014, bernerStochasticParameterizationNew2017}.
For identical initial conditions, \gls{sppt} generates  medium-range 
ensemble spread comparable to multi-model ensembles 
\parencite{magnussonSkillMediumrangeForecast2022}, which are considered the
skillful sample of the model uncertainty space given their fundamental differences
in model design \parencite{palmerRepresentingModelUncertainty2005, parkTIGGEPreliminaryResults2008, krishnamurtiReviewMultimodelSuperensemble2016}.

Generally, stochastic parametrization schemes have been shown to partly alleviate the
shortcomings of deterministic parametrization, improving model biases,
ensemble reliability and spread-skill relationships
 \parencite{leutbecherStochasticRepresentationsModel2017, langRevisionStochasticallyPerturbed2021}.
Stochasticity can also improve the representation of weather regime characteristics
in a model \parencite{bernerSystematicModelError2012, dawsonSimulatingWeatherRegimes2015}.
On a conceptual level, stochasticity can
contribute significantly to the exploration of a system's state space and
the misrepresentation or negligence of which can, in turn, severely alter the
the occurrence and transition frequencies between metastable regions of the state
space. For \gls{nwp} models,
this notion translates to weather regime statistics, whose occurrence and transitions
are governed by synoptic forcing and the \enquote{noisy} influence of 
sub-grid scale processes\parencite{molteniRegimesWintertimeCirculation1990}.
Frequently, this interaction occurs via tropical teleconnections preconditioning
weather regime behaviour on seasonal timescales 
\parencite{jungInfluenceStochasticParameterization2005}. Consistent with 
a changing exploration of the system's attractor rather than a changing attractor
structure itself, the regimes themselves might not change \parencite{weisheimerAddressingModelError2014}.
The influence of stochastic parametrization
on the representation of blockings as one kind of weather regimes will be discussed
in more detail in the next section.   

As epitomized by the location-specific effect of the above presented 
stochastic schemes, predictability is severely flow-dependent. Error growth rates,
the dominant error
source, and the resulting forecast uncertainty all vary substantially with
the synoptic regime, geographical region, and weather system under
consideration \parencite{magnussonFactorsInfluencingSkill2013,ardilouzeFlowDependenceWintertime2021,
rodwellCharacteristicsOccasionalPoor2013, ferrantiFlowdependentVerificationECMWF2015}.
This flow-dependence has multiple origins, several of which have already
been mentioned in the preceding sections. Initial-condition errors scale with 
the observational coverage and quality and, in turn, project
preferentially onto dynamically unstable structures, so the same nominal
analysis uncertainty produces different forecast spread depending on the
underlying flow \parencite{buizzaTargetingObservationsUsing1999}.
Parametrized processes are themselves activated unevenly: convection,
gravity-wave drag, and boundary-layer mixing contribute to model
uncertainty in ways that depend on whether the local atmosphere is
convectively active, stably stratified, or in a quiescent regime, so that
stochastic parametrizations also produce flow-dependent spread
\parencite{christensenParametrizationWeatherClimate2022}. The weather
systems themselves may differ in their intrinsic propensity to amplify disturbances:
 rapidly
intensifying cyclones, warm conveyor belts, and convective episodes are
typically associated with larger relative forecast errors than slowly
evolving large-scale features
\parencite{ferrantiFlowdependentVerificationECMWF2015,
gramsAtmosphericDynamicsPerspective2018, clarkeDownstreamInfluenceMesoscale2019}. The large scale flow constrains the 
convective scales to differing degrees implying differing levels of predictability
and parametrizations have been shown to aid in representing this flow-dependence
in models \parencite{keilRelativeContributionSoil2019, matsunobuFlowScaledependentSpatial2024}

The two features of synoptic variability central for the work presented in this
thesis are both associated with very particular properties and behaviour in 
terms of forecasting and predictability and have accordingly attracted a 
great deal of attention by the scientific community. The results of which will be
presented in the following.

\subsection{Predictability of blockings} \label{sec:intro_predictability_blockings}
Atmospheric blocking events are frequently discussed in the context of operational
predictability on subseasonal timescales in particular.
On the one hand, this is due to their extraordinary impact on
natural and societal systems outlined in \cref{sec:intro_block_Phenomenology}.
On the other hand, \gls{nwp} models
have historically struggled to accurately forecast blocking events, especially
their onset and decay \parencite{dandreaNorthernHemisphereAtmospheric1998,
mauritsenBlockingPredictionEnsemble2004, quandtForecastVariabilityBlocking2017,
matsuedaBlockingPredictabilityOperational2009,tibaldiOperationalPredictabilityBlocking1990}
whereas medium-range foreast skill is sometimes enhanced during blocking episodes
(\cite{spaethFlowDependenceEnsembleSpread2024}; cf.~also \cite{prestel-kupfererPredictabilityMidlatitudeRossbywave2024}
for a similar behaviour for \glspl{rwp}). As discussed in \cref{sec:intro_blocking_theories},
 the onset  and decay phases
of blockings are notoriously rich in processes on a wide range of scales, especially
with respect to upstream development like cyclogenesis, which projects onto its 
predictability \parencite{maddisonUpstreamCycloneInfluence2019}. 

Given the long history research into the dynamical nature of blockings, it comes
as no surprise that its predictability has also been studied using some of the
abstracted low-order models introduced in \cref{sec:intro_blocking_theories}.
From a mathematical perspective, the propensity of a dynamical system to 
escalate minuscule perturbation is measured by Lyapunov exponents, Lyapunov vectors
and their finite-time counterparts \parencite{youngMathematicalTheoryLyapunov2013,
ginelliCovariantLyapunovVectors2013}.
The accompanying theory has been applied to a wide variety of low-order models
\parencite{decruzExploringLyapunovInstability2018, vannitsemStatisticalDynamicalProperties2016,
vannitsemPredictabilityLargescaleAtmospheric2017, schubertCovariantLyapunovVectors2015,
viennetGuidelinesDatadrivenApproaches2022, yodenFiniteTimeLyapunovStability1993}
or real-world data \parencite{blachutTaleTwoVortices2020a,
rypinaApplyingDynamicalSystems2022} and has entered operational forecasting in the form of
singular vector perturbations that inflate initial perturbations in the most
unstable directions of the flow \parencite{buizzaTargetingObservationsUsing1999, palmerSingularVectorsPredictability2013}.

The theory has also seen application to blocking in increasingly complex models. 
To the extent that such abstracted insights into the structural stability of 
the attractor of a dynamical system can be related to the predictability of a real
world phenomenon, several studies have found evidence of larger instability
during blocking onset and decay, but not during the blocked phase itself 
\parencite{schubertDynamicalAnalysisBlocking2016,lucariniNewMathematicalFramework2020}.
Observation-based evaluation of this behaviour need to solve the problem of
reducing the dimensionality of the system, since the computation of Lyapunov
exponents and related quantities scales non-linearly with the number of degrees of freedom.
Under this provision, several studies have found the stability properties assessed
in models reproduced \parencite{quinnDynamicalAnalysisReduced2021}.
More generally, transitions between meta-stable states have been associated with
enhanced instability also in studies based on observations
\parencite{farandaSwitchingZonalBlocked2016, quinnSystematicCalculationFinitetime2022}.
The stability of a system's state as measured, for example, by the number of locally
unstable dimenions, is tightly coupled to its predictability in 
so far as it relates to multidimensional error growth. The higher the instability,
the argument goes, the more unstable directions need to be sampled in the initial
ensemble to properly capture forecast uncertainty -- a requirement that is 
hard to meet especially at high resolutions 
\parencite{craigDistributionsConvergenceForecast2022, tempestConvergenceForecastDistributions2023}. 

The operational predictability of blockings depends on their definition and location.
Based on the weather regime classification
introduced by \textcite{gramsBalancingEuropesWindpower2017} (cf.~\cite{gramsLifeCycleDefinition2026}
for an updated version),
\textcite{buelerYearroundSubseasonalForecast2021} and \textcite{osmanMultimodelAssessmentSubseasonal2023}
provided evidence that blockings are both the best and worst predicted regimes in the Euro-Atlantic sector,
depending on their location with a blocking over Greenland achieving the longest and
blockings over Central Europe or Scandinavia the shortest forecast skill horizon.
In a similar manner, but using a different set of regimes and only data in winter,
\textcite{ferrantiFlowdependentVerificationECMWF2015} found that operational
forecasts struggled most with transitions to a blocking regime over Northern Europe
while also underestimating their persistence.

The issues faced by \gls{nwp} models in forecasting blockings are reflected in
atmospheric models' difficulties in representing them realistically in the first place,
which is apparent from considerable biases in blocking frequency, intensity and persistence
\parencite{daviniNorthernHemisphereAtmospheric2016}. \textcite{leeIncreasingFrequencyPersistence2026}
recently reported that a seasonal large-ensemble prediction model was able to
reproduce Greenland blocking frequency and persistence but failed to reproduce its trend
due to climate change, making the picture even more complex.

It seems like there are multiple causes for the misrepresentation of blockings,
ranging from insufficient model resolution, coupling to other earth systems
and representation of sub-grid scale processes---all of which exacerbated in the case
of climate models \parencite{woollingsBlockingItsResponse2018}. Several studies 
have investigated the impact of parametrizations on blocking representation with
mixed results. \textcite{dawsonSimulatingWeatherRegimes2015} observed that the regime
structure generally and the representation of blockings in particular was improved
by introducing \gls{sppt} to a similar extent as doubling the horizontal resolution.
Similarly, \textcite{christensenSimulatingWeatherRegimes2015} found improved blocking
persistence and structure in an idealized model when introducing autocorrelated noise
(interestingly, white noise did not have a comparable effect). 
The persistence increasing effect of random perturbations has been identified in 
both the \textcite{lorenzDeterministicNonperiodicFlow1963} model by 
\textcite{kwasniokEnhancedRegimePredictability2014} and in the 
\textcite{charneyMultipleFlowEquilibria1979} model by \textcite{dorringtonInteractionStochasticForcing2023}.

\textcite{molteniRegimesWintertimeCirculation1990} argue that improved
representation of blockings in model, be it through resolution increase
or more accurate parametrization, does not necessarily translate to more
predictable blockings, however. In their conception, the representation 
pertain to the long term statistics of the system, determined by correct 
attractor structure and stochastic forcing, but when exactly a blocking event
occurs stays random (stochastic). If it is indeed the case that \gls{nwp} models
tend to relax to their climatological mean state, then it makes sense that 
stochasticity enables the system to develop meridonal jet configurations and,
by extension, blockings -- e.g. in the Pacific sector \parencite{weisheimerAddressingModelError2014,
palmerRepresentingModelUncertainty2005, jungInfluenceStochasticParameterization2005}.


 Using the same climate model,
\textcite{hartungResolutionPhysicsAtmosphere2017}
observed that parametrization upgrades improved blocking representation 
especially in winter, while \textcite{filippucciImpactStochasticPhysics2024}
found that blocking representation deteriorated upon inclusion of both
\gls{sppt} and \gls{skeb}, arguing for a retuning of model parameters.
\textcite{daviniRepresentationWinterNorthern2021} on the other hand found
that the inclusion of \gls{sppt} and \gls{skeb} improved blocking representation
in the \gls{ecmwf} seasonal prediction model, though they also note that the
improvements are not sufficient to eliminate significant biases in the European sector.

In an evaluation of the
operational seasonal prediction model of the \gls{ecmwf}, \textcite{daviniRepresentationWinterNorthern2021}
found that while improvements have taken place, significant biases remain in the
European sector in particular. They note that resolution increases improved blocking
representation considerably and highlight that errors in the atmospheric system are
most relevant as opposed to errors in ocean representation, though that behaviour seems
to be different in the climate setting. Climatological evaluations of blocking representation
sometimes also have difficulties in identifying robust signals due to the large 
interannual variability of blocking occurrence \parencite{hartungResolutionPhysicsAtmosphere2017}.

Similarly for climate models, \textcite{maddisonMissingIncreaseSummer2024} found that the
increase in summer blocking over Greenland observed in since the beginning of
the century is not reprocuced and suggest this might be linked to \glspl{sst},
sea ice biases or anthopogenic aerosols. Indeed, given what has been mentioned about
the interaction of atmospheric blockings with other earth system components varying
on longer timescales, it seems natural to expect
that the the predictive skill of models predicting blockings depends on whether they
can accurately represent those components and their interaction with blockings.

\subsection{Uncertainty in \glspl{wcb}} \label{sec:intro_wcb_uncertainty}
\glspl{wcb} are tightly coupled to extratropical cyclogenesis 
and the downstream evolution of the large scale flow through intense diabatic heating 
and transport of air masses. As a consequence, even small initial-condition or model 
errors in \gls{wcb} inflow, ascent, and cloud–precipitation processes can undergo 
rapid error growth and project efficiently onto baroclinic and diabatically driven
instabilities of the developing cyclone, thereby amplifying ensemble spread and 
forecast uncertainty downstream \parencite{rodwellWhyWarmConveyor2018,baumgartProcessesGoverningAmplification2019,
wandelSystematicEvaluationWarm2021}.
\textcite{picklWarmConveyorBelts2023} show that for medium range forecasts in the 
Euro-Atlantic sector, high \gls{wcb} activity is associated with anticyclonic
patterns downstream and elevated levels of overall forecast uncertainty. They argue that
this is due to upstream initial condition errors as well as model errors intrinsic to
processes dominant in the cyclone.
This sensitivity makes the parametrized physics within \gls{wcb}s a first-order
uncertainty source: deep-convection schemes can substantially alter \gls{wcb}
structure and its interaction with the jet \parencite{riviereImpactDeepConvection2021},
while microphysical parametrizations modulate latent heating profiles, and precipitation
characteristics \parencite{joosInfluenceMicrophysicalProcesses2012,mazoyerMicrophysicsImpactsWarm2021,mazoyerImpactMixedphaseCloud2023}. 
In addition, stochastic perturbations by \gls{sppt} with zero mean have been shown
to exert a
non-zero mean effect on \gls{wcb} by triggering nonlinear instabilities, making them
more frequent and more intense \parencite{picklEffectStochasticallyPerturbed2022}.

Importantly, misrepresentation of diabatic processes can lead to insufficient
Rossby wave amplification and ridge-building \parencite{graySystematicModelForecast2014}
and seemingly minor microphysics changes can induce large downstream flow 
differences consistent with the idea that \gls{wcb} outflow perturbations can trigger 
disproportionate Rossby-wave responses and regime transitions \parencite{joosImpactDifferentIFS2016}. 
Since \glspl{wcb} frequently heavily affect blocking events (\cref{sec:intro_block_diabatic}),
this linkage is particularly acute for high-impact forecasts involving such events: 
misrepresentation of \gls{wcb} outflow has been identified as a key factor in severe
forecast busts with a missed blocking onset \parencite{gramsAtmosphericDynamicsPerspective2018},
but also more systematically associated with elevated forecast uncertainty in blocking events \parencite{wandelWhyMoistDynamic2024}. 

Consistently, \textcite{madonnaVerificationNorthAtlantic2015} have found improvements
in the representation of \glspl{wcb} across the 2000s in the \gls{ifs}, while 
still noting cases of severe forecast errors and \textcite{maddisonImpactModelUpgrades2020} find that model upgrades 
aimed at diabatic and cloud-process realism can mitigate \gls{wcb}-related forecast busts of blockings. 
More recently, sensitivity analyses have also provided insights into whether uncertainty 
is dominated by parametric choices or by the synoptic inflow state, 
with recent evidence suggesting that inflow conditions can control bulk ascent and
flow modification while microphysics primarily imprints fine-scale precipitation
structure \parencite{hieronymusAlgorithmicDifferentiationSensitivity2022,oertelSensitivitiesWarmConveyor2025}.
Whether this holds for all flow states, however, remains an open question. Nevertheless, it 
it is fair to say that \glspl{wcb} can be seen as propagators and magnifiers
of forecast uncertainty highlighting the need for further understanding.

\section{Aims and thesis roadmap}\label{sec:intro_roadmap}

\subsection{Research questions}\label{sec:intro_questions}

Atmospheric blocking and \glspl{wcb} are important features of the mid-latitude
circulation with large impacts on natural and societal systems. They are also 
associated with predictability issues both on theoretical and practical grounds.
Adequately, a growing body of research has been dedicated to understanding 
the interactions between the two, their representation in models
as well as intrinsic dynamical nature. The aim of this thesis is to contribute
to this research. The complex multiscale nature of the phenomena suggests 
important insights may be uncovered through advanced, non-linear methods from 
dynamical systems theory and statistics. The translation and application of
such methods to the phenomena of interest constitutes a key contribution of this
thesis. More specifically, the following research questions are addressed:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item How does stochasticity interact with the regime dynamics of a dynamical
system? What does this interaction tell us about the predictability and stability
of the system? Can we study these effects from an observational perspective
and without access to the underlying dynamics?
    \item What governs assimilation uncertainty on synoptic scales? What are the
relative roles of observation, model and intrinsic uncertainty? How do the answers
to these questions depend on the flow state and the presence of diabatic processes?
    \item Can we understand \glspl{wcb} as spatio-temporally coherent structures
of the flow? How does their coherence differ depending on lifecycle phases and
what does this tell us about their interaction with blockings?
    \item What do the answers to the above questions imply for the representation
and predictability of blockings and \glspl{wcb} in models? How do they connect
to the established theories and what questions do they raise for future research?
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Thesis structure}\label{sec:intro_structure}
Each of the questions above is addressed in a separate ensuing chapter. The first
three of which are based on published or to be published manuscripts, while
the last one is an original synthesis of the results and their implications.

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Stochastic inertia (\cref{ch:stochasticInertia}):} In this chapter,
 the interaction of stochasticity with the regime dynamics of a
low-order model are investigated. A novel method to study this interaction from a
data-driven perspective is introduced and applied to several Toy models and the 
\textcite{charneyMultipleFlowEquilibria1979} model. What is found is that stochasticity
can induce a non-zero mean effect on the system's dynamics, increasing the 
lifetime of regimes of the system under certain conditions -- an effect called
stochastic inertia. 
Adopting a transfer-operator framework facilitates establishing a method to 
better understand and identify the existence and quantify the
strength of this effect, without access to the underlying dynamics. 
    \item \textbf{Weather regimes and assimilation uncertainty (\cref{ch:weatherRegimes}):}
The nature of assimilation uncertainty for a selected reanalysis,
 \gls{era5}, is examined. Through a novel statistical framework and by a unique
combination of well-established datasets, the several influencing factors 
are isolated and their relative importance assessed. The development of the
observation system leaves a dominant imprint on the uncertainty on long-time
scales with important regional and seasonal variations. On shorter time scales,
a significant flow-dependence is found, that can be explained to large part 
by the presence moist processes. This dependence is caused mostly by observation
uncertainties, while model uncertainties seem to project more onto essentially
dry dynamics. \gls{wcb} occurrence predominantly increases assimilation uncertainty
in a local and non-local manner that varies by weather regime.
    \item \textbf{Coherence of warm conveyor belts (\cref{ch:coherence}):}
A novel method is developed that clusters trajectories in the earth system based on their 
spatio-temporal coherence. Though agnostic with respect to scale and application,
its application is demonstrated for \glspl{wcb} interacting with blockings.
This framework understands \glspl{wcb} as three-dimensional and temporally evolving
objects interacting with the large-scale flow on a range of scales. In this
Lagrangian perspective, \glspl{wcb} shape the atmospheric blocking and vice versa
and the coherence of the trajectories is found to be closely linked to the
lifecycle phase of the blocking.
    \item \textbf{Conclusions and outlook (\cref{ch:conclusions}):} The insights
obtained in the previous chapters are connected by a common thread. The interaction
of \glspl{wcb} and blockings is inherently multiscale and non-linear, with a lot
of important questions still unanswered. Progress in mathematical research provides
an invaluable resource to tackle these questions and translating such methods
to real-world phenomena is guaranteed to yield important insights into both the
nature of the phenomena and the methods themselves. For the present case of 
blockings and \glspl{wcb}, an thorough understanding of the interaction and predictability
of the phenomena certainly cannot be achieved
without consistently considering the range of factors involved, including the role
of stochasticity and sub-grid scale processes, the sources of uncertainty on each
of the relevant scales and dual role of the phenomena as both Eulerian features
acting on the flow and Lagrangian objects shaped by the flow.    
\end{enumerate}
