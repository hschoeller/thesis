The three studies in this thesis approach blocking and \glspl{wcb}
from very different angles---an abstract dynamical-systems analysis of
noise-induced regime persistence, a climatological characterization of
assimilation uncertainty, and a process-oriented study of \glspl{wcb} and other
coherent air streams through blockings---yet they converge on a
small number of recurring themes. The following draws out where they
reinforce, qualify, and extend one another and where they collectively pose
new questions.

\section{Unstable dimensions}

A quantity that surfaces in each chapter, under different guises, is the local
dimension of the system either in state space or in physical space.
In the dynamical-systems
study of \cref{ch:stochasticInertia}, \emph{stochastic inertia}
was established
for a regime associated with
an unstable fixed point possessing a \emph{single} unstable dimension, the
geometry of which---a flow accelerating away from the fixed point along that
one direction---is what produces the effect. An analogous toy model with
three unstable dimensions lacked this property, and a forthcoming publication
shows that this holds in generality. This is a particularly interesting result,
because local persistence and local dimension are usually discussed as separate
measures in recent literature and studying in how far they are related 
in other settings offers a promising direction for future work.

The relevance of \emph{stochastic inertia} as a mechanism in full-complexity
models and the real atmosphere is
immediately bound by its dependence on a single unstable dimension, as this
is a strong assumption, indeed, and the dynamical dimensionality of atmospheric
blocking
is itself an open question. As outlined in the introduction, the
instability of blockings in this sense depends on the location of the
blocking, the time of year, and its life-cycle stage, as well as on whether it
is assessed in a model or in reanalysis. On top of this, how exactly to measure
the attractor structure of the atmosphere and what exactly this tells us is 
still a matter of debate, even though the topic has been subject of active
research for decades.

\medskip

The dynamical dimension also connects the abstract setting to the
assimilation perspective of \cref{ch:weatherRegimes}. The inability of an
ensemble assimilation system to sample all relevant directions is an
instance of the infamous \emph{curse of dimensionality}; explicitly
accounting for the unstable directions is therefore a promising avenue for
designing data assimilation schemes that use their resources efficiently.
Singular and bred vectors target precisely these unstable subspaces when
constructing initial spread, though they do so independently of the
observation uncertainties. The currently operational \gls{ifs} routine
reflects this division of labour: it inflates the initial \gls{eda}
perturbations with singular vectors to ensure sufficient spread along the
most unstable directions in particular.

That the two are genuinely distinct is itself a result of
\cref{ch:weatherRegimes}. The regime-dependent assimilation uncertainty found
there does not single out any regimes as particularly uncertain, as would be
expected if the differences in local attractor geometry reported in other
studies either caused or were caused by this uncertainty. This is
unsurprising: there is no a priori reason for the observation uncertainty to
project onto the attractor geometry of the system. The dynamical and the
observational contributions to flow-dependent uncertainty are, in this sense,
independent.

This independence is also what makes their combination awkward. Because the
observation-induced uncertainty and the most unstable directions are in general
independent, it is unclear what proportion each component should
contribute, and the operational reliance on post-hoc inflation is a symptom
of this. An integrated scheme that represents the flow-dependent uncertainty
of both the observations and the model simultaneously, without requiring such
inflation, would be a more elegant---though considerably more
challenging---solution. The \gls{eda} system does combine the two aspects to
some extent by employing stochastic physics and through the background-error
covariance, but the investigations of \cref{ch:weatherRegimes} support 
the insufficiency of adressing unstable directions given expression 
in the necessity of adding singular vector perturbations.
A natural systematic extension of the work of \cref{ch:weatherRegimes}
would be to study how the results change once singular vectors are included 
and how the flow-dependency of assimilation uncertainty looks if \emph{only}
singular vectores were used.

\medskip

A third notion of dimension enters in physical space. As shown in
\cref{sec:CS_eps}, the stability of a set of air parcels against perturbation
can be used to infer the geometry of the flow, and the apparent dimension of
the air masses constituting a blocking characterises its life cycle. The
dynamical and the physical dimension are plausibly related, since the
three-dimensional equivalent-barotropic structure of a blocking is tightly
coupled to its quasi-stationarity: in the case studies considered, a breakdown
of coherence and the associated reduction in the traced air masses'
geometrical dimension accompanied the block's destabilisation and decay.
Perhaps counterintuitively, a large set of air masses persisting in a
three-dimensional configuration implies that the flow they are embedded in
behaves rather two-dimensionally---tying back to the role of turbulence and
its active dimensionality in blocking and \glspl{wcb}
(\cref{sec:intro_block_baroclinic}). 

The derived measure of local dimension employed in the aforementioned analysis
as well as in \cref{ch:stochasticInertia}
is a powerful tool that can be applied
in any space---state space or physical space and Euclidean or not---and
offers the additional advantage of being independent of differences in 
sampling density, which is an issue in other measures of local dimension. 
Though its interpretation and generalisation are bound by the implied design
choices, an application of the method to the state space of more complex
models could add to the clarification of the notion of local dimension 
and what that tells us about the system's behaviour in terms of predictability,
hyperbolicity or sampling requirements for the assimilation setting.  

All of the above go to show that properly reflecting on the local dimensions
of a system may be a \emph{blessing rather than a curse} and an equally
optimistic 
perspective applies to noise: this thesis has shown that---if carefully 
considered---noise can be a \emph{tool rather than a nuisance}.

\section{Noise and scales}

While \cref{ch:stochasticInertia} established the existence of stochastic
inertia for fixed points with a single unstable dimension,
a state with several unstable dimensions may still exhibit
the phenomenon if the stochastic forcing projects onto only one of
them or one dimension is disproportionately more unstable than the
others---in the same way that a three-dimensional fluid system can behave
according to two-dimensional turbulence. Similar to this turbulence analogy,
such a behaviour could be indicative of a separation of scales, with the effect
then dependent on the scales considered in the analysis.
These observations make the directional structure of perturbations in
relation to the
local Lyapunov vectors decisive rather than
incidental. If stochastic schemes are to represent the upscale forcing of
small-scale processes, what matters is whether they excite the same unstable
directions that the small scales would, and it is unclear whether current
schemes do so.

This concern is sharpened by the nature of the schemes
themselves: \gls{sppt} perturbs the total physics tendency, yet a large
tendency need not coincide with a large uncertainty or a strongly unstable
direction---a well-forecast surface cyclone may carry tendencies large in
magnitude
but low uncertainty compared to the initiation of an isolated convective cell 
whose prediction is highly uncertain and the associated tendencies possibly 
near zero. \gls{spp} perturbs the uncertain parameters more selectively
and may align better with the relevant directions, e.g.~by varying initiation
thresholds, but whether either
scheme activates the dynamically unstable subspace remains largely untested.
Future work could explore this questions along a hierarchy of models with 
the simplest ones offering the most direct assessment of attractor structure.
This coincides with the natural extension of the inquiry into stochastic
inertia, which would consider other kinds of noise---multiplicative like 
\gls{sppt} for example---and more complex models. 

\medskip

The convergence of the dynamical-systems perspective with the impacts
of stochasticitiy in full-scale models also hinges on treating the additive
noise of the low-order model as a stand-in for
stochastic parametrization of model error. This identification, however,
deserves scrutiny.
The \gls{cdv} system is barotropic, whereas blocking dynamics are considerably
shaped by baroclinic and diabatic processes; the noise that would mimic
the relevant model error is therefore not generic but specifically
baroclinic and diabatic in origin---of which \gls{wcb} latent
heating is the prime example. A complementary
reading is that the noise represents the coarse-graining inherent to any
discretised model: unresolved scales act back on the resolved flow as an
effectively stochastic forcing in the spirit of \gls{skeb}, which is additive
in nature. Both readings are partial, and which
dominates depends on the grid scale and on which processes are resolved
rather than parametrized---precisely the ambiguity that makes the
convective grey zone so problematic.

This connects to a tension that runs through the blocking literature and
was left unresolved in \cref{sec:intro_predictability_blockings}: the
representation of blocking does
not improve monotonically with resolution, and the inclusion of stochastic
physics can improve or degrade it depending on the configuration. If the
effect of unresolved physics on regime behaviour is genuinely non-linear in
the way stochastic inertia suggests---a non-monotonic response to
perturbation amplitude---then neither finer resolution nor stronger
stochastic forcing should be expected to help uniformly. The
dynamical-systems result thus offers a conceptual rationale for why the
empirical picture is so mixed.

\medskip

Observing every relevant proces and scale is as impossible as representing all
scales in a model, and this predicament is dealt with in a similar way by
introducing noise in observation space for assimilation. The generation of 
an initial ensemble through \gls{eda} can be seen as an exploration of the 
local neighborhood of the analysis through noise, which already points to 
its pivotal role determining whether assimilation ensembles
truly add predictive value or are \enquote{just} an efficient way of
denoising. Since \gls{eda} is just one way of carrying out this exploration,
the consistency of the results of \cref{ch:weatherRegimes} across other 
assimilation systems is a worthwile task. A related question pertains to the
precise way in which observation perturbations are added durig assimilation;
drawing from a zero-mean Gaussian distribution as performed for the 
\gls{eda} is a design choice that may not be optimal in all cases and dedicated
experiments could explore what impacts other choices entail.

\medskip

In the Lagrangian study of air streams in \cref{ch:coherence}, noise 
enabled separation of robust and coherent structures, which inherits its
importance
from the ubiquitous uncertainty in models and observations. 
The sensitivity of \glspl{wcb} to
perturbations, for instance, is itself diagnostic of the processes that
govern their evolution and because \glspl{wcb} are so
sensitive to perturbation, defining them as coherent sets that are robust
to perturbation is a natural way to identify them objectively as dynamically
meaningful systems. The noise in this setting was applied in physical space
to the locations of the air parcels, but since the method is scale-invariant 
and agnostic with respect to the metric space considered, the same principle
can be applied
in a state space like in \cref{ch:stochasticInertia} or to other variables to
identify other kinds of coherent
structures or to assess the the coherence of strucutres identified by other 
means. 

In a similar vein, the sensitivity of \glspl{wcb} to variations in model
physics and initial and boundary conditions has so far been studied on the 
basis of individual trajectories. \Cref{ch:coherence} has shown that much 
can be learned by considering coherent sets of trajectories and adopting that
view in sensitivity studies for \glspl{wcb} or blockings more generally may
yield further important insights into the propagation of uncertainty. The
construction of such coherent sets suggest that they are kinematically robust
to perturbations, so studying how their dynamical properties change across
their life cycle between different experiment realizations may allow comparing
them in a more appropriate object-centered way.

\section{Implications for prediction}

The analyses in this thesis were diagnostic and largely post-hoc, but their
implications bear directly on practical prediction. Two levers emerge with
particular clarity. The first is initial-condition uncertainty:
\cref{ch:weatherRegimes}
showed that a substantial part of the flow-dependent uncertainty in active
regions enters through the observations and the assimilation rather than
through model error alone, so that improving the observing network and the
assimilation of difficult, cloud-affected scenes remains a major route to
better forecasts. Dedicated measurements are carried out for
impactful weather systems like tropical cyclones
or severe thunderstorms, but resource constraints limit the extent to which
such pathways to targeted observational campaigns can be implemented
routinely. Still, the insights into the flow-dependence of observation 
uncertainty and its impact on assimilation and forecast may prove valuable 
for guiding future developments. Those impacts could be 
reinforced by dedicated \gls{ose} or \gls{osse} and extended to other regions
apart from the North Atlantic-European sector considered herein.

\medskip

Model uncertainty is the second predictability lever: the scaling cascades
discussed in \cref{sec:intro_predictability_chaos} and the conflicting effects
of resolution increases on blocking representation together indicate that the
proper
representation of unresolved physics will remain pivotal, and that it cannot
be addressed by resolution alone. The dynamical-systems perspective adds a
distinctive and practically valuable corollary here. Because \emph{stochastic
      inertia} is detectable from the \emph{deterministic} trajectory alone,
      the
behaviour of a model under stochastic perturbation may be at least partly
anticipated from its deterministic dynamics---which is the only handle
available, given that no alternative realisation of the real atmosphere
exists against which to calibrate. Coarse, deterministic model behaviour may
therefore carry more information about the likely effect of a
parametrization than is commonly assumed.

These implications become particularly salient---as has been shown in this
thesis---for blockings and \glspl{wcb}, which are both highly sensitive to
perturbations and major sources of forecast uncertainty. The research directions
outlined above may therefore be especially fruitful in the context of these
phenomena. For instance, \cref{ch:weatherRegimes} has shown that elevated
assimilation uncertainty is present not for blocking regions themselves, but
rather the upstream regions and those associated with \glspl{wcb}
in particular. Assimilation experiments are frequently designed for 
convective phenomena, but similar experiments for the interaction of
\glspl{wcb} with blockings appear to be
lacking, even though the impact of initial condition uncertainty in that 
context has been assessed. 

\medskip

Finally, the focus throughout has been on the present-day atmosphere as
represented in reanalysis and idealised models, with no use of
climate-model output or climate-scale data. Given the demonstrated
relevance of moist processes and unresolved physics for blocking---both of
which behave
differently under a changing climate and at the coarser resolutions typical
of climate models---extending the analysis to that setting is a natural and
important next step. This perspective is partially inherent to the
dynamical systems approach of \cref{ch:stochasticInertia}, since the effects
of noise on blockings have often been assessed in climate models, but 
a changing climate also affects the role of moisture in particular and the
circulation more generally. It is, therefore, unclear whether the insights
obtained in \cref{ch:weatherRegimes,ch:coherence} hold in a warmer world 
and on coarser scales. Analyzing future assimilation uncertainty is, of
course, not possible, but the evident problems climate models have with 
correctly representing blocking and \glspl{wcb} may be better understood
when adopting a coherence-based perspective. 

The climate model question connects to another broad direction: coupling to
other Earth-system components. The role of the ocean in particular merits
attention, as sea-surface temperatures and ocean fronts modulate both the
diabatic forcing of \glspl{wcb} and the baroclinicity available to
developing cyclones, and may therefore shape the flow-dependent uncertainty
in ways not resolved by an atmosphere-only perspective. Relatedly, the
interaction of blocking with sea ice, ocean heat content, and
longer-timescale variability---already implicated in the climate-model
biases discussed earlier---offers a route to connect the synoptic-scale
uncertainty studied here to the subseasonal and seasonal predictability of
the regimes themselves. Though varying on longer timescales, these components
are also associated with particular and substantial observation uncertainties,
so that the assimilation perspective of \cref{ch:weatherRegimes} may provide
further insights on longer timescales as well.

\bigskip

Taken together, this thesis makes a case that extends beyond the
specific phenomena it addresses. Blocking and \gls{wcb} served as
demanding test cases---multiscale, non-linear, and stubbornly difficult for
models---but the thread connecting the chapters is
methodological and conceptual as much as physical. A single family of
tools, built on perturbations and clustered by
dynamical coherence, proved able to characterise the persistence of a
regime, the flow-dependence of analysis uncertainty, and the integrity of
coherent air streams alike. What unites these applications is a change of
attitude towards the two quantities that ordinarily appear as obstacles. The
local dimension of a system, usually invoked as the \emph{curse} that makes
high-dimensional estimation intractable, became a lens on its predictability
and its sampling requirements; and noise, usually treated as the
\emph{nuisance} that corrupts a forecast or an analysis, became a probe of
which directions matter and which structures endure. Properly considered,
neither uncertainty nor dimensionality is merely a limitation to be
suppressed---both are diagnostics of how the atmosphere and its model analogues
behave.
How the specific mechanisms and insights identified here translate to other 
spatial scales and levels of complexity remains to be established, but the
broader lesson is more robust:
that confronting uncertainty directly, rather than treating it as something
to be minimised away, is itself a route to understanding the dynamics and
the predictability of the atmosphere.
