\section{Synthesis}\label{sec:synth}
% This is the core and what distinguishes a thesis conclusion from a paper
% conclusion. Don't summarise each paper in turn — that reads like an abstract

The three studies in this thesis approach blocking and \glspl{wcb}
from very different angles---an abstract dynamical-systems analysis of
noise-induced regime persistence, a climatological characterization of
assimilation uncertainty, and a process-oriented study of \glspl{wcb} and other
coherent air streams through blockings---yet they converge on a
small number of recurring themes. The following draws out where they
reinforce, qualify, and extend one another.

\subsection{Unstable dimensions}

A quantity that surfaces in each chapter, under different guises, is the
local dimensionality of the unstable subspace. In the dynamical-systems
study, \emph{stochastic inertia} was established for a regime associated with
an
unstable fixed point possessing a \emph{single} unstable dimension, the
geometry of which---a flow accelerating away from the fixed point along
that one direction---is what produces the effect. An analogous toy model
with three unstable dimensions lacked this property and it will be shown in
a forthcoming publication that this holds in generality. The implications
of \emph{stochastic intertia} as a mechanism possibly relevant in full-complexity
models and the real world are immediately bound by this: the existence of a
single unstable dimension is a strong assumption and the dimensionality of
atmospheric blocking in the dynamical sense is an open question
\parencite{farandaDynamicalProxiesNorth2017,
      quinnSystematicCalculationFinitetime2022}.
Evidence suggests that
instability peaks during blocking onset and decay but not the blocked phase
\parencite{schubertDynamicalAnalysisBlocking2016,
      lucariniNewMathematicalFramework2020} underlining again that this
dimensionality is strongly flow-dependent. Even if a state exhibits multiple
unstable dimensions, however, \emph{stochastic intertia} may still be observed
once the stochasticity projects only onto one of those dimensions in a similar
way that a three-dimensional fluid system may behave according to two-dimensional
turbulence as discussed in \cref{sec:intro_predictability_chaos}. If
stochastic perturbations should represent small-scale forcing onto the large
scales it is therefore critical whether it activates the same dimensions as the
small scales would and it is unclear if this is currently the case.

Flow-dependent unstable dimensions reappear in the assimilation context, since
they govern how well an ensemble---and therefore an ensemble data
assimilation---represents the analysis error
\parencite{craigDistributionsConvergenceForecast2022,
      tempestConvergenceForecastDistributions2023}. The flow-dependence of
assimilation uncertainty documented in \cref{ch:weatherRegimes} can thus be
read as the observational counterpart to the state-dependent instability
studied in the abstract setting: where the flow is dynamically active and
its unstable subspace richer, both the intrinsic error growth and the
difficulty of constraining the state through observations increase
together. The dynamical-systems and reanalysis perspectives therefore
describe two faces of the same underlying flow-dependence---one intrinsic
to the attractor, one imprinted through the observing system.

Finally, stability against perturbations may also be used in physical space
to infer the geometry of the flow and its associated dimension as was shown in
\cref{sec:CS_eps}. The apparent dimension of the air
parcels that consitute the blocking characterize the blocking's lifecycle.
The two notions of dimension---the dynamical one and the physical one---may
very well be related, since the three-dimensional equivalent-barotropic shape
of a blocking is tightly coupled to its quasi-stationarity. In the case
studies considere, a breakdown of the blocking's coherence and thus a reduction
of the traced air masses' geometrical dimension went along with its
destabilization and decay. Perhaps counterintuitively, a large set of air
masses persisting in a three-dimensional configuration implies the flow
they are embedded in behaves rather two-dimensionally which ties back to the
considerations about the role of turbulence and its (active) dimensionality
in the context of blocking and \glspl{wcb} raised in \cref{sec:intro_block_baroclinic}.

\subsection{Noise and unresolved scales}

Treating the additive noise of the low-order model as a stand-in for
stochastic parametrization of model error is what makes the dynamical-systems
\cref{ch:stochasticInertia} relevant
to following ones. This identification, however, deserves scrutiny.
The \gls{cdv} system is barotropic, whereas blocking dynamics are considerably
shaped by baroclinic and diabatic processes; the noise that would mimic
governed by baroclinic and diabatic processes; the noise that would mimic
the relevant model error is therefore not generic but specifically
baroclinic and diabatic in origin---of which \gls{wcb} latent
heating is the prime example. A complementary
reading is that the noise represents the coarse-graining inherent to any
discretised model: unresolved scales act back on the resolved flow as an
effectively stochastic forcing. Both readings are partial, and which
dominates depends on the grid scale and on which processes are resolved
rather than parametrized---precisely the ambiguity that makes the
convective grey zone so problematic.

This connects to a tension that runs through the blocking literature and
was left unresolved in \cref{sec:intro_predictability_blockings}: the
representation of blocking does
not improve monotonically with resolution, and the inclusion of stochastic
physics can improve or degrade it depending on the configuration. If the
effect of unresolved physics on regime behaviour is genuinely non-linear in
the way stochastic inertia suggests---a non-monotonic response to
perturbation amplitude---then neither finer resolution nor stronger
stochastic forcing should be expected to help uniformly. The
dynamical-systems result thus offers a conceptual rationale for why the
empirical picture is so mixed.

Observing every relevant proces and scale is as impossible as representing all
scales in a model, and this predicament is dealt with in a similar way by
introducing noise in obersvation space for assimilation. Given the tremendous
influence this has, it is crucial to design these observational perturbations
in a way consistent with the physics of the system or, in other words, along
the relevant dimensions. It is unclear to date, in how far this is the case,
but is probably a pivotal aspect determining whether assimilation ensembles
truly add predictive value or are \enquote{just} and efficient way of
denoising \parencite{fengEnsembleForecastingForay2024}.

% \subsection{Uncertainty as a tool}

A theme common to all three studies is that uncertainty, properly
considered, is a source of understanding rather than merely a limitation.
The flow-dependence of assimilation spread reveals \emph{where} and
\emph{through which processes} the observing system constrains the
atmosphere most weakly; the response of a regime to noise reveals the
geometry of its underlying instability; and the sensitivity of \glspl{wcb} to
perturbations is itself diagnostic of the processes that
govern their evolution. This last point motivates a methodological
convergence worth highlighting: because \glspl{wcb} are so
sensitive to perturbation, defining them as coherent sets that are robust
to perturbation is a natural way to identify them objectively as dynamically
meaningful systems. The same family of mathematical tools aimed at
characterising
stability has recently been turned to identifying blocking through its
quasi-stationarity \parencite{badzaIdentifyingOnsetDecay2024}, underlining
how concepts developed to quantify stability and uncertainty can be
repurposed as identification and diagnostic tools across very different
settings.

\subsection{Implications for prediction}

The analyses in this thesis were diagnostic and largely post-hoc, but their
implications bear directly on practical prediction. Two levers emerge with
particular clarity. The first is initial-condition uncertainty:
\cref{ch:weatherRegimes}
showed that a substantial part of the flow-dependent uncertainty in active
regions enters through the observations and the assimilation rather than
through model error alone, so that improving the observing network and the
assimilation of difficult, cloud-affected scenes remains a major route to
better forecasts. Dedicated measurements are carried out for
impactful weather systems like tropical cyclones
or severe thunderstorms, but resource constraints limit the extent to which
such pathways to targeted observational campaigns can be implemented
routinely.

Model uncertainty is the second predictability lever: the scaling cascades
discussed in \cref{sec:intro_predictability_chaos} and the conflicting effects
of resolution increases on blocking representation together indicate that the
proper
representation of unresolved physics will remain pivotal, and that it cannot
be addressed by resolution alone. The dynamical-systems perspective adds a
distinctive and practically valuable corollary here. Because \emph{stochastic
      inertia} is detectable from the \emph{deterministic} trajectory alone, the
behaviour of a model under stochastic perturbation may be at least partly
anticipated from its deterministic dynamics---which is the only handle
available, given that no alternative realisation of the real atmosphere
exists against which to calibrate. Coarse, deterministic model behaviour may
therefore carry more information about the likely effect of a
parametrization than is commonly assumed.

\section{Future directions}\label{sec:Imp_Fut}

The conclusions drawn in this thesis are subject to several limitations,
each of which points towards a natural avenue for future work.

The most fundamental limitation concerns the methodological scope. All
three studies are data-driven and diagnostic: they characterise uncertainty
and dynamical behaviour from existing trajectories---whether observed,
reanalysed, or generated by a low-order model---rather than from controlled
numerical experiments. This is a deliberate strength where the goal is to
let the data speak with minimal modelling assumptions, but it also limits
the ability to establish causality directly. The flow-dependence of
assimilation uncertainty documented in \cref{ch:weatherRegimes}, for
instance, is inferred from statistical association rather than from
experiments in which individual observation types or assimilation settings
are withheld. Dedicated observing-system experiments (OSEs) or
observing-system simulation experiments (OSSEs) would allow the
contributions of specific observation sources to the regime-dependent
uncertainty patterns to be isolated, complementing the purely statistical
attribution pursued here---though such experiments are of course heavily
limited by their computational cost. Short of this, the descriptive
climatology could be linked more explicitly to the underlying observation
network---its coverage, instrument mix, and the assimilation treatment of
difficult scenes---turning it into a diagnostic of where observational
investment would most improve the analysis. The dataset produced is well
suited to such follow-up, and its decades-long extent makes it possible to
study how the flow-dependence of uncertainty has itself evolved as the
observing system matured.

The same diagnostic character constrains the dynamical-systems study, where
the stochastic-inertia mechanism of \cref{ch:stochasticInertia} was
identified in a single low-order model; confirming its relevance to the
real atmosphere requires its detection across a hierarchy of models of
increasing complexity. This ascent of the model hierarchy is the most
direct extension of that chapter. Stochastic inertia has so far been
demonstrated only in systems with a single unstable dimension; applying the
data-driven detection method to intermediate-complexity models, and
ultimately to convection-permitting or operational output, would test
whether the mechanism survives the higher-dimensional, non-stationary
instability of realistic flow. This is in principle possible, since the
diffusion-maps framework employed in
\cref{ch:stochasticInertia,ch:coherence} has favourable scaling properties;
careful consideration must, however, be given to the metric in which the
distances between atmospheric states are measured. Because the method
requires only a single deterministic trajectory, it is moreover directly
applicable to reanalysis or long control integrations, offering a path from
the abstract result towards an observational test. A particularly
informative intermediate step would be a baroclinic, moisture-capable
model, since the barotropic Charney--DeVore setting cannot represent the
diabatic forcing that the synthesis identified as the most plausible
physical counterpart of the abstract noise.

This points to a related idealisation in the same study: the noise applied
in the \gls{cdv} system is constant in space and time and acts equally in
all directions---convenient, but certainly not a realistic representation of
model error. In the spirit of conceiving noise as a tool for understanding
rather than only a nuisance, future work could explore perturbations with
more realistic characteristics---spatially structured, flow-dependent, or
preferentially aligned with the unstable subspace---and ask how the
stochastic-inertia effect responds. Such experiments would also sharpen the
interpretation of the noise as a proxy for unresolved diabatic and
baroclinic physics rather than as generic forcing.

A further limitation is the restriction to the existing analysis state. The
\gls{eda} characterizes the random component of analysis uncertainty but
does not capture systematic model biases or correlated errors, which may
themselves be flow-dependent. Nor does the work engage with the forecast
evolution directly: the analyses are confined to the assimilated state and
its uncertainty, leaving the question of how this initial uncertainty
subsequently grows in a forecast for separate investigation. Closing this
gap---propagating the flow-dependent initial uncertainty characterised here
through a forecast model and tracking which regimes and processes amplify
it---would connect the present diagnostic results to the practical
predictability questions that motivated them.

Finally, the focus throughout has been on the present-day atmosphere as
represented in reanalysis and idealised models, with no use of
climate-model output or climate-scale data. Given the demonstrated
relevance of moist processes and unresolved physics---both of which behave
differently under a changing climate and at the coarser resolutions typical
of climate models---extending the analysis to that setting is a natural and
important next step. This connects to a third broad direction: coupling to
other Earth-system components. The role of the ocean in particular merits
attention, as sea-surface temperatures and ocean fronts modulate both the
diabatic forcing of warm conveyor belts and the baroclinicity available to
developing cyclones, and may therefore shape the flow-dependent uncertainty
in ways not resolved by an atmosphere-only perspective. Relatedly, the
interaction of blocking with sea ice, ocean heat content, and
longer-timescale variability---already implicated in the climate-model
biases discussed earlier---offers a route to connect the synoptic-scale
uncertainty studied here to the subseasonal and seasonal predictability of
the regimes themselves.

Taken together, these directions share a common orientation: moving from
the post-hoc, diagnostic characterisation of uncertainty and dynamical
behaviour pursued in this thesis towards their controlled manipulation and
their use in the practical prediction setting, where the proper
representation of both initial-condition and model uncertainty remains a
central challenge.
